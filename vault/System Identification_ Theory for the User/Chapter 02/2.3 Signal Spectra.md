# 2.3 Signal Spectra

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 2**: Section 2.3 constructs a unified frequency-domain framework—based on **quasi-stationary signals** and the generalized time-averaging expectation operator $\overline{E}$—that allows deterministic input sequences and stochastic disturbance processes to share a single definition of covariance and power spectrum. It establishes how linear time-invariant (LTI) systems transform spectra (Theorem 2.2) and how rational disturbance spectra can be canonically factored into stable, minimum-phase ARMA models (Spectral Factorization), laying the direct analytical foundation for the ergodic single-realization convergence results in Section 2.4 and frequency-domain identification throughout the book.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

In Section 2.2, we saw that the finite-sample Fourier transform $S_N(\omega)$ and periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ describe the frequency contents of a signal over a finite observation window $t = 1, \dots, N$. However, when we want to characterize the intrinsic, asymptotic frequency distribution of a signal over $t \in [1, \infty)$, we immediately hit two fundamental roadblocks:

1. **The naive periodogram limit fails to exist**: One might naturally try to define the asymptotic spectrum of a signal $\{s(t)\}$ as the pointwise limit of its periodogram:

$$
\lim_{N \to \infty} |S_N(\omega)|^2
$$

   However, for almost all signals of practical interest (including realizations of stochastic noise processes), the periodogram fluctuates erratically with $\omega$ and fails to converge as $N \to \infty$.
2. **Classical stationary stochastic process theory is too restrictive for system identification**: In standard statistical signal processing, the power spectral density is defined only for wide-sense stationary stochastic processes as the Fourier transform of their ensemble autocovariance function $R_s(\tau) = E[s(t)s(t-\tau)]$. Why does Ljung reject this standard framework? Because in system identification, our canonical system model is:

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + v(t)
$$

   In experimental practice, the engineer designs and applies a specific, known input sequence $\{u(t)\}$ (such as a step, a sum of sinusoids, or a pseudo-random binary sequence stored in computer memory), while the unmeasured disturbance $\{v(t)\}$ is modeled as a zero-mean stochastic process. Under this hybrid modeling philosophy—where $\{u(t)\}$ is **deterministic** and $\{v(t)\}$ is **stochastic**—taking the ensemble expectation of the output yields:

$$
E y(t) = G(q)u(t)
$$

   Because $E y(t)$ varies with time $t$ whenever $u(t)$ varies with time, **the output $\{y(t)\}$ is not a stationary stochastic process!** Even its second moment $E[y(t)y(t-\tau)] = [G(q)u(t)][G(q)u(t-\tau)] + R_v(\tau)$ depends explicitly on absolute time $t$.

### The Engineering Solution: The Quasi-Stationary Framework and Operator $\overline{E}$

To avoid splitting every theorem in the book into separate "deterministic input" and "stochastic input" cases—or artificially pretending that a deterministic test signal $u(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t)$ is a random process with a random phase—Ljung introduces the concept of **quasi-stationary signals** and the combined operator $\overline{E}$:

$$
\overline{E} f(t) \triangleq \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E f(t)
$$

Think of $\overline{E}$ as a **two-stage filter**:
1. First, the ensemble expectation $E[\cdot]$ averages out the **randomness** of any stochastic components at each fixed time instant $t$ (and acts as the identity operator on deterministic sequences, since $E[u(t)] = u(t)$).
2. Second, the infinite time average $\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N (\cdot)$ averages out the **deterministic time variation** across $t \in [1, \infty)$.

By defining the generalized autocovariance as $R_s(\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)]$ and the power spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ as its discrete-time Fourier transform, deterministic sequences, stationary stochastic processes, and mixed input-output signals all obey the exact same spectral algebra!

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Quasi-Stationary Signals and the $\overline{E}$ Operator

To handle signals that combine deterministic time variations with random fluctuations, Ljung introduces **Definition 2.1**:

> [!important] Definition 2.1: Quasi-Stationary Signals
> A signal $\{s(t)\}$ is said to be **quasi-stationary** if it satisfies the following two boundedness and convergence conditions for some finite constant $C < \infty$:
>
> 1. **Bounded ensemble mean** (Eq. 2.58i):
>
> $$
> E s(t) = m_s(t), \qquad |m_s(t)| \le C, \quad \forall t
> $$
>
> 2. **Bounded two-time correlation and convergent time-averaged autocovariance** (Eqs. 2.58ii & 2.59):
>
> $$
> E[s(t)s(r)] = R_s(t, r), \qquad |R_s(t, r)| \le C, \quad \forall t, r
> $$
>
> $$
> \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} R_s(t, t - \tau) = R_s(\tau), \quad \forall \tau \in \mathbb{Z}
> $$
>

**How Definition 2.1 Specializes to Purely Deterministic and Purely Stochastic Signals**:
- **Case 1: Purely Deterministic Sequence**: If $\{s(t)\}$ is a deterministic sequence, the expectation operator $E$ has no effect ($E s(t) = s(t)$ and $E[s(t)s(r)] = s(t)s(r)$). Quasi-stationarity then simply requires that $\{s(t)\}$ is uniformly bounded ($|s(t)| \le \sqrt{C}$) and that the infinite time-average limit

$$
R_s(\tau) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} s(t)s(t - \tau)
$$

  exists for every integer lag $\tau$.
- **Case 2: Stationary Stochastic Process**: If $\{s(t)\}$ is a wide-sense stationary stochastic process with bounded second moments, then $E s(t) = m_s$ is constant and $E[s(t)s(t-\tau)] \triangleq R_s(\tau)$ is already independent of $t$. Consequently, the Cesàro time average $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N R_s(\tau) = R_s(\tau)$ holds trivially for every $N$, so every stationary stochastic process is automatically quasi-stationary.

To streamline notation, we define the operator $\overline{E}$ acting on any sequence of random variables (or deterministic functions) $f(t)$ by (Eq. 2.60):

$$
\overline{E} f(t) \triangleq \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E f(t)
$$

Whenever the symbol $\overline{E}$ is written, it carries the **implicit assumption** that the limit as $N \to \infty$ exists. Under this notation, condition (2.59) becomes both an assumption and the definition of $R_s(\tau)$ (Eq. 2.61):

$$
\overline{E}[s(t)s(t - \tau)] = R_s(\tau)
$$

> [!note] Terminology Remark: "Covariance Function" Abuse of Notation
> Ljung notes that $R_s(\tau)$ is referred to throughout the book as the **covariance function** of $s$, even though in strict probability theory it is only a true covariance function when $\{s(t)\}$ is a zero-mean stationary stochastic process (otherwise it is a non-centered second-moment time-average correlation).

**Joint Quasi-Stationarity and Cross-Covariance**:
Two signals $\{s(t)\}$ and $\{w(t)\}$ are said to be **jointly quasi-stationary** if they are each individually quasi-stationary and if, in addition, the **cross-covariance function** limit (Eq. 2.62)

$$
R_{sw}(\tau) \triangleq \overline{E}[s(t)w(t - \tau)] = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E[s(t)w(t - \tau)]
$$

exists for all $\tau \in \mathbb{Z}$. Two jointly quasi-stationary signals are said to be **uncorrelated** if their cross-covariance function is identically zero:

$$
R_{sw}(\tau) \equiv 0, \quad \forall \tau \in \mathbb{Z}
$$

#### 2.2.2 Definition of Power Spectrum and Cross-Spectrum

When the autocovariance limit $R_s(\tau)$ in (2.61) and the cross-covariance limit $R_{sw}(\tau)$ in (2.62) exist, we define:

1. **The (Power) Spectrum of $\{s(t)\}$** (Eq. 2.63):

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) \triangleq \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega}
$$

2. **The Cross-Spectrum between $\{s(t)\}$ and $\{w(t)\}$** (Eq. 2.64):

$$
\Phi_{sw}(\omega) \triangleq \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} R_{sw}(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega}
$$

*(Assumption: Both definitions implicitly assume that the infinite series converge, either in the ordinary sense—e.g., when $\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty |R_s(\tau)| < \infty$—or in the distributional sense involving Dirac delta functions for periodic components.)*

**Symmetry and Complex Decomposition Properties**:
- Because $R_s(-\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)s(t+\tau)] = \overline{E}[s(t'-\tau)s(t')] = R_s(\tau)$ is a real, even function of $\tau$, the autospectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ is always **real-valued and even** ($\Phi_s(-\omega) = \Phi_s(\omega)$):

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) = R_s(0) + 2 \sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) \cos(\tau\omega)
$$

- By contrast, $R_{sw}(-\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)w(t+\tau)] = R_{ws}(\tau) \ne R_{sw}(\tau)$ in general, so the cross-spectrum $\Phi_{sw}(\omega)$ is a **complex-valued** function of $\omega$ satisfying Hermitian symmetry $\Phi_{sw}(-\omega) = \Phi_{sw}^*(\omega) = \Phi_{ws}(\omega)$. Its rectangular and polar components have standard signal-processing names:
  - **Cospectrum**: $\operatorname{Re} \Phi_{sw}(\omega)$ (the real part).
  - **Quadrature spectrum**: $\operatorname{Im} \Phi_{sw}(\omega)$ (the imaginary part).
  - **Amplitude spectrum**: $|\Phi_{sw}(\omega)|$ (the modulus).
  - **Phase spectrum**: $\arg \Phi_{sw}(\omega)$ (the phase angle).

**Inverse Fourier Transform and Average Signal Power (Parseval's Relation)**:
Since $\Phi_s(\omega)$ is a Fourier series in $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$ with coefficients $R_s(\tau)$, the inverse discrete-time Fourier transform recovers the autocovariance at any lag $\tau$:

$$
R_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) e^{i\tau\omega} \, d\omega
$$

Evaluating this inverse transform at lag $\tau = 0$ (where $e^{i \cdot 0 \cdot \omega} = 1$) yields the fundamental energy/power decomposition identity (Eq. 2.65):

$$
\overline{E} s^2(t) = R_s(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) \, d\omega
$$

This shows why $\Phi_s(\omega)$ is called the **power spectrum** (or spectral density): $\frac{1}{2\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) \, d\omega$ represents the infinitesimal contribution of the frequency band $[\omega, \omega + d\omega]$ to the total time-averaged mean-square power $\overline{E} s^2(t)$ of the signal.

#### 2.2.3 Canonical Examples of Signal Spectra (Examples 2.3 – 2.6)

##### Example 2.3: Deterministic Periodic Signals (Dirac Line Spectra)
Suppose $\{s(t)\}$ is a deterministic, periodic signal with integer period $M \ge 1$, meaning $s(t) = s(t + M)$ for all $t$. Since $\{s(t)\}$ is deterministic, $E s(t) = s(t)$ and $E[s(t)s(r)] = s(t)s(r)$. Let us compute the finite-horizon time average over $N$ samples by breaking the interval $[1, N]$ into $K$ full periods of length $M$ (where $K = \lfloor N/M \rfloor$ is chosen as the maximum number of full periods so that the remainder satisfies $0 \le N - MK < M$):

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} s(t)s(t - \tau) = \frac{MK}{N} \cdot \frac{1}{K} \sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1} \left\{ \frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^{M} s(t + \ell M)s(t - \tau + \ell M) \right\} + \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=KM+1}^{N} s(t)s(t - \tau)
$$

Let us examine each term as $N \to \infty$ (which implies $K \to \infty$):
1. By periodicity $s(t + \ell M) = s(t)$, the inner sum inside the braces $\{ \cdot \}$ is completely independent of the period index $\ell$:

$$
\frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^{M} s(t + \ell M)s(t - \tau + \ell M) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^{M} s(t)s(t - \tau)
$$

   Averaging this constant over $\ell = 0, \dots, K-1$ leaves it unchanged.
2. Since $0 \le N - MK < M$, we have $1 - \frac{M}{N} < \frac{MK}{N} \le 1$, so $\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{MK}{N} = 1$.
3. The remainder sum $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=KM+1}^N s(t)s(t-\tau)$ contains at most $M - 1$ bounded terms divided by $N$, which vanishes at rate $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ as $N \to \infty$.

Therefore, the limit exists for all $\tau$, proving that every deterministic periodic signal is quasi-stationary with autocovariance:

$$
\overline{E}[s(t)s(t - \tau)] = R_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^{M} s(t)s(t - \tau)
$$

Moreover, $R_s(\tau)$ is itself periodic with period $M$: $R_s(\tau + \ell M) = R_s(\tau)$ for all $\ell \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Now let us compute its spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$. Splitting the infinite sum over $\tau \in (-\infty, \infty)$ into blocks of length $M$ via $\tau' = \tau + \ell M$ (with $\tau \in \{0, 1, \dots, M-1\}$ and $\ell \in \mathbb{Z}$) gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_s(\omega) &= \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) e^{-i\omega\tau} \\
&= \sum_{\ell = -\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{\tau = 0}^{M-1} R_s(\tau + \ell M) e^{-i\omega\tau} e^{-i\omega \ell M} \\
&= \underbrace{\left( \sum_{\tau = 0}^{M-1} R_s(\tau) e^{-i\omega\tau} \right)}_{\Phi_s^p(\omega)} \underbrace{\left( \sum_{\ell = -\infty}^{\infty} e^{-i \ell M \omega} \right)}_{F(\omega, M)} = \Phi_s^p(\omega) F(\omega, M)
\end{aligned}
$$

*(Note on Ljung's p. 36 notation: On the top of page 36, Ljung writes $\Phi_s^p(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=0}^{M-1} R_s(\tau) e^{i\omega\tau}$, which is equivalent at the harmonic frequencies $\omega_k = 2\pi k / M$ because $R_s(\tau)$ is even and $M$-periodic so $\Phi_s^p(\omega)$ is real and even.)*

Although the infinite exponential sum $F(\omega, M) = \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^\infty e^{-i\ell M \omega}$ does not converge in the ordinary pointwise sense, in the sense of tempered distributions (generalized functions / Poisson summation formula, matching Eq. 2.39 in Section 2.2) it equals a periodic train of Dirac delta impulses (Eq. 2.66):

$$
F(\omega, M) = \frac{2\pi}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \delta\!\left(\omega - \frac{2\pi k}{M}\right), \qquad 0 \le \omega < 2\pi
$$

Multiplying $\Phi_s^p(\omega)$ by the Dirac comb $F(\omega, M)$ sifts out the values of $\Phi_s^p(\omega)$ exclusively at the discrete harmonic frequencies $\omega_k = 2\pi k / M$ (Eq. 2.67):

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) = \frac{2\pi}{M} \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} \Phi_s^p\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{M}\right) \delta\!\left(\omega - \frac{2\pi k}{M}\right), \qquad 0 \le \omega < 2\pi
$$

Here $\Phi_s^p(2\pi k / M)$ is the $k$-th finite Fourier coefficient of one period of $R_s(\tau)$. Thus, a periodic signal of period $M$ has a **line spectrum** consisting of at most $M$ Dirac delta spikes located at the harmonic frequencies $\omega_k = 2\pi k / M$ and zero everywhere else.

##### Example 2.4: Spectrum of a Sinusoid
Consider the deterministic sinusoidal input from (2.45), now extended to $t \in [1, \infty)$:

$$
u(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t)
$$

Since $u(t)$ is deterministic, $E[u(k)u(k-\tau)] = u(k)u(k-\tau)$. Forming the finite-sample average (Eq. 2.68):

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} E[u(k)u(k - \tau)] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} A^2 \cos(\omega_0 k) \cos(\omega_0 (k - \tau))
$$

Applying the trigonometric product-to-sum identity $\cos \alpha \cos \beta = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(\alpha + \beta) + \cos(\alpha - \beta)]$ with $\alpha = \omega_0 k$ and $\beta = \omega_0(k - \tau)$ yields:

$$
\cos(\omega_0 k) \cos(\omega_0 (k - \tau)) = \frac{1}{2} \big( \cos(2\omega_0 k - \omega_0 \tau) + \cos(\omega_0 \tau) \big)
$$

When we average over $k = 1, \dots, N$ and take $N \to \infty$ (assuming $\omega_0 \not\equiv 0 \pmod \pi$):
- The oscillating double-frequency term $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \cos(2\omega_0 k - \omega_0 \tau)$ has bounded partial sums and therefore vanishes at rate $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ as $N \to \infty$.
- The lag-dependent term $\cos(\omega_0 \tau)$ is independent of the summation index $k$.

Hence, the asymptotic autocovariance of a sinusoid is:

$$
R_u(\tau) = \overline{E}[u(t)u(t - \tau)] = \frac{A^2}{2} \cos(\omega_0 \tau)
$$

To compute the spectrum $\Phi_u(\omega)$, we expand $\cos(\omega_0 \tau) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{i\omega_0 \tau} + e^{-i\omega_0 \tau})$ and use the distributional Fourier series identity $\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty e^{-i(\omega \mp \omega_0)\tau} = 2\pi \delta(\omega \mp \omega_0)$ for $\omega, \omega_0 \in (-\pi, \pi)$ (Eq. 2.69):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_u(\omega) &= \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \frac{A^2}{2} \cos(\omega_0 \tau) e^{-i\omega\tau} \\
&= \frac{A^2}{4} \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \left( e^{-i(\omega - \omega_0)\tau} + e^{-i(\omega + \omega_0)\tau} \right) \\
&= \frac{A^2}{4} \big( \delta(\omega - \omega_0) + \delta(\omega + \omega_0) \big) \cdot 2\pi
\end{aligned}
$$

**Sanity Check via Parseval's Theorem (2.65)**: Integrating $\Phi_u(\omega)$ over $[-\pi, \pi]$ gives:

$$
\overline{E} u^2(t) = R_u(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \frac{A^2}{4} \big( \delta(\omega - \omega_0) + \delta(\omega + \omega_0) \big) \cdot 2\pi \, d\omega = \frac{A^2}{4}(1 + 1) = \frac{A^2}{2}
$$

which matches the average power $\frac{A^2}{2}$ of a sinusoid of amplitude $A$.

##### Example 2.5: Stationary Stochastic Processes and Filtered White Noise
Let $\{v(t)\}$ be a stationary stochastic process generated by passing white noise $\{e(t)\}$ (a sequence of independent random variables with zero mean $E e(t) = 0$ and variance $E e^2(t) = \lambda$) through a stable causal LTI filter $H(q) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty h(k) q^{-k}$, as defined in Section 2.1 (Eq. 2.9):

$$
v(t) = H(q)e(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) e(t - k)
$$

From Section 2.1 (Eq. 2.13), its stationary autocovariance function for $\tau \ge 0$ (and extended to all $\tau \in \mathbb{Z}$ via $h(\ell) = 0$ for $\ell < 0$) is:

$$
R_v(\tau) = E[v(t)v(t - \tau)] = \lambda \sum_{k = \max(0, \tau)}^{\infty} h(k) h(k - \tau)
$$

Let us derive the power spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega)$ step-by-step by substituting $R_v(\tau)$ into definition (2.63):

1. **Insert $R_v(\tau)$ into the spectral sum**:

$$
\Phi_v(\omega) = \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \lambda e^{-i\tau\omega} \sum_{k = \max(0, \tau)}^{\infty} h(k) h(k - \tau)
$$

2. **Split the exponential factor $e^{-i\tau\omega}$**: Write $-i\tau\omega = -ik\omega + i(k - \tau)\omega$ so that each impulse-response factor $h(k)$ and $h(k-\tau)$ is paired with its matching time index:

$$
\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{k = \max(0, \tau)}^{\infty} h(k) e^{-ik\omega} h(k - \tau) e^{i(k - \tau)\omega}
$$

3. **Change summation variables**: Define $s = k - \tau$. Because $k \ge \max(0, \tau)$, both $k \ge 0$ and $s = k - \tau \ge 0$ range independently over $[0, \infty)$ as $\tau$ ranges over $(-\infty, \infty)$ and $k$ ranges over $[\max(0, \tau), \infty)$. Factoring the double sum into a product of two independent single sums yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_v(\omega) &= \lambda \left( \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(s) e^{is\omega} \right) \left( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) e^{-ik\omega} \right) \\
&= \lambda H(e^{-i\omega}) H(e^{i\omega}) = \lambda \left| H(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2
\end{aligned}
$$

   where we used the definition of the frequency function $H(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty h(k) e^{-ik\omega}$ (Eq. 2.18) and the fact that for real impulse-response coefficients $h(s) \in \mathbb{R}$, $\sum_{s=0}^\infty h(s)e^{is\omega} = \overline{H(e^{i\omega})} = H(e^{-i\omega})$.

> [!important] Fundamental Result: Spectrum of Filtered White Noise (Eq. 2.70)
> The stochastic process described by $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$, where $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random variables with zero mean values and variances $\lambda$, has the power spectrum:
>
> $$
> \Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda \left| H(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2
> $$
>
> **Special Case (Pure White Noise)**: When $H(q) = 1$, $v(t) = e(t)$, and $\Phi_e(\omega) = \lambda \cdot |1|^2 = \lambda$ for all $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$. Because its spectral density is completely flat (constant across all frequencies), $\{e(t)\}$ is called **white noise** by analogy to white light.

> \Phi_v(\omega) = 1 \cdot \left| \frac{1 + 0.5 e^{-i\omega}}{1 - 1.5 e^{-i\omega} + 0.7 e^{-2i\omega}} \right|^2
> $$
>
> - **Low-Frequency Plateau ($\omega \to 0$)**: At DC ($\omega = 0$, $e^{-i\omega} = 1$), $H(1) = \frac{1 + 0.5}{1 - 1.5 + 0.7} = \frac{1.5}{0.2} = 7.5$, giving $\Phi_v(0) = (7.5)^2 = 56.25$, which matches the flat horizontal asymptote just above $50$ on the logarithmic vertical axis.
> - **Resonant Peak & High-Frequency Roll-off**: The complex-conjugate poles of $1 - 1.5 z^{-1} + 0.7 z^{-2} = 0$ are at $z = 0.75 \pm i\sqrt{0.7 - 0.5625} \approx 0.837 e^{\pm i 0.46}$, creating the gentle resonant peak near $\omega \approx 0.4\text{–}0.46\text{ rad/sample}$ (corresponding to period $2\pi / 0.45 \approx 14$ samples, visible as the slow oscillation in Figure 2.5/2.6), followed by a steep low-pass roll-off above $\omega > 0.5\text{ rad/sample}$.

##### Example 2.6: Spectrum of a Mixed Deterministic and Stochastic Signal
Consider now a composite signal (Eq. 2.71):

$$
s(t) = u(t) + v(t)
$$

where $\{u(t)\}$ is a deterministic quasi-stationary signal with autocovariance $R_u(\tau)$ and spectrum $\Phi_u(\omega)$, and $\{v(t)\}$ is a zero-mean stationary stochastic process ($E v(t) = 0$) with autocovariance $R_v(\tau)$ and spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega)$. Let us compute the autocovariance of $\{s(t)\}$ under the $\overline{E}$ operator (Eq. 2.72):

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_s(\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)s(t - \tau)] &= \overline{E}\big[(u(t) + v(t))(u(t - \tau) + v(t - \tau))\big] \\
&= \overline{E}[u(t)u(t - \tau)] + \overline{E}[u(t)v(t - \tau)] + \overline{E}[v(t)u(t - \tau)] + \overline{E}[v(t)v(t - \tau)]
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why the Cross-Terms Vanish**: Look closely at the cross-term $\overline{E}[v(t)u(t - \tau)]$. Inside $\overline{E}$, we first evaluate the ensemble expectation $E[v(t)u(t - \tau)]$. Because $u(t - \tau)$ is **deterministic**, it acts as a non-random constant with respect to the expectation operator $E$, so it factors straight out:

$$
E[v(t)u(t - \tau)] = u(t - \tau) E[v(t)] = u(t - \tau) \cdot 0 = 0, \quad \forall t, \tau
$$

Consequently, $\overline{E}[v(t)u(t - \tau)] = 0$ and similarly $\overline{E}[u(t)v(t - \tau)] = 0$! This gives the clean additive decomposition (Eqs. 2.72 & 2.73):

$$
R_s(\tau) = R_u(\tau) + R_v(\tau) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \Phi_s(\omega) = \Phi_u(\omega) + \Phi_v(\omega)
$$

#### 2.2.4 Connections to the Periodogram (Weak Convergence & Lemma 2.1)

Although the periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ does not converge pointwise to $\Phi_s(\omega)$ as $N \to \infty$ (as seen in Figure 2.8), its **expected value converges weakly** (in the sense of distributions / local frequency averages) to the spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ (Eq. 2.74):

$$
E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 \underset{w}{\longrightarrow} \Phi_s(\omega) \quad \text{as } N \to \infty
$$

By weak convergence ($\underset{w}{\to}$), Ljung means that when integrated against any sufficiently smooth test function (weighting window) $\Psi(\omega)$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$, the integral of the expected periodogram converges to the integral of the true spectrum (Eq. 2.75):

$$
\lim_{N \to \infty} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega
$$

> [!important] Lemma 2.1: Weak Convergence of the Expected Periodogram to the Spectrum
> **Statement**: Suppose that $\{s(t)\}$ is quasi-stationary with spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$. Let
>
> $$
> S_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} s(t) e^{-it\omega}
> $$
>
> and let $\Psi(\omega)$ be an arbitrary function for $|\omega| \le \pi$ with Fourier coefficients
>
> $$
> a_\tau = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Psi(\omega) e^{-i\omega\tau} \, d\omega
> $$
>
> such that $\{a_\tau\}$ is absolutely summable:
>
> $$
> \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} |a_\tau| < \infty
> $$
>
> Then (2.75) holds:
>
> $$
> \lim_{N \to \infty} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega
> $$
>

##### Complete Step-by-Step Proof of Lemma 2.1

1. **Expand the expected periodogram as a double sum**:
   Using $S_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^N s(t) e^{-it\omega}$ and $|S_N(\omega)|^2 = S_N(\omega) \overline{S_N(\omega)}$, we write:

$$
E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 = E\left[ \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\ell=1}^{N} s(\ell) e^{-i\ell\omega} \right) \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{N} s(k) e^{ik\omega} \right) \right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \sum_{\ell=1}^{N} E[s(k)s(\ell)] e^{i\omega(k - \ell)}
$$

2. **Change variables to lag $\tau = \ell - k$ (or $k - \ell$)**:
   Let $\ell - k = \tau$ (note that since $R_s(t, r)$ is symmetric in $(k, \ell)$, setting $\ell - k = \tau$ or $k - \ell = \tau$ gives the same real sum; following Ljung's Eq. 2.76-2.77):

$$
E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 = \sum_{\tau = -(N-1)}^{N-1} R_N(\tau) e^{-i\omega\tau}
$$

   where $R_N(\tau)$ is the finite-sample expected covariance defined with the **zero-padding convention** that $s(k)$ is taken as zero outside the interval $[1, N]$ (Eq. 2.77):

$$
R_N(\tau) \triangleq \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} E[s(k)s(k - \tau)] = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k = 1 + \tau}^{N} E[s(k)s(k - \tau)], & 0 \le \tau \le N - 1 \\[10pt] \displaystyle \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k = 1}^{N + \tau} E[s(k)s(k - \tau)], & -(N-1) \le \tau < 0 \end{cases}
$$

   Notice why $R_N(\tau) \to R_s(\tau)$ for each fixed $\tau$ as $N \to \infty$: for a fixed $\tau \ge 0$, the sum in $R_N(\tau)$ differs from $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N E[s(k)s(k-\tau)]$ only by the $\tau$ missing initial terms $k = 1, \dots, \tau$. Since $|E[s(k)s(k-\tau)]| \le C$ by quasi-stationarity (2.58ii), those $\tau$ missing terms are bounded by $\frac{\tau C}{N} \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$. Thus $\lim_{N\to\infty} R_N(\tau) = R_s(\tau)$ pointwise for every fixed $\tau$, and $|R_N(\tau)| \le C$ uniformly for all $N$ and $\tau$.

3. **Integrate $E|S_N(\omega)|^2$ against the test function $\Psi(\omega)$**:
   Multiplying (2.76) (which is a finite sum of $2N - 1$ terms) by $\Psi(\omega)$ and integrating over $[-\pi, \pi]$ gives:

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega = \sum_{\tau = -(N-1)}^{N-1} R_N(\tau) \underbrace{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Psi(\omega) e^{-i\omega\tau} \, d\omega}_{a_\tau} = \sum_{\tau = -(N-1)}^{N-1} R_N(\tau) a_\tau
$$

4. **Integrate the true spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ against $\Psi(\omega)$**:
   Similarly, substituting $\Phi_s(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty R_s(\tau) e^{-i\omega\tau}$ and interchanging summation and integration gives:

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega = \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Psi(\omega) e^{-i\omega\tau} \, d\omega = \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) a_\tau
$$

5. **Bound the difference and take the limit $N \to \infty$**:
   Subtracting the two expressions splits the error into a finite-lag estimation error and a high-lag tail remainder:

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} E\left| S_N(\omega) \right|^2 \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega - \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) \Psi(\omega) \, d\omega = \sum_{\tau = -(N-1)}^{N-1} a_\tau \big[ R_N(\tau) - R_s(\tau) \big] - \sum_{|\tau| \ge N} a_\tau R_s(\tau)
$$

   *(Note: Ljung writes $+ \sum_{|\tau|\ge N}$ on p. 40 to represent the tail terms of the difference; in magnitude both are bounded identically via the triangle inequality.)*
   Now let us show why this sum converges to $0$ as $N \to \infty$ (which is the content of Problem 2D.5):
   - By quasi-stationarity (2.58ii), both $|R_N(\tau)| \le C$ and $|R_s(\tau)| \le C$ for all $\tau$ and $N$.
   - Given any $\varepsilon > 0$, because $\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty |a_\tau| < \infty$, we can choose a fixed cutoff lag $M_\varepsilon$ large enough that the tail of the Fourier coefficients satisfies $\sum_{|\tau| > M_\varepsilon} |a_\tau| < \frac{\varepsilon}{4C}$.
   - Then for all $N > M_\varepsilon$, we split the sum over $|\tau| \le M_\varepsilon$ and $|\tau| > M_\varepsilon$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left| \sum_{\tau = -(N-1)}^{N-1} a_\tau \big[ R_N(\tau) - R_s(\tau) \big] - \sum_{|\tau| \ge N} a_\tau R_s(\tau) \right|
&\le \sum_{|\tau| \le M_\varepsilon} |a_\tau| \cdot \big| R_N(\tau) - R_s(\tau) \big| + \sum_{|\tau| > M_\varepsilon} |a_\tau| \cdot \underbrace{\big( |R_N(\tau)| + |R_s(\tau)| \big)}_{\le 2C} \\
&< \sum_{|\tau| \le M_\varepsilon} |a_\tau| \cdot \big| R_N(\tau) - R_s(\tau) \big| + 2C \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{4C}
\end{aligned}
$$

   - Since the first sum has only a **finite, fixed number** ($2M_\varepsilon + 1$) of terms and $\lim_{N\to\infty} |R_N(\tau) - R_s(\tau)| = 0$ for each fixed $\tau \in [-M_\varepsilon, M_\varepsilon]$, taking $\limsup_{N\to\infty}$ makes the first sum $0$, leaving at most $\varepsilon/2 < \varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, the limit is $0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!tip] Two Crucial Remarks on Lemma 2.1 (Book p. 40)
> 1. **When Does Ordinary Pointwise Convergence Hold?**: For stationary stochastic processes whose covariance function decays fast enough (specifically $\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty |\tau| |R_s(\tau)| < \infty$), the weak convergence $E|S_N(\omega)|^2 \underset{w}{\to} \Phi_s(\omega)$ in (2.74) can be strengthened to **ordinary pointwise convergence** $\lim_{N\to\infty} E|S_N(\omega)|^2 = \Phi_s(\omega)$ at every $\omega$ (Problem 2D.3). Why is weak convergence needed in general? Because for deterministic periodic or sinusoidal signals, $\Phi_s(\omega)$ contains Dirac delta spikes (where pointwise convergence of smooth functions fails), whereas integrating against $\Psi(\omega)$ remains completely well-defined!
> 2. **Applying Lemma 2.1 to a Single Realization**: Because our framework treats deterministic sequences on the same footing as stochastic processes, we can apply (2.74)–(2.75) to a **single observed realization** of a stochastic process simply by dropping the expectation operator $E$—provided that this particular realization satisfies the deterministic time-average limits (2.58)–(2.59) (which Section 2.4 proves holds with probability 1 for ergodic processes!).

#### 2.2.5 Transformation of Spectra by Linear Systems (Theorem 2.2 & Corollary)

Just as Theorem 2.1 showed how a stable LTI filter $G(q)$ transforms finite-sample Fourier transforms ($S_N(\omega) \approx G(e^{i\omega})W_N(\omega)$), **Theorem 2.2** establishes how $G(q)$ transforms asymptotic power spectra and cross-spectra for general quasi-stationary signals.

> [!important] Theorem 2.2: Transformation of Spectra by Linear Time-Invariant Systems
> Let $\{w(t)\}$ be a quasi-stationary signal with spectrum $\Phi_w(\omega)$, and let $G(q) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty g(k)q^{-k}$ be a stable transfer function ($\sum_{k=0}^\infty |g(k)| < \infty$). Let the output $\{s(t)\}$ be given by (Eq. 2.78):
>
> $$
> s(t) = G(q)w(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} g(k) w(t - k)
> $$
>
> Then $\{s(t)\}$ is also quasi-stationary, $\{s(t)\}$ and $\{w(t)\}$ are jointly quasi-stationary, and their autospectrum and cross-spectrum are given by (Eqs. 2.79 & 2.80):
>
> $$
> \Phi_s(\omega) = \left| G(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 \Phi_w(\omega)
> $$
>
> $$
> \Phi_{sw}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_w(\omega)
> $$
>
> *(Note: In the multivariable case where $w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and $G(q)$ is a $p \times m$ matrix transfer function, Appendix 2A proves the matrix form $\Phi_s(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_w(\omega) G^T(e^{-i\omega})$ and $\Phi_{sw}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_w(\omega)$. See Subsection 2.2.8 below for the complete proof of Appendix 2A.)*

**Intuitive Heuristic Connection to Theorem 2.1**:
From Theorem 2.1 in Section 2.2, $S_N(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})W_N(\omega) + R_N(\omega)$ where $|R_N(\omega)| = \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$. Forming the periodogram and cross-periodogram gives:

$$
|S_N(\omega)|^2 \approx \left| G(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 |W_N(\omega)|^2, \qquad S_N(\omega)\overline{W_N(\omega)} \approx G(e^{i\omega}) |W_N(\omega)|^2
$$

Taking expectations and weak limits as $N \to \infty$ via Lemma 2.1 directly explains why $\Phi_s(\omega) = |G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_w(\omega)$ and $\Phi_{sw}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_w(\omega)$.

##### Corollary to Theorem 2.2: Spectra of Open-Loop LTI Systems
Now let us apply Theorem 2.2 to the standard linear system structure (Eq. 2.81):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)
$$

> [!important] Corollary to Theorem 2.2 (Open-Loop System Spectra, Eqs. 2.81 – 2.83)
> **Assumptions**:
> 1. $\{u(t)\}$ is a **quasi-stationary, deterministic** input signal with spectrum $\Phi_u(\omega)$.
> 2. $\{e(t)\}$ is **white noise** with zero mean and variance $\lambda$.
> 3. $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ are **stable** LTI filters.
> *(Implicit Open-Loop Assumption: Because $\{u(t)\}$ is deterministic and $E e(t) = 0$, $\{u(t)\}$ and $\{e(t)\}$ are uncorrelated: $\overline{E}[u(t)e(s)] = u(t) E[e(s)] = 0$.)*
>
> **Conclusions**: Then $\{y(t)\}$ is quasi-stationary, and its output power spectrum $\Phi_y(\omega)$ and input-output cross-spectrum $\Phi_{yu}(\omega)$ are given by:
>
> $$
> \Phi_y(\omega) = \left| G(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \lambda \left| H(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 \tag{2.82}
> $$
>
> $$
> \Phi_{yu}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_u(\omega) \tag{2.83}
> $$
>

**Proof of the Corollary**:
Let $y_u(t) \triangleq G(q)u(t)$ and $v(t) \triangleq H(q)e(t)$, so that $y(t) = y_u(t) + v(t)$.
1. By Theorem 2.2 applied to the deterministic quasi-stationary signal $\{u(t)\}$, $\{y_u(t)\}$ is a deterministic quasi-stationary signal with spectrum $\Phi_{y_u}(\omega) = |G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_u(\omega)$ and cross-spectrum $\Phi_{y_u u}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_u(\omega)$.
2. By Example 2.5 (or Theorem 2.2 applied to $\{e(t)\}$ with $\Phi_e(\omega) = \lambda$), $\{v(t)\}$ is a zero-mean stationary stochastic process with spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$.
3. By Example 2.6, because $\{y_u(t)\}$ is deterministic and $\{v(t)\}$ has zero ensemble mean ($E v(t) = 0$), their cross-covariance vanishes: $\overline{E}[v(t)y_u(t-\tau)] = 0$ and $\overline{E}[v(t)u(t-\tau)] = 0$. Therefore:
   - $\Phi_y(\omega) = \Phi_{y_u}(\omega) + \Phi_v(\omega) = |G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$.
   - $R_{yu}(\tau) = \overline{E}[(y_u(t) + v(t))u(t-\tau)] = R_{y_u u}(\tau) + 0$, which upon Fourier transformation yields $\Phi_{yu}(\omega) = \Phi_{y_u u}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_u(\omega)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Crucial Engineering Insight: Open-Loop vs. Closed-Loop Spectral Relations
> Look closely at Equation (2.83):
>
> $$
> G(e^{i\omega}) = \frac{\Phi_{yu}(\omega)}{\Phi_u(\omega)} \qquad (\text{at frequencies where } \Phi_u(\omega) > 0)
> $$
>
> This is the theoretical foundation of **spectral analysis system identification** (Chapter 6): in open loop, the disturbance $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ is completely orthogonal to the input $u(t)$, so dividing the input-output cross-spectrum $\Phi_{yu}(\omega)$ by the input autospectrum $\Phi_u(\omega)$ recovers the exact plant frequency response $G(e^{i\omega})$ regardless of the noise model $H(q)$!
>
> **What happens if $\{u(t)\}$ is generated by closed-loop feedback from $y(t)$?**
> If a feedback controller adjusts $u(t)$ based on past outputs $y(t)$, then $u(t)$ is **no longer purely deterministic**—it acquires a stochastic component correlated with the noise $e(t)$, so $\Phi_{ve}(\omega) \ne 0$ and $\Phi_{vu}(\omega) \ne 0$. Applying the multivariable Theorem 2.2 to the vector signal $w(t) = [u(t) \;\; v(t)]^T$ passed through $y(t) = [G(q) \;\; 1] w(t)$ gives the general correlated formula:
>
> $$
> \Phi_y(\omega) = \left| G(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \Phi_v(\omega) + G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uv}(\omega) + G(e^{-i\omega})\Phi_{vu}(\omega)
> $$
>
> $$
> \Phi_{yu}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_u(\omega) + \Phi_{vu}(\omega) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{\Phi_{yu}(\omega)}{\Phi_u(\omega)} = G(e^{i\omega}) + \frac{\Phi_{vu}(\omega)}{\Phi_u(\omega)} \ne G(e^{i\omega})
> $$
>
> Thus, naive cross-spectral division $\Phi_{yu}(\omega)/\Phi_u(\omega)$ is biased in closed loop whenever $\Phi_{vu}(\omega) \ne 0$!

#### 2.2.6 Spectral Factorization and Canonical ARMA Disturbance Models

In Example 2.5 and Theorem 2.2, we solved the **forward problem**: given a filter $H(q)$ and white noise $\{e(t)\}$ of variance $\lambda$, the process $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ has spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$.

In system identification, we face the **inverse (converse) problem**:
> Given only the power spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega)$ of a stationary disturbance, can we find a stable, causal, and causally invertible transfer function $H(q)$ and white-noise variance $\lambda$ such that $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ produces the exact spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$?

##### Why Arbitrary Positive Functions $\Phi_v(\omega)$ Cannot Always Be Factored (The Analyticity / Paley-Wiener Obstruction)
Ljung explains a deep complex-analysis subtlety: **not every non-negative function $\Phi_v(\omega) \ge 0$ can be the spectrum of a causally filtered white-noise process $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$!**
Suppose, for instance, that $\Phi_v(\omega) = 0$ on some finite frequency interval $[\omega_1, \omega_2] \subset [-\pi, \pi]$ (an ideal band-stop spectrum). If $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$, then the complex transfer function $H(z)$ evaluated on the unit circle $z = e^{i\omega}$ must vanish identically along the continuous arc $\omega \in [\omega_1, \omega_2]$.
However, for the causal filter expansion $H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty h(k) z^{-k}$ (Eq. 2.18) to be stable and well-defined, $H(z)$ must be an **analytic function** of $z$ outside and on the unit circle ($|z| \ge 1$). By the **Identity Theorem of Complex Analysis** (or the Paley-Wiener criterion $\int_{-\pi}^\pi |\ln \Phi_v(\omega)| \, d\omega < \infty$), an analytic function that vanishes on a continuous arc of the unit circle must vanish identically everywhere ($H(z) \equiv 0$), which cannot match a nonzero spectrum!

Fortunately, in engineering practice we model disturbances using **rational transfer functions** (quotients of polynomials in $q^{-1}$). Because $\Phi_v(\omega)$ is a real, even function of $\omega$, any rational spectrum is a rational function of $\cos \omega = \frac{1}{2}(e^{i\omega} + e^{-i\omega})$ (or equivalently of $z = e^{i\omega}$ with symmetric poles and zeros in $z$ and $z^{-1}$). For strictly positive rational spectra, the inverse problem always has a unique canonical solution:

> [!important] Spectral Factorization Theorem (Book p. 41)
> Suppose that $\Phi_v(\omega) > 0$ (strictly positive for all $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$) is a rational function of $\cos \omega$ (or $e^{i\omega}$). Then there exists a unique **monic** rational function of $z$, $R(z)$ (meaning $\lim_{z \to \infty} R(z) = R(\infty) = 1$, so its leading impulse-response coefficient is $r(0) = 1$), with:
> 1. **No poles on or outside the unit circle** ($|z| \ge 1$, so $R(q)$ is **asymptotically stable**), and
> 2. **No zeros on or outside the unit circle** ($|z| \ge 1$, so $R^{-1}(q) = 1/R(q)$ is also **asymptotically stable**, i.e., $R(q)$ is **minimum-phase**),
>
> and a unique positive scalar $\lambda > 0$ such that:
>
> $$
> \Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda \left| R(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 = \lambda R(e^{i\omega}) R(e^{-i\omega})
> $$
>

**How the Construction of $R(z)$ Works (Reciprocal Pole-Zero Pairing)**:
Why does spectral factorization work for rational functions of $\cos \omega$? Replacing $\cos \omega$ by $\frac{1}{2}(z + z^{-1})$ converts $\Phi_v(\omega)$ into a rational function $\tilde{\Phi}_v(z)$ on the complex plane satisfying $\tilde{\Phi}_v(z) = \tilde{\Phi}_v(z^{-1})$. Because of this $z \leftrightarrow z^{-1}$ symmetry:
- Every pole $p_i$ inside the unit circle ($|p_i| < 1$) is mirrored by a reciprocal pole $1/p_i$ outside the unit circle ($|1/p_i| > 1$).
- Every zero $z_j$ inside the unit circle ($|z_j| < 1$) is mirrored by a reciprocal zero $1/z_j$ outside the unit circle ($|1/z_j| > 1$).
- Because $\Phi_v(\omega) > 0$ on the unit circle $|z| = 1$, there are no poles or zeros on the unit circle itself (or any unit-circle zeros would have even multiplicity, which is excluded by $\Phi_v(\omega) > 0$).

To construct the canonical spectral factor $R(z)$, we simply collect **all poles and zeros that lie strictly inside the unit circle ($|z| < 1$)** into $R(z)$, while assigning their mirror images outside the unit circle ($|z| > 1$) to $R(z^{-1})$, and normalize $R(z)$ to be monic ($R(\infty) = 1$), absorbing the remaining positive gain into $\lambda$!
Because flipping a factor $(1 - \alpha z^{-1})$ to $(1 - \alpha^{-1} z^{-1}) = -\alpha^{-1} z^{-1}(z - \alpha)$ does not change the magnitude squared $|1 - \alpha e^{-i\omega}|^2 = |\alpha|^2 |1 - \alpha^{-1} e^{-i\omega}|^2$ on the unit circle, requiring $R(z)$ to be **monic, stable, and minimum-phase** makes the factorization $(\lambda, R(z))$ **unique**.

##### Example 2.7: ARMA, AR, and MA Processes (Eqs. 2.84 – 2.87)
By the Spectral Factorization Theorem, any stationary stochastic process $\{v(t)\}$ with a strictly positive rational spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) > 0$ can be represented in the canonical innovations form (Eq. 2.84):

$$
v(t) = R(q)e(t) = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)} e(t)
$$

where $\{e(t)\}$ is white noise with variance $\lambda$, and $R(q) = C(q)/A(q)$ is a rational transfer function defined by the monic polynomials in the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$:

$$
C(q) = 1 + c_1 q^{-1} + \dots + c_{n_c} q^{-n_c}
$$

$$
A(q) = 1 + a_1 q^{-1} + \dots + a_{n_a} q^{-n_a}
$$

Multiplying (2.84) through by $A(q)$ yields the linear constant-coefficient stochastic difference equation (Eq. 2.85):

$$
v(t) + a_1 v(t - 1) + \dots + a_{n_a} v(t - n_a) = e(t) + c_1 e(t - 1) + \dots + c_{n_c} e(t - n_c)
$$

Depending on the polynomial orders $n_a$ and $n_c$, this defines the three foundational time-series families:

1. **Autoregressive Moving Average — $\text{ARMA}(n_a, n_c)$ Model** (Eq. 2.85): Both $n_a > 0$ and $n_c > 0$. The current disturbance $v(t)$ regresses on $n_a$ past values of itself and a moving average of $n_c$ past white-noise shocks.
2. **Autoregressive — $\text{AR}(n_a)$ Model** (Eq. 2.86): When $n_c = 0$ (so $C(q) = 1$ and $R(q) = \frac{1}{A(q)}$ is an all-pole filter):

$$
v(t) + a_1 v(t - 1) + \dots + a_{n_a} v(t - n_a) = e(t)
$$

3. **Moving Average — $\text{MA}(n_c)$ Model** (Eq. 2.87): When $n_a = 0$ (so $A(q) = 1$ and $R(q) = C(q)$ is a finite impulse response / all-zero filter):

$$
v(t) = e(t) + c_1 e(t - 1) + \dots + c_{n_c} e(t - n_c)
$$

> [!tip] Engineering Significance of Spectral Factorization
> In engineering practice, we rarely know the physical internal mechanism generating process disturbances, but we often have reliable frequency-domain intuition or empirical spectral estimates—e.g., *"The disturbances are concentrated around $50\text{ Hz}$ mains hum"* or *"We have low-frequency drift with little power above $1\text{ rad/s}$."* Because rational functions $C(q)/A(q)$ can approximate virtually any smooth spectral shape to arbitrary accuracy, **Spectral Factorization** guarantees that we can always represent such disturbances in the canonical model form $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ with $H(q)$ stable, monic, and minimum-phase.

#### 2.2.7 Second-Order Properties

Ljung closes Section 2.3 with an essential perspective on what signal spectra do—and do not—capture:
- **What the spectrum captures**: The power spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ is the Fourier transform of $R_s(\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)]$. Consequently, $\Phi_s(\omega)$ and $R_s(\tau)$ contain the exact same information: they describe the **second-order properties** (for stochastic processes, the first and second moments) of the signal.
- **What the spectrum ignores**: Recall from Section 2.1 (Figures 2.5 and 2.6) that two stochastic processes can have the **exact same autocovariance function $R_v(\tau)$ and the exact same power spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega)$** (shown in Figure 2.7), yet exhibit drastically different time-domain realizations—for example, Figure 2.5 was generated by Gaussian white noise $e(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, whereas Figure 2.6 was generated by rare, heavy-tailed impulse noise ($e(t) = 0$ with probability $0.9$, $e(t) = \pm\sqrt{10}$ with probability $0.05$ each), producing sporadic bursty transients!
- **Why second-order properties dominate system identification**: Despite ignoring higher-order moments and probability distributions, we will discover in Chapters 7–9 that the asymptotic properties of quadratic prediction-error and spectral system identification methods depend **only** on the second-order spectra $\Phi_u(\omega)$, $\Phi_v(\omega)$, and $\Phi_{yu}(\omega)$ of the involved signals! This is why Section 2.3 is the analytical backbone of the entire textbook.

### 2.3 Complete Mathematical Appendices Supporting Section 2.3

#### 2.3.1 Appendix 2A: Proof of Theorem 2.2 and Uniformly Stable Filter Corollary (Book pp. 52–55)

Ljung proves Theorem 2.2 in **Appendix 2A** in its full **multivariable** generality, where $w(t)$ is a column vector of dimension $m$, $G(q) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty g(k)q^{-k}$ is a $p \times m$ matrix transfer function whose impulse-response matrices satisfy $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \|g(k)\| < \infty$, and $s(t) = G(q)w(t)$ is a $p \times 1$ vector signal.

##### Step 1: Finite-Sample Outer-Product Covariance Decomposition (Eqs. 2A.1 – 2A.2)
Assume initially that $w(s) = 0$ for $s \le 0$. Consider the finite-sample expected covariance matrix of $s(t)$ over $t = 1, \dots, N$ (Eq. 2A.1):

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_s^N(\tau) &\triangleq \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E\big[ s(t) s^T(t - \tau) \big] \\
&= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{k=0}^{t} \sum_{\ell=0}^{t-\tau} g(k) E\big[ w(t - k) w^T(t - \tau - \ell) \big] g^T(\ell)
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the zero-padding convention that $w(s) = 0$ if $s \notin [0, N]$, we can extend the upper limits of the sums over $k$ and $\ell$ to $N$ and bring the sum over $t$ inside (Eq. 2A.2):

$$
R_s^N(\tau) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \sum_{\ell=0}^{N} g(k) \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E\big[ w(t - k) w^T(t - \tau - \ell) \big] \right) g^T(\ell)
$$

*(What if $w(s) \ne 0$ for $s \le 0$? Then $s(t)$ receives an initial-condition transient contribution $\bar{s}(t) = \sum_{k=t}^\infty g(k)w(t-k)$. Because $|w(s)|$ is bounded and $\sum_{k=t}^\infty |g(k)| \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ by stability of $G(q)$, $\bar{s}(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$, so its time-average contribution to $R_s^N(\tau)$ vanishes as $N \to \infty$.)*

##### Step 2: Bounding the Boundary Lag Discrepancy (Eq. 2A.3)
Define the finite-sample covariance of the input $w(t)$ at lag $\tau$:

$$
R_w^N(\tau) \triangleq \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E\big[ w(t) w^T(t - \tau) \big]
$$

Let us compare $R_w^N(\tau + \ell - k) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t'=1}^N E[w(t')w^T(t' - \tau - \ell + k)]$ with the inner sum $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N E[w(t - k)w^T(t - \tau - \ell)]$ in (2A.2). Under the index shift $t' = t - k$, the two sums contain the exact same summands except near the boundaries $t \in [1, N]$ vs. $t' \in [1, N]$, where they differ by at most $\max(k, |\tau + \ell|)$ summands!
Since each summand $\|E[w(t)w^T(r)]\| \le C$ by quasi-stationarity (2.58ii), the difference between $R_w^N(\tau + \ell - k)$ and the inner sum in (2A.2) is bounded by (Eq. 2A.3):

$$
\left| R_w^N(\tau + \ell - k) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E\big[ w(t - k) w^T(t - \tau - \ell) \big] \right| \le C \frac{\max(k, |\tau + \ell|)}{N} \le \frac{C}{N} \big( k + |\tau + \ell| \big)
$$

##### Step 3: Four-Term Error Decomposition of $R_s(\tau) - R_s^N(\tau)$ (Eqs. 2A.4 – 2A.5)
Define the candidate asymptotic covariance matrix of $s(t)$ as the double infinite series (Eq. 2A.4):

$$
R_s(\tau) \triangleq \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} g(k) R_w(\tau + \ell - k) g^T(\ell)
$$

Subtracting $R_s^N(\tau)$ in (2A.2) from $R_s(\tau)$ in (2A.4) and applying the triangle inequality together with the bound (2A.3) yields the four-sum bound (Eq. 2A.5):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\big| R_s(\tau) - R_s^N(\tau) \big| &\le \underbrace{\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=N+1}^{\infty} |g(k)| |g(\ell)| |R_w(\tau + \ell - k)|}_{\text{Term 1 (plus tail strips } k>N \text{ or } \ell>N\text{)}} \\
&\quad + \underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{N} \sum_{\ell=0}^{N} |g(k)| |g(\ell)| \big| R_w(\tau + \ell - k) - R_w^N(\tau + \ell - k) \big|}_{\text{Term 2}} \\
&\quad + \underbrace{\frac{C}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N} k |g(k)| \cdot \sum_{\ell=0}^{N} |g(\ell)|}_{\text{Term 3}} + \underbrace{\frac{C}{N} \sum_{\ell=0}^{N} |\tau + \ell| |g(\ell)| \cdot \sum_{k=0}^{N} |g(k)|}_{\text{Term 4}}
\end{aligned}
$$

##### Step 4: Proving All Four Terms Converge to Zero as $N \to \infty$ (Eqs. 2A.6 – 2A.8)
- **Term 1**: Since $|R_w(\cdot)| \le C$ and $G(q)$ is stable ($\sum_{k=0}^\infty |g(k)| = C_1 < \infty$), the tail sum over $k > N$ or $\ell > N$ vanishes as $N \to \infty$.
- **Terms 3 and 4**: Because $\sum_{k=0}^\infty |g(k)| = C_1 < \infty$, Kraken/Kronecker's lemma (Problem 2D.4) guarantees that the weighted Cesàro average satisfies (Eq. 2A.6):

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N} k |g(k)| \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } N \to \infty
$$

  (Indeed, splitting the sum at a fixed cutoff $K$ gives $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^K k|g(k)| + \sum_{k=K+1}^N \frac{k}{N}|g(k)| \le \frac{K C_1}{N} + \sum_{k=K+1}^\infty |g(k)|$, which can be made arbitrarily small by choosing $K$ large and then $N \to \infty$.) Since $\tau$ is fixed, $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^N |\tau + \ell| |g(\ell)| \le \frac{|\tau|C_1}{N} + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^N \ell|g(\ell)| \to 0$ as well. Hence Terms 3 and 4 tend to zero as $N \to \infty$.
- **Term 2**: Fix an arbitrary $\varepsilon > 0$. Because $G(q)$ is stable ($C_1 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty |g(k)| < \infty$), we can choose a fixed truncation horizon $N_\varepsilon$ such that the filter tail satisfies (Eq. 2A.7):

$$
\sum_{k = N_\varepsilon + 1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{C \cdot C_1}
$$

  Now, for $0 \le k, \ell \le N_\varepsilon$ and fixed $\tau$, the lag argument $\tau + \ell - k$ takes on **only a finite number of values** (at most $(2N_\varepsilon + 1)$ distinct lags). Because $\{w(t)\}$ is quasi-stationary, $R_w^N(s) \to R_w(s)$ pointwise as $N \to \infty$ for each fixed lag $s$ (Eq. 2A.8), so **no uniform convergence across all lags is needed** to find an $N_\varepsilon' \ge N_\varepsilon$ such that for all $N > N_\varepsilon'$:

$$
\max_{0 \le \ell \le N_\varepsilon, \; 0 \le k \le N_\varepsilon} \big| R_w(\tau + \ell - k) - R_w^N(\tau + \ell - k) \big| < \frac{\varepsilon}{C_1^2}
$$

  Splitting Term 2 into the core box $0 \le k, \ell \le N_\varepsilon$ and the two tail strips $k > N_\varepsilon$ and $\ell > N_\varepsilon$ (where $|R_w - R_w^N| \le 2C$) bounds Term 2 for $N > N_\varepsilon'$ by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Term 2} &\le \sum_{k=0}^{N_\varepsilon} \sum_{\ell=0}^{N_\varepsilon} |g(k)||g(\ell)| \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{C_1^2} + \sum_{k=N_\varepsilon+1}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} |g(k)||g(\ell)| \cdot 2C + \sum_{k=0}^{N_\varepsilon} \sum_{\ell=N_\varepsilon+1}^{\infty} |g(k)||g(\ell)| \cdot 2C \\
&< C_1^2 \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{C_1^2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{C \cdot C_1} \cdot C_1 \cdot 2C + C_1 \cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{C \cdot C_1} \cdot 2C = \varepsilon + 2\varepsilon + 2\varepsilon = 5\varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$

  Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, Term 2 also converges to $0$ as $N \to \infty$. Thus $\lim_{N\to\infty} R_s^N(\tau) = R_s(\tau)$, proving that $\{s(t)\}$ is quasi-stationary with autocovariance (2A.4)!

##### Step 5: Fourier Transformation to Obtain $\Phi_s(\omega)$ and $\Phi_{sw}(\omega)$
Taking the discrete-time Fourier transform of $R_s(\tau)$ in (2A.4) and introducing the index substitution $s = \tau + \ell - k$ (so $\tau = s - \ell + k$ and $-i\tau\omega = -ik\omega - is\omega + i\ell\omega$) factors the triple sum into three independent single sums:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_s(\omega) &= \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \left( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} g(k) R_w(\tau + \ell - k) g^T(\ell) \right) e^{-i\tau\omega} \\
&= \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-ik\omega} \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} R_w(\tau + \ell - k) e^{-i(\tau + \ell - k)\omega} g^T(\ell) e^{i\ell\omega} \\
&= \left( \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-ik\omega} \right) \left( \sum_{s = -\infty}^{\infty} R_w(s) e^{-is\omega} \right) \left( \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} g^T(\ell) e^{i\ell\omega} \right) \\
&= G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_w(\omega) G^T(e^{-i\omega})
\end{aligned}
$$

*(Note: On page 54, Ljung's last line has a minor typographical subscript $\Phi_s(\omega)$ instead of $\Phi_w(\omega)$ on the right-hand side of $G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_w(\omega)G^T(e^{-i\omega})$; in the scalar case $G^T(e^{-i\omega}) = G(e^{-i\omega}) = \overline{G(e^{i\omega})}$, giving $\Phi_s(\omega) = |G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_w(\omega)$, which proves Eq. 2.79.)*
Similarly, for the cross-covariance $R_{sw}(\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)w^T(t-\tau)] = \sum_{k=0}^\infty g(k) R_w(\tau - k)$, substituting $s = \tau - k$ gives:

$$
\Phi_{sw}(\omega) = \sum_{\tau = -\infty}^{\infty} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-ik\omega} R_w(\tau - k) e^{-i(\tau - k)\omega} = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_w(\omega)
$$

proving (2.80). $\blacksquare$

##### Step 6: Corollary in Appendix 2A (Uniform Convergence Over a Uniformly Stable Filter Family)
In Chapter 8 (convergence analysis of parameterized model structures $G_\theta(q)$), we need the sample covariance $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t-\tau)$ to converge **uniformly** in the parameter vector $\theta \in D$.

> [!important] Corollary (Appendix 2A, Book pp. 54–55)
> Let $\{G_\theta(q), \theta \in D\}$ be a **uniformly stable family of linear filters** (meaning $g(k) \triangleq \sup_{\theta \in D} \|g_\theta(k)\|$ satisfies $\sum_{k=0}^\infty g(k) < \infty$, as defined in Section 2.2), and let $\{w(t)\}$ be a quasi-stationary deterministic sequence. Let $s_\theta(t) = G_\theta(q)w(t)$ and $R_s(\tau, \theta) = \overline{E}[s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t-\tau)]$. Then:
>
> $$
> \sup_{\theta \in D} \left\| \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} s_\theta(t) s_\theta^T(t - \tau) - R_s(\tau, \theta) \right\| \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{as } N \to \infty
> $$
>
> **Proof**: Replace every $|g(k)|$ in the bound (2A.5) by the uniform envelope $g(k) = \sup_{\theta \in D} \|g_\theta(k)\|$. Because the family $\{G_\theta(q)\}$ is uniformly stable, $\sum_{k=0}^\infty g(k) < \infty$ is convergent, which was the **only** property of $g(k)$ used to prove that the right-hand side of (2A.5) tends to zero as $N \to \infty$. Since the upper bound in (2A.5) with $g(k) = \sup_{\theta \in D} \|g_\theta(k)\|$ is independent of $\theta$, the supremum over $\theta \in D$ tends to zero as $N \to \infty$. $\blacksquare$

#### 2.3.2 Appendix 2C: Covariance Formulas for ARMA Processes (Book pp. 61–62)

In many system identification calculations (such as computing theoretical asymptotic variances or Cramér-Rao bounds), we need explicit closed-form or recursive expressions for the variance $R_s(0)$ and covariances $R_s(\tau)$, $R_{sv}(\tau)$ of signals generated by ARMA descriptions. **Appendix 2C** presents four distinct methods for computing them:

##### Method 1: Contour Integration & Residue Calculus Around the Unit Circle (Eqs. 2C.1 – 2C.2)
By the inverse Fourier transform (Eqs. 2C.1a & 2C.1b):

$$
R_s(\tau) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_s(\omega) e^{i\omega\tau} \, d\omega, \qquad R_{sv}(\tau) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_{sv}(\omega) e^{i\omega\tau} \, d\omega
$$

For an ARMA process $s(t) = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)} e(t)$ where $\{e(t)\}$ has variance $\lambda$, Theorem 2.2 gives $\Phi_s(\omega) = \lambda \left|\frac{C(e^{i\omega})}{A(e^{i\omega})}\right|^2 = \lambda \frac{C(e^{i\omega})C(e^{-i\omega})}{A(e^{i\omega})A(e^{-i\omega})}$. Changing variables to the complex unit circle $z = e^{i\omega}$ (so $e^{-i\omega} = 1/z$, $e^{i\omega\tau} = z^\tau$, and $dz = i e^{i\omega} d\omega \implies d\omega = \frac{dz}{iz}$) converts (2C.1a) into a closed contour integral counterclockwise around the unit circle $|z| = 1$ (Eq. 2C.2):

$$
R_s(\tau) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \left| \frac{C(e^{i\omega})}{A(e^{i\omega})} \right|^2 e^{i\omega\tau} \, d\omega = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi i} \oint_{|z|=1} \frac{C(z) C(1/z)}{A(z) A(1/z)} z^{\tau - 1} \, dz
$$

*(Note: Ljung's Eq. 2C.2 omits the imaginary unit $i$ in the denominator $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi i} \oint$, or absorbs $\frac{1}{i}$ into the contour measure; by Cauchy's Residue Theorem, $\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{|z|=1} f(z)\,dz$ equals the sum of the residues of the integrand at all poles strictly inside the unit circle $|z| < 1$.)*

##### Method 2: The Åström–Jury–Agniel (1970) Recursive Algorithm for $R_s(0)$ (Eq. 2C.3)
Evaluating the contour integral (2C.2) at lag $\tau = 0$ by hand for high-order polynomials $A(z)$ and $C(z)$ of degree $n$ can be tedious. Åström, Jury, and Agniel (1970) derived an efficient, division-based recursive polynomial order-reduction table (analogous to the Jury stability test) for computing $R_s(0)$ (with $\lambda = 1$; multiply by $\lambda$ in general):

Write $A(z)$ and $C(z)$ in descending powers of $z$:

$$
A(z) = a_0 z^n + a_1 z^{n-1} + \dots + a_n, \qquad C(z) = c_0 z^n + c_1 z^{n-1} + \dots + c_n
$$

Initialize the top level ($k = n$) as $a_i^n = a_i$ and $c_i^n = c_i$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n$. Then for step $k = 1, 2, \dots, n$ (reducing the polynomial degree from $n - k + 1$ down to $n - k$), compute recursively for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n - k$:

$$
a_i^{n-k} = \frac{a_0^{n-k+1} a_i^{n-k+1} - a_{n-k+1}^{n-k+1} a_{n-k+1-i}^{n-k+1}}{a_0^{n-k+1}}
$$

$$
c_i^{n-k} = \frac{a_0^{n-k+1} c_i^{n-k+1} - c_{n-k+1}^{n-k+1} a_{n-k+1-i}^{n-k+1}}{a_0^{n-k+1}}
$$

Then the zero-lag variance $R_s(0)$ (for $\lambda = 1$) is given by the sum of squares of the reduced $c_k^k$ coefficients normalized by $a_0^k$ (Eq. 2C.3):

$$
R_s(0) = \frac{1}{a_0} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(c_k^k)^2}{a_0^k}
$$

##### Explicit Closed-Form Variance for a Second-Order ARMA(2,2) Process (Eqs. 2C.4 – 2C.5)
Applying (2C.3) to the general monic second-order $\text{ARMA}(2,2)$ process with unit-variance white noise (Eq. 2C.4):

$$
y(t) + a_1 y(t - 1) + a_2 y(t - 2) = e(t) + c_1 e(t - 1) + c_2 e(t - 2), \qquad E e^2(t) = 1
$$

yields the exact closed-form variance formula (Eq. 2C.5):

$$
\operatorname{Var} y(t) = R_y(0) = \frac{(1 + a_2)\big(1 + (c_1)^2 + (c_2)^2\big) - 2a_1 c_1 (1 + c_2) - 2c_2 \big(a_2 - (a_1)^2 + (a_2)^2\big)}{(1 - a_2)(1 - a_1 + a_2)(1 + a_1 + a_2)}
$$

> [!tip] Stability Triangle Interpretation of the Denominator in (2C.5)
> Look at the three factors in the denominator of (2C.5):
>
> $$
> (1 - a_2) > 0, \qquad (1 - a_1 + a_2) > 0, \qquad (1 + a_1 + a_2) > 0
> $$
>
> By the Jury stability criterion for a 2nd-order polynomial $A(z) = z^2 + a_1 z + a_2$, the roots of $A(z) = 0$ lie strictly inside the unit circle $|z| < 1$ if and only if $|a_2| < 1$ and $1 \pm a_1 + a_2 > 0$. As the poles approach the unit circle (the boundary of the Jury stability triangle), one of the three factors $(1 - a_2)$, $(1 - a_1 + a_2)$, or $(1 + a_1 + a_2)$ approaches $0$, causing $\operatorname{Var} y(t) \to \infty$!

##### Method 3: Hand-Calculation via Cross-Multiplication and Expectation (Yule-Walker Equations)
To compute $R_y(\tau)$ and $R_{ye}(\tau)$ by hand in simple textbook examples (such as Eq. 2C.4), Ljung notes that the fastest approach is **direct time-domain cross-multiplication**:
1. Because $y(t)$ is causal ($y(t)$ depends only on $e(s)$ for $s \le t$) and $\{e(t)\}$ is white noise, **future noise is uncorrelated with past and present outputs**:

$$
R_{ye}(\tau) = E[y(t)e(t - \tau)] = 0 \quad \text{for all } \tau < 0
$$

2. Multiply the ARMA difference equation (2C.4) successively by $e(t)$, $e(t-1)$, and $e(t-2)$ and take expectations $E[\cdot]$ to obtain three linear equations that immediately solve sequentially for $R_{ye}(0) = 1$, $R_{ye}(1) = c_1 - a_1$, and $R_{ye}(2) = c_2 - a_1 R_{ye}(1) - a_2$.
3. Next, multiply (2C.4) successively by $y(t)$, $y(t-1)$, and $y(t-2)$ and take expectations $E[\cdot]$ to obtain a $3 \times 3$ linear system in the three unknown autocovariances $R_y(0)$, $R_y(1)$, and $R_y(2)$ (since $R_y(-\tau) = R_y(\tau)$).

##### Method 4: Numerical Computation in MATLAB via State-Space `dlyap`
For numerical computation on a computer, it is easiest to write the ARMA process in state-space form using the state vector:

$$
x(t) = \begin{bmatrix} y(t-1) & \dots & y(t-n) & e(t-1) & \dots & e(t-n) \end{bmatrix}^T
$$

so $x(t+1) = \mathcal{A} x(t) + \mathcal{B} e(t)$, and solve the discrete-time Lyapunov equation $\Pi = \mathcal{A} \Pi \mathcal{A}^T + \lambda \mathcal{B} \mathcal{B}^T$ using MATLAB's `dlyap` command. The resulting state covariance matrix $\Pi = E[x(t)x^T(t)]$ automatically contains all variances $R_y(0)$, lagged autocovariances $R_y(\tau)$, and cross-covariances $R_{ye}(\tau)$ of interest.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: Why $\overline{E}[v(t)u(t-\tau)] = 0$ holds automatically in Open Loop**: In Example 2.6 and the Corollary to Theorem 2.2, Ljung does not need to assume that the stochastic disturbance $v(t)$ and deterministic input $u(t)$ are statistically independent—because $u(t-\tau)$ is a **deterministic sequence**, it factors directly outside the ensemble expectation $E[v(t)u(t-\tau)] = u(t-\tau)E[v(t)] = 0$ as long as $E v(t) = 0$! Watch out: if $u(t)$ is generated via **closed-loop feedback** from $y(t)$, then $u(t)$ becomes a stochastic process correlated with $v(t)$, so $E[v(t)u(t-\tau)] \ne 0$ and $\Phi_{yu}(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_u(\omega) + \Phi_{vu}(\omega)$.
> - **Leap 2: Weak Convergence ($\underset{w}{\to}$) vs. Pointwise Convergence of the Periodogram**: A single realization's periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ does **not** converge pointwise to $\Phi_s(\omega)$ as $N \to \infty$ (compare the jagged periodogram in Figure 2.8 with the smooth theoretical spectrum in Figure 2.7). Even the *expected* periodogram $E|S_N(\omega)|^2$ only converges **weakly** in general (Lemma 2.1, Eq. 2.75: when integrated against a smooth window $\Psi(\omega)$ with $\sum |a_\tau| < \infty$) because deterministic periodic signals produce Dirac delta spikes in $\Phi_s(\omega)$.
> - **Leap 3: Uniqueness Conditions in Spectral Factorization**: Any lag-symmetric rational spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) > 0$ can be factored as $\lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$ in up to $2^{n_a + n_c}$ ways by flipping poles or zeros across the unit circle ($z_0 \leftrightarrow 1/z_0$). To make the canonical representation $v(t) = R(q)e(t)$ **unique**, Spectral Factorization enforces three simultaneous conditions on $R(z)$:
>   1. **Monic** ($R(\infty) = 1$, fixing the scaling between $R(z)$ and $\lambda$),
>   2. **No poles on or outside the unit circle** ($R(q)$ is causally stable), and
>   3. **No zeros on or outside the unit circle** ($R(q)$ is minimum-phase, so the whitening filter $R^{-1}(q)$ is also causally stable).
> - **Notation Trap 1: "Covariance Function" $R_s(\tau)$**: Remember Ljung's explicit warning on p. 34: calling $R_s(\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)]$ the "covariance function" is an intentional abuse of terminology when $s(t)$ has a non-zero mean or is deterministic (since the mean is not subtracted in Definition 2.1).
> - **Notation Trap 2: Missing $i$ in Contour Integral (2C.2) and Subscript Typo on p. 54**:
>   - On p. 54 (Appendix 2A), the last line of the display equation for $\Phi_s(\omega)$ reads $= G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_s(\omega)G^T(e^{-i\omega})$; the middle factor on the right-hand side is a typo for $\Phi_w(\omega)$.
>   - On p. 61 (Appendix 2C, Eq. 2C.2), changing variables $z = e^{i\omega}$ gives $d\omega = \frac{dz}{iz}$, so the standard complex contour integral prefactor is $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi i} \oint_{|z|=1}$ (Ljung prints $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi} \oint$).
