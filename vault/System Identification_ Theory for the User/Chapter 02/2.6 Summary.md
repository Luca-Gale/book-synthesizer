# 2.6 Summary

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 2**: Section 2.6 consolidates the canonical discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system representation, its frequency-domain characterization, and the unified quasi-stationary spectral framework established across Sections 2.1–2.5. Together, these equations form the non-parametric mathematical foundation upon which Chapter 3 (Simulation and Prediction) and Chapter 4 (Parametric Model Structures) are built.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Chapter 2 constructs a single, unified mathematical architecture capable of describing both deterministic control inputs and stochastic random disturbances acting on a discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) system:

1. **Time-Domain Impulse Response Architecture**: Any causal LTI system subject to additive random disturbances is completely specified by its deterministic impulse response sequence $\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ (packaged into the transfer operator $G(q)$) and its monic noise impulse response sequence $\{h(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ (packaged into the noise filter $H(q)$), driven by normalized white noise $\{e(t)\}$ with variance $\lambda$ (or covariance matrix $\Lambda$ in the multivariable case).
2. **Bridge to Parametric Models (Forward Pointer to Chapter 4)**: In engineering practice, linear systems are rarely specified by listing infinitely many impulse response coefficients $\{g(k)\}$ and $\{h(k)\}$. Instead, practitioners use **state-space models** and **finite-order difference equations** (such as ARX, ARMAX, Output-Error, and Box-Jenkins models). As Ljung highlights, all such parametric structures are simply finite-dimensional parameterizations of the infinite impulse response sequences $\{g(k)\}$ and $\{h(k)\}$, which will be treated systematically in Chapter 4.
3. **Frequency-Domain & Spectral Unification**: By evaluating the shift-operator transfer functions on the unit circle ($q = e^{i\omega}$) and introducing the **quasi-stationary** expectation operator $\overline{E}$, deterministic sequences and stochastic processes can be analyzed side-by-side. The finite-interval Fourier transform $U_N(\omega)$ bridges finite experimental datasets to asymptotic auto-covariance functions $R_s(\tau)$ and power spectra $\Phi_s(\omega)$, culminating in the fundamental spectral input-output decomposition for open-loop systems.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 1. Canonical Time-Domain System Description & Stability

Section 2.6 opens by restating the canonical representation of a discrete-time linear time-invariant system subject to additive random disturbances (**Equation 2.97**):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t) \tag{2.97}
$$

where the transfer operators $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ are defined via the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$ as causal infinite series:

$$
G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)q^{-k}
$$

$$
H(q) = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)q^{-k}
$$

- **Stochastic Source Assumptions**: The driving noise $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random variables with zero mean values ($E e(t) = 0$) and variances $\lambda$ (or, in the multi-output case where $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$, covariance matrices $\Lambda = E e(t)e^T(t)$, with the leading monic term $1$ in $H(q)$ replaced by the $p \times p$ identity matrix $I$).
- **Strict One-Sample Input Delay Convention**: Notice that the summation for $G(q)$ begins at $k = 1$ ($g(0) = 0$), encoding a convention of at least a one-sample delay from input $u(t)$ to output $y(t)$, whereas $H(q)$ is normalized to be **monic** ($h(0) = 1$ or $I$), so that the instantaneous innovation $e(t)$ enters $y(t)$ with unit gain at lag $0$.
- **BIBO Stability Condition**: The linear filter $G(q)$ is bounded-input bounded-output (BIBO) **stable** if its impulse response is absolutely summable ($\ell_1$-stability):

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty
$$

Under this condition, any bounded input $|u(t)| \le C_u$ produces a bounded deterministic output response, and the Laurent series $G(z) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty g(k)z^{-k}$ converges uniformly on the unit circle $z = e^{i\omega}$. (Analogously, stability and invertibility of $H(q)$ require $\sum_{k=1}^\infty |h(k)| < \infty$ and stability of $H^{-1}(q)$, which underpins predictor synthesis in Chapter 3.)

---

#### 2. Frequency-Domain Representation & Finite-Interval Fourier Transform

Evaluating $G(q)$ at $q = e^{i\omega}$ yields the **frequency function** $G(e^{i\omega})$, which characterizes the steady-state amplitude scaling $|G(e^{i\omega})|$ and phase shift $\arg G(e^{i\omega})$ imparted by the system to a pure input sinusoid of angular frequency $\omega$.

To analyze the frequency content of observed signals over a finite experimental record $t = 1, 2, \dots, N$, the **finite-interval Fourier transform** is defined (**Equation 2.98**):

$$
U_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} u(t)e^{-i\omega t} \tag{2.98}
$$

- **Role of the $1/\sqrt{N}$ Normalization**: Scaling by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$ ensures that the periodogram $|U_N(\omega)|^2$ has the same scaling as the average signal power $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N |u(t)|^2$ via Parseval's relation, and converges in expectation (for quasi-stationary signals) to the true power spectrum $\Phi_u(\omega)$ as $N \to \infty$.

---

#### 3. Quasi-Stationary Signals, Covariance Functions & Spectral Transformation

To treat deterministic sequences (such as periodic inputs or step signals) and stochastic processes (such as filtered white noise) within a single second-order calculus, Ljung defines the **generalized expectation operator** $\overline{E}$ acting on any time-indexed function $f(t)$:

$$
\overline{E} f(t) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} E f(t)
$$

where $E$ drops out whenever $f(t)$ is purely deterministic. Using $\overline{E}$, a signal $s(t)$ is defined to be **quasi-stationary** if its bounded first-order moments exist and the following limit exists for every integer lag $\tau \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$
\overline{E} s(t)s(t-\tau) = R_s(\tau)
$$

Assuming absolute summability of the autocovariance sequence ($\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty |R_s(\tau)| < \infty$), the **spectrum** (power spectral density) of a quasi-stationary signal $s(t)$ is defined as the discrete-time Fourier transform of $R_s(\tau)$ (**Equation 2.99**):

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau)e^{-i\omega\tau} \tag{2.99}
$$

Finally, when the output $y(t)$ is generated by the canonical system (2.97) under the key structural assumption that the input $\{u(t)\}$ and the white-noise disturbance $\{e(t)\}$ are **independent** (more generally, uncorrelated across all lags so that $\Phi_{ue}(\omega) \equiv 0$, corresponding to open-loop operation without output feedback into $u(t)$), the output spectrum decomposes additively into its input-driven and noise-driven components:

$$
\Phi_y(\omega) = \left| G(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \lambda \left| H(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2
$$

*(In the multivariable case from Eq. 2.96, this generalizes to $\Phi_y(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_u(\omega)G^T(e^{-i\omega}) + H(e^{i\omega})\Lambda H^T(e^{-i\omega})$).*

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Conceptual Leap — Infinite Impulse Responses vs. Finite Parametric Models**: Chapter 2 treats $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ as non-parametric infinite series determined by sequences $\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ and $\{h(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$. Keep in mind Ljung's forward pointer on page 46: familiar state-space models and linear difference equations are not alternative system classes—they are simply finite-dimensional parameterizations of $\{g(k)\}$ and $\{h(k)\}$ (which will be parameterized by a vector $\theta$ as $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ in Chapter 4).
> - **Crucial Independence Trap in the Output Spectrum Formula**: The clean additive decomposition $\Phi_y(\omega) = |G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$ holds **if and only if** $\{u(t)\}$ and $\{e(t)\}$ are uncorrelated ($\Phi_{ue}(\omega) = 0$). Whenever data is collected under **closed-loop feedback control**, $u(t)$ reacts to past outputs $y(t-k)$ and therefore correlates with past innovations $e(t-k)$, introducing the cross-spectral term $2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{ue}(\omega)H(e^{-i\omega})\right)$ derived at the top of page 45 (Eq. 2.95).
> - **Notation Trap — Operator Argument $G(q)$ vs. Complex Function $G(e^{i\omega})$**: Ljung uses the same letter $G$ for both the time-domain shift operator $G(q)$ acting on sequences $u(t)$ and the complex-valued frequency function $G(e^{i\omega})$ evaluated at $z = e^{i\omega}$. Always check whether the argument is $q$ (time-domain filtering) or $e^{i\omega}$ (frequency-domain multiplication/spectrum shaping).
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *Given known filters $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ in Eq. (2.97), how can we invert the monic noise model $H(q)$ to compute the optimal one-step-ahead prediction $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ of future outputs from past inputs and outputs?* (This is the central question of Chapter 3 immediately following.)
>   2. *Why is $H(q)$ required to start with $1 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty h(k)q^{-k}$ while $G(q)$ starts at $k=1$?* (Ensuring $H(q)$ is monic removes scaling ambiguity between $H(q)$ and the innovation variance $\lambda$, and makes $H^{-1}(q)$ causal and monic for optimal prediction.)
