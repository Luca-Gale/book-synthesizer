# 2.4 Single Realization Behavior and Ergodicity Results

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 2**: This section bridges the gap between theoretical ensemble probability ($E$, taken over an imaginary universe of experiments) and physical reality (where an engineer observes only a single time-series realization of length $N$). By establishing **ergodicity** (Theorem 2.3 and its uniform multivariable generalization in Appendix 2B), Ljung proves that when stochastic disturbances are modeled as uniformly stable linear filters driven by independent white noise (or martingale differences) with bounded fourth moments, sample time averages of a single realization converge **with probability 1 (almost surely)** as $N \to \infty$ to the theoretical quasi-stationary limits—meaning the empirical spectrum of one observed dataset coincides almost surely with the theoretical ensemble spectrum.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

### 1.1 The "Single Realization" Dilemma
In Section 2.3, the quasi-stationary framework defined the autocovariance $R_s(\tau) = \overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)]$ and the power spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ using the combined operator $\overline{E}[\cdot] \triangleq \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N E[\cdot]$, which takes an **ensemble expectation** $E[\cdot]$ across all possible realizations before averaging over time.

Yet as Ljung candidly notes, all the definitions of Section 2.3—spectra, linear system frequency transformations (Theorem 2.2), and periodogram relationships (Lemma 2.1)—remain algebraically identical if we treat $\{s(t)\}$ as a **single deterministic sequence**, drop the expectation operator $E$, and interpret $\overline{E}[f(t)]$ purely as the deterministic time average:

$$
\overline{E}[f(t)] \equiv \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N f(t)
$$

This raises a natural engineering question: **In practice, we only ever record one finite dataset $\{s(1), s(2), \dots, s(N)\}$ from our plant. Why bother embedding this single observed realization into a hypothetical stochastic process and taking averages over an ensemble of potential observations we will never see?**

Ljung gives two pragmatic answers:
1. **Analytical Tractability**: The probabilistic framework makes many theoretical calculations vastly simpler (e.g., taking expectations of uncorrelated white noise cross-terms immediately zeroes them out, whereas finite or infinite deterministic time sums require delicate limiting arguments).
2. **Repeatability & Variance Quantification**: Only an ensemble framework allows us to ask and answer the question: *"What would happen if the identification experiment were repeated tomorrow under different noise realizations?"* Without probability, we cannot derive confidence intervals or parameter covariance matrices.

### 1.2 Why Ergodicity is the Linchpin of System Identification
Once we adopt a probabilistic ensemble to design and analyze our models, we face a critical consistency question: **Does the deterministic spectrum computed from the single realization $\{s(t)\}$ we actually observe in the lab match the theoretical ensemble spectrum defined via $E[\cdot]$?**

This is the fundamental question of **ergodic theory** (time averages vs. ensemble averages).
- If a process is **not ergodic** (for example, $s(t) = A$ where $A \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ is drawn once at $t = -\infty$ and held constant forever), the ensemble variance is $E[s^2(t)] = 1$, but any single experiment sees a constant DC offset $a$ whose sample second moment is $a^2 \neq 1$. Time averaging never washes out the randomness because the memory of the initial draw never decays.
- If the stochastic component $v(t) = s(t) - m(t)$ is generated by passing independent innovations $e(t)$ through a **uniformly stable linear filter** $H_t(q)$ (whose impulse response $h_t(k)$ decays fast enough that distant past shocks are forgotten), then different segments of a long realization become asymptotically independent. Time averaging over $t = 1, \dots, N$ effectively averages over many nearly independent "sub-experiments" embedded sequentially in time!

Consequently, Theorem 2.3 guarantees that **with probability 1** (for almost every realization you could ever observe in nature), the sample time-average correlations converge directly to the theoretical ensemble-averaged correlations $R_s(\tau)$. For all second-order properties (covariances and spectra), **the distinction between a deterministic signal and a single realization of a stochastic process disappears**.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Deterministic vs. Probabilistic Interpretation of $\overline{E}[f(t)]$
Recall from Section 2.3 that a stochastic process $\{s(t)\}$ is **quasi-stationary** if:
1. $E[s(t)] = m(t)$, where $|m(t)| \le C_m, \forall t$.
2. $E[s(t)s(r)] = R_s(t, r)$, where $|R_s(t, r)| \le C_R, \forall t, r$, and the limit

$$
\overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)] \triangleq \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N E[s(t)s(t-\tau)] = R_s(\tau)
$$

exists for every lag $\tau \in \mathbb{Z}$.

If instead $\{s(t)\}$ is a **single given deterministic sequence** (or a single observed trajectory of a random process treated as a fixed sequence of numbers), we can drop the ensemble expectation $E$ and define the operator $\overline{E}$ on any function $f(t)$ purely as the infinite time average:

$$
\overline{E}[f(t)] \triangleq \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N f(t)
$$

Under this deterministic reading, all results of Section 2.3—the definition of the power spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=-\infty}^\infty R_s(\tau)e^{-i\omega\tau}$, the spectral transformation formula $\Phi_y(\omega) = |G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_u(\omega)$ (Theorem 2.2), and the asymptotic relationship with the periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ (Lemma 2.1)—hold unchanged provided the purely deterministic time-average limits $\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N s(t)s(t-\tau)$ exist. The central task of Section 2.4 is to prove that for a very broad class of stochastic processes, those single-realization time limits **do** exist with probability 1 and equal the ensemble-averaged limits $R_s(\tau)$.

#### 2.2.2 Theorem 2.3: Main Ergodicity & Single-Realization Convergence Results
To guarantee that time averages along a single realization converge to ensemble averages, we must impose a structural assumption on how the stochastic component $v(t) = s(t) - m(t)$ is generated. Specifically, $v(t)$ must be representable as the output of a uniformly stable linear filter driven by independent random variables with bounded fourth moments.

> [!important] Theorem 2.3 (Ergodicity of Quasi-Stationary Filtered White Noise)
> Let $\{s(t)\}$ be a quasi-stationary signal with deterministic mean sequence $E[s(t)] = m(t)$. Assume that the zero-mean stochastic perturbation $v(t) = s(t) - m(t)$ admits the representation:
>
> $$
> s(t) - m(t) = v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty h_t(k)e(t-k) = H_t(q)e(t) \tag{2.88}
> $$
>
> subject to the following **four core assumptions**:
> 1. **Independence & Zero Mean**: $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random variables with $E[e(t)] = 0$.
> 2. **Time-Varying Second Moments**: $E[e^2(t)] = \lambda_t$.
> 3. **Uniformly Bounded Fourth Moments**: There exists a finite constant $C_e < \infty$ such that $E|e(t)|^4 \le C_e$ for all $t$ (which automatically implies $\lambda_t \le \sqrt{C_e} < \infty$).
> 4. **Uniformly Stable Family of Filters**: $\{H_t(q), t = 1, 2, \dots\}$ is a uniformly stable family of filters, meaning there exists an absolute constant $C_H < \infty$ and envelope sequence $\bar{h}(k) = \sup_t |h_t(k)|$ such that:
>
> $$
> \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sup_t |h_t(k)| = C_H < \infty
> $$
>
> *(plus boundedness $|m(t)| \le C_m$ from quasi-stationarity of $s(t)$).*
> 
> **Conclusion**: With probability 1 (almost surely) as $N \to \infty$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N s(t)s(t-\tau) \to \overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)] = R_s(\tau) \tag{2.89a}
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \big[ s(t)m(t-\tau) - E[s(t)m(t-\tau)] \big] \to 0 \tag{2.89b}
> $$
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \big[ s(t)v(t-\tau) - E[s(t)v(t-\tau)] \big] \to 0 \tag{2.89c}
> $$
>

##### Detailed Breakdown of Equations (2.89a)–(2.89c)
Let us inspect what each of the three limits in (2.89) actually states by substituting $s(t) = m(t) + v(t)$:
- **Look at (2.89b)**: Since $m(t-\tau)$ is deterministic and $E[s(t)] = m(t)$, we have $E[s(t)m(t-\tau)] = m(t)m(t-\tau)$, and thus:

$$
s(t)m(t-\tau) - E[s(t)m(t-\tau)] = \big(m(t) + v(t)\big)m(t-\tau) - m(t)m(t-\tau) = v(t)m(t-\tau)
$$

Therefore, (2.89b) states that $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N v(t)m(t-\tau) \to 0$ with probability 1. In physical terms: **a zero-mean filtered white noise process $\{v(t)\}$ is asymptotically uncorrelated in time average with any bounded deterministic signal $\{m(t)\}$**. Notice that choosing $m(t) \equiv 1$ recovers the Strong Law of Large Numbers for the sample mean: $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N v(t) \to 0$ w.p. 1!
- **Look at (2.89c)**: Since $s(t) = m(t) + v(t)$ and $E[m(t)v(t-\tau)] = 0$, the summand in (2.89c) expands as:

$$
s(t)v(t-\tau) - E[s(t)v(t-\tau)] = m(t)v(t-\tau) + \big[ v(t)v(t-\tau) - E[v(t)v(t-\tau)] \big]
$$

Since $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N m(t)v(t-\tau) \to 0$ by the same mechanism as (2.89b), equation (2.89c) guarantees that the sample autocovariance of the purely stochastic part converges to its ensemble expectation:

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \big[ v(t)v(t-\tau) - E[v(t)v(t-\tau)] \big] \to 0 \quad \text{w.p. 1}
$$

- **Look at (2.89a)**: Combining (2.89b) and (2.89c) via the identity $s(t)s(t-\tau) = s(t)m(t-\tau) + s(t)v(t-\tau)$ yields:

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \big[ s(t)s(t-\tau) - E[s(t)s(t-\tau)] \big] \to 0 \quad \text{w.p. 1}
$$

Because $\{s(t)\}$ is assumed quasi-stationary, the ensemble time-average $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N E[s(t)s(t-\tau)]$ converges as $N \to \infty$ to the limit $\overline{E}[s(t)s(t-\tau)] = R_s(\tau)$. Adding these two facts together yields (2.89a): the sample autocorrelation $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N s(t)s(t-\tau)$ converges with probability 1 directly to $R_s(\tau)$.

#### 2.2.3 Physical & Spectral Consequences of Theorem 2.3
Ljung highlights three major engineering consequences of Theorem 2.3 on page 43:

1. **Coincidence of Single-Realization and Ensemble Spectra**:
   Provided the stochastic part of $\{s(t)\}$ can be described as filtered white noise as in (2.88), **the spectrum of an observed single realization of $\{s(t)\}$, computed as if it were a deterministic signal, coincides with probability 1 with the theoretical spectrum of the stochastic process $\{s(t)\}$ defined by ensemble averages ($E$) in (2.61).**
2. **Equivalence of Deterministic and Stochastic Signals for Second-Order Properties**:
   When we only care about second-order properties (autocovariances $R_s(\tau)$ and power spectra $\Phi_s(\omega)$), the distinction between deterministic and stochastic signals is de-emphasized. For instance, any deterministic signal $\{s(t)\}$ whose deterministic spectrum happens to be flat, $\Phi_s(\omega) \equiv \lambda$ (such as a pseudorandom binary sequence, PRBS, over a long period), may—for all calculations involving second-order properties—be regarded as a realization of white noise with variance $\lambda$.
3. **Link Between Theoretical Spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$ and the Observed Periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$**:
   How does our "theoretical" spectrum $\Phi_s(\omega)$—defined in (2.63) using the physically unrealizable concepts of an ensemble expectation $E$ and an infinite horizon limit $\lim_{N\to\infty}$—relate to the finite-sample periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{N} \left|\sum_{t=1}^N s(t)e^{-i\omega t}\right|^2$ defined in (2.43) from real data?
   - Recall from Section 2.1 (Figures 2.7 and 2.8) that the raw periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ of a noisy signal fluctuates wildly with $\omega$ and does **not** converge pointwise to $\Phi_s(\omega)$ as $N \to \infty$.
   - However, combining **Theorem 2.3** (which says sample covariances $\hat{R}_s^N(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\sum s(t)s(t-\tau)$ converge w.p. 1 to $R_s(\tau)$) with **Lemma 2.1** (which relates weighted/smoothed integrals of the periodogram to sample covariances) proves that **"smoothed" versions of $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ will look like $\Phi_s(\omega)$ for large $N$** (analyzed in full detail in Section 6.3).

#### 2.2.4 Complete Walkthrough of Appendix 2B: Proof of Theorem 2.3 (and Uniform Multivariable Generalization)

In Appendix 2B (pages 55–60), Ljung proves a significantly stronger, multivariable, **uniformly parameterized** version of Theorem 2.3—**Theorem 2B.1**. Why prove a uniform bound over a parameter set $\theta \in D_\theta$ right now? Because in **Chapter 8 (Convergence Analysis of Prediction Error Methods)**, the predictor filters $G_\theta(q)$ and $M_\theta(q)$ depend on an unknown model parameter vector $\theta \in D_\mathcal{M}$. To prove that the parameter estimate $\hat{\theta}_N$ minimizing the sample prediction error cost converges w.p. 1 to the theoretical minimum, pointwise convergence for each fixed $\theta$ is not enough—we need **uniform convergence** ($\sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \|\cdot\| \to 0$ w.p. 1).

Let us walk through the entire 6-step architecture of Appendix 2B without skipping a single algebraic step.

---

##### Step 1: Statement of Theorem 2B.1 & Reduction to Theorem 2.3
> [!important] Theorem 2B.1 (Uniform Multivariable Ergodicity)
> Let $\{G_\theta(q), \theta \in D_\theta\}$ and $\{M_\theta(q), \theta \in D_\theta\}$ be **uniformly stable families of matrix filters**, meaning their impulse responses $g_\theta(k)$ and $m_\theta(k)$ satisfy:
>
> $$
> \sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \|g_\theta(k)\| \le \bar{g}(k), \quad \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bar{g}(k) = C_G < \infty
> $$
>
> $$
> \sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \|m_\theta(k)\| \le \bar{m}(k), \quad \sum_{k=0}^\infty \bar{m}(k) = C_M < \infty
> $$
>
> *(Note: the textbook prints $\sum_{k=1}^\infty$ on p. 59 as a minor typo for $\sum_{k=0}^\infty$, since the sums in (2B.13)–(2B.14) start at $k=0$).*
> Assume that the deterministic vector signal $\{w(t)\}, t = 1, 2, \dots$ is uniformly bounded:
>
> $$
> |w(t)| \le C_w, \quad \forall t \tag{2B.1}
> $$
>
> For each $\theta \in D_\theta$, define the vector signal $s_\theta(t)$ by:
>
> $$
> s_\theta(t) = G_\theta(q)v(t) + M_\theta(q)w(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty g_\theta(k)v(t-k) + \sum_{k=0}^\infty m_\theta(k)w(t-k) \tag{2B.2}
> $$
>
> where $\{v(t)\}$ obeys the conditions of Theorem 2.3: $v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty h_t(k)e(t-k)$ with $\{e(t)\}$ independent, zero-mean, covariance matrix $E[e(t)e^T(t)] = \Lambda_t$, bounded fourth moment $\sup_t E|e(t)|^4 = C_e < \infty$, and $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \sup_t \|h_t(k)\| = C_H < \infty$.
> 
> **Conclusion**:
>
> $$
> \sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \left\| \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \big[ s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) - E[s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t)] \big] \right\| \to 0 \quad \text{w.p. 1, as } N \to \infty \tag{2B.3}
> $$
>

**How (2B.3) implies Theorem 2.3 (Remark, p. 55)**:
Fix any lag $\tau \ge 0$ and take $D_\theta = \{\theta^*\}$ (a singleton set). Set $w(t) = m(t)$ (which is bounded since $s(t)$ is quasi-stationary). Construct the $6 \times 1$ (or at lag $\tau = 0$, $3 \times 1$) stacked vector $s_\theta(t)$ using simple delay operators $q^{-\tau}$ in $G_{\theta^*}(q)$ and $M_{\theta^*}(q)$. Specifically, at $\tau = 0$, Ljung writes:

$$
s_\theta(t) = \begin{bmatrix} s(t) \\ m(t) \\ v(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} v(t) + \begin{bmatrix} w(t) \\ w(t) \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} v(t) + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} w(t) \tag{2B.4}
$$

(and more generally, appending $s(t-\tau), m(t-\tau), v(t-\tau)$ using the stable delay filter $q^{-\tau}$). Then the outer product matrix $s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t)$ contains all the cross-products $s(t)s(t-\tau)$, $s(t)m(t-\tau)$, and $s(t)v(t-\tau)$ as its individual scalar entries! Thus, convergence of the matrix norm in (2B.3) to $0$ w.p. 1 immediately implies (2.89a), (2.89b), and (2.89c).

##### Step 2: Lemma 2B.1 — Fourth-Moment Bound & The 4-Index Coincidence Counting Argument
Why does Theorem 2.3 require a bounded **fourth** moment $E|e(t)|^4 \le C_e$ when we are only estimating **second** moments $E[v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell)]$? Because to prove that the sample second moment converges to its mean, we must bound the **variance of the sample second moment**—and the variance of a quadratic form in $e(t)$ is a **fourth-order** moment!

> [!important] Lemma 2B.1 (Mean-Square Growth of Partial Covariance Sums)
> Let $\{v(t)\}$ obey the conditions of Theorem 2.3 and define the constants:
>
> $$
> C_H = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sup_t \|h_t(k)\|, \qquad C_e = \sup_t E|e(t)|^4, \qquad C_w = \sup_t |w(t)|
> $$
>
> *(Note: Ljung prints $k=1$ in $C_H$ and $C_l$ for $C_e$ on p. 56; the proof explicitly sums from $k=0$ and writes $C_e$).*
> Then, for all integers $r \le N$ and all lags $k, \ell \ge 0$:
>
> $$
> E \left\| \sum_{t=r}^N \big[ v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell) - E v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell) \big] \right\|^2 \le 4 \cdot C_e \cdot C_H^4 \cdot (N - r) \tag{2B.5}
> $$
>
> $$
> E \left\| \sum_{t=r}^N v(t-k)w^T(t-\ell) \right\|^2 \le 4 \cdot C_e \cdot C_H^2 \cdot C_w^2 \cdot (N - r) \tag{2B.6}
> $$
>
> *(Strictly speaking, a sum from $t=r$ to $N$ has $N - r + 1$ terms; Ljung writes $(N-r)$ as shorthand throughout Appendix 2B since in all subsequent steps $r=1$ gives $N-1 \le N$ or $r = N^2+1$ gives $k - N^2$ terms).*

###### Step-by-Step Proof of Lemma 2B.1:
1. **Shift Reduction ($k = \ell = 0$)**: Without loss of generality, we may take $k = \ell = 0$ (for general $k, \ell$, we simply absorb the shifts into the filter indexing or apply Corollary to Lemma 2B.1 below). Define the partial sum matrix $S_r^N$:

$$
S_r^N \triangleq \sum_{t=r}^N \big[ v(t)v^T(t) - E v(t)v^T(t) \big]
$$

2. **Expand $v(t)$ in Terms of the Innovations $e(t-k)$**:
   Since $v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty h_t(k)e(t-k)$, the outer product is:

$$
v(t)v^T(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty h_t(k) e(t-k) e^T(t-\ell) h_t^T(\ell)
$$

   Taking the expectation uses the independence and zero-mean property of $\{e(t)\}$: $E[e(t-k)e^T(t-\ell)] = \Lambda_{t-\ell}\delta_{k\ell}$, where $\delta_{k\ell}$ is the Kronecker delta. Subtracting the expectation gives:

$$
S_r^N = \sum_{t=r}^N \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty h_t(k) \alpha(t, k, \ell) h_t^T(\ell) \tag{2B.7}
$$

   where the zero-mean innovation product matrix $\alpha(t, k, \ell)$ is defined as:

$$
\alpha(t, k, \ell) \triangleq e(t-k)e^T(t-\ell) - \Lambda_{t-\ell}\delta_{k\ell} \tag{2B.8}
$$

3. **Square the $(i, j)$ Entry of $S_r^N$**:
   To bound the expected squared norm $E\|S_r^N\|^2$, we examine the square of the $(i, j)$ scalar entry, $(S_r^N(i, j))^2$. Writing out the product of two copies of the triple sum (2B.7)—one with dummy indices $(t, k_1, \ell_1)$ and the other with $(s, k_2, \ell_2)$—yields a **6-fold sum**:

$$
\big(S_r^N(i, j)\big)^2 = \sum_{t=r}^N \sum_{s=r}^N \sum_{k_1=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell_1=0}^\infty \sum_{k_2=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell_2=0}^\infty \gamma(t, s, k_1, k_2, \ell_1, \ell_2)
$$

   where the scalar summand $\gamma$ is:

$$
\gamma(t, s, k_1, k_2, \ell_1, \ell_2) = h_t^{(i)}(k_1) \alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1) \big[h_t^{(j)}(\ell_1)\big]^T h_s^{(i)}(k_2) \alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2) \big[h_s^{(j)}(\ell_2)\big]^T
$$

   and superscript $(i)$ denotes the $i$-th row of the matrix $h_t(\cdot)$.

4. **The Crucial 4-Index Coincidence Argument**:
   Take the expectation $E[\gamma(t, s, k_1, k_2, \ell_1, \ell_2)]$. The random part of $\gamma$ is the product of $\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1)$ (which depends ONLY on the random vectors $e(t-k_1)$ and $e(t-\ell_1)$) and $\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)$ (which depends ONLY on $e(s-k_2)$ and $e(s-\ell_2)$).
   - Notice that by construction in (2B.8), **both $\alpha$ factors have zero mean**: $E[\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1)] = 0$ and $E[\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)] = 0$.
   - Because $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of **independent** random variables, if the set of time indices $\{t - k_1, t - \ell_1\}$ is disjoint from the set of time indices $\{s - k_2, s - \ell_2\}$, then $\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1)$ and $\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)$ are **statistically independent**!
   - By independence, the expectation of their product factors into the product of their expectations:

$$
E\big[\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1) \alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)\big] = E\big[\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1)\big] \cdot E\big[\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)\big] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0
$$

   - Therefore, **$E[\gamma(t, s, k_1, k_2, \ell_1, \ell_2)] = 0$ UNLESS at least one time index in $\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1)$ coincides with a time index in $\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)$**, i.e., unless at least one of the following four linear equations holds:

$$
t - k_1 = s - k_2 \quad \text{or} \quad t - k_1 = s - \ell_2 \quad \text{or} \quad t - \ell_1 = s - k_2 \quad \text{or} \quad t - \ell_1 = s - \ell_2
$$

5. **Collapse of the Double Time Sum $\sum_t \sum_s$ to a Single Time Sum**:
   Suppose we fix the five indices $t, k_1, k_2, \ell_1, \ell_2$. For how many values of $s \in \{r, \dots, N\}$ can the four coincidence equations above be satisfied? **At most four values of $s$!** Specifically:
   - $s = t - k_1 + k_2$
   - $s = t - k_1 + \ell_2$
   - $s = t - \ell_1 + k_2$
   - $s = t - \ell_1 + \ell_2$

   For each of those at most $4$ values of $s$, Cauchy-Schwarz and the fourth-moment bound $E|e(t)|^4 \le C_e$ bound the expectation of $\gamma$ by:

$$
|E\gamma(t, s, k_1, k_2, \ell_1, \ell_2)| \le C_e \cdot |h(k_1)| \cdot |h(k_2)| \cdot |h(\ell_1)| \cdot |h(\ell_2)|
$$

   *(where $|h(k)| \triangleq \sup_t \|h_t(k)\|$).*
   Summing over $s$ therefore replaces the entire sum $\sum_{s=r}^N$ (which had $N - r$ terms) by **at most 4 non-zero terms**, eliminating one factor of $(N - r)$! Summing over the remaining five indices yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big(S_r^N(i, j)\big)^2 &\le \sum_{k_1=0}^\infty |h(k_1)| \cdot \sum_{k_2=0}^\infty |h(k_2)| \cdot \sum_{\ell_1=0}^\infty |h(\ell_1)| \cdot \sum_{\ell_2=0}^\infty |h(\ell_2)| \cdot \sum_{t=r}^N 4 C_e \\
&\le C_H \cdot C_H \cdot C_H \cdot C_H \cdot 4 C_e (N - r) = 4 \cdot C_e \cdot C_H^4 \cdot (N - r)
\end{aligned}
$$

   which proves (2B.5).
   - **Proof of (2B.6)**: Even simpler! In $\sum_{t=r}^N v(t-k)w^T(t-\ell) = \sum_{t=r}^N \sum_{m=0}^\infty h_t(m)e(t-k-m)w^T(t-\ell)$, squaring produces $E[e(t-k-m_1)e^T(s-k-m_2)]$, which is zero unless $t - k - m_1 = s - k - m_2 \implies s = t - m_1 + m_2$ (only $1 \le 4$ value of $s$, using $E|e(t)|^2 \le \sqrt{C_e}$ or $C_e$), giving the bound $4 C_e C_H^2 C_w^2 (N - r)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!tip] Corollary to Lemma 2B.1 (Two Different Filtered White Noise Signals)
> If $w(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \alpha_t(k)e(t-k)$ and $v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \beta_t(k)e(t-k)$ with $C_w = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sup_t |\alpha_t(k)| < \infty$ and $C_v = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sup_t |\beta_t(k)| < \infty$, the exact same 4-index coincidence proof yields:
>
> $$
> E \left\| \sum_{t=r}^N \big[ w(t)v(t) - E w(t)v(t) \big] \right\|^2 \le C \cdot C_w^2 \cdot C_v^2 \cdot (N - r)
> $$
>

##### Step 3: Lemma 2B.2 — Lifting the Bound to the Uniform Supremum over $\theta \in D_\theta$
Next, Ljung shows that the linear growth rate $O(N - r)$ of the second moment in Lemma 2B.1 is preserved even when we take the **supremum over all $\theta \in D_\theta$** inside the expectation!

> [!important] Lemma 2B.2 (Expected Squared Supremum Bound)
> Define the supremum of the partial centered covariance sum over $\theta \in D_\theta$:
>
> $$
> R_r^N \triangleq \sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \left\| \sum_{t=r}^N \big[ s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) - E s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) \big] \right\| \tag{2B.9}
> $$
>
> Then there exists a finite constant $C < \infty$ (independent of $N$ and $r$) such that:
>
> $$
> E\big[(R_r^N)^2\big] \le C(N - r) \tag{2B.10}
> $$
>

###### Step-by-Step Proof of Lemma 2B.2:
1. **Preliminary Cauchy-Schwarz Fact for Infinite Random Series ((2B.11)–(2B.12))**:
   Suppose a random vector $\varphi$ is given by the infinite series:

$$
\varphi = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a(k)z(k) \tag{2B.11}
$$

   where $\{a(k)\}$ is a sequence of deterministic matrices satisfying $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \|a(k)\| \le C_a < \infty$, and $\{z(k)\}$ is a sequence of random vectors with uniformly bounded second moments $E|z(k)|^2 \le C_z < \infty$ for all $k$. Then we can bound $E|\varphi|^2$ without requiring $\{z(k)\}$ to be uncorrelated across $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E|\varphi|^2 &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty \operatorname{tr}\big[ a(k) E[z(k)z^T(\ell)] a^T(\ell) \big] \\
&\le \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty \|a(k)\| \cdot \big[ E|z(k)|^2 \big]^{1/2} \cdot \big[ E|z(\ell)|^2 \big]^{1/2} \cdot \|a(\ell)\| \quad \text{(by Cauchy-Schwarz)} \\
&\le C_z \cdot \left[ \sum_{k=0}^\infty \|a(k)\| \right]^2 \le C_z \cdot C_a^2 \tag{2B.12}
\end{aligned}
$$

2. **Expand $R_\theta(N, r)$ into Filter Coefficients and $\theta$-Independent Sums ((2B.13))**:
   Substitute $s_\theta(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty g_\theta(k)v(t-k) + \sum_{k=0}^\infty m_\theta(k)w(t-k)$ into $R_\theta(N, r) \triangleq \sum_{t=r}^N [s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) - E s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t)]$.
   - Notice that the purely deterministic product $\big(M_\theta(q)w(t)\big)\big(M_\theta(q)w(t)\big)^T$ equals its own expectation and **cancels out completely** when subtracting $E[s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t)]$.
   - Notice also that the cross-terms between $v$ and $w$ have zero expectation because $E[v(t-k)] = 0$ and $w(t-\ell)$ is deterministic.
   Therefore, expanding the sums and pulling the $\theta$-dependent filter coefficients $g_\theta(k), m_\theta(\ell)$ outside the time sum $\sum_{t=r}^N$ gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_\theta(N, r) &= \sum_{t=r}^N \big[ s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) - E s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) \big] \\
&= \sum_{t=r}^N \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty g_\theta(k) \big[ v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell) - E v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell) \big] g_\theta^T(\ell) \\
&\quad + \sum_{t=r}^N \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty g_\theta(k) v(t-k) w^T(t-\ell) m_\theta^T(\ell) \\
&\quad + \sum_{t=r}^N \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty m_\theta(k) w(t-k) v^T(t-\ell) g_\theta^T(\ell) \tag{2B.13}
\end{aligned}
$$

3. **Separate $\theta$ from the Random Data Sums to Take $\sup_{\theta \in D_\theta}$ ((2B.14))**:
   Define the $\theta$-independent random matrices:

$$
S_r^N(k, \ell) \triangleq \sum_{t=r}^N \big[ v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell) - E v(t-k)v^T(t-\ell) \big]
$$

$$
\tilde{S}_r^N(k, \ell) \triangleq \sum_{t=r}^N v(t-k)w^T(t-\ell)
$$

   Taking the matrix norm of (2B.13) and then taking $\sup_{\theta \in D_\theta}$ on both sides pushes the supremum directly onto the deterministic filter gains $\bar{g}(k) = \sup_\theta \|g_\theta(k)\|$ and $\bar{m}(\ell) = \sup_\theta \|m_\theta(\ell)\|$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_r^N = \sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \|R_\theta(N, r)\| &\le \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty \sup_\theta \|g_\theta(k)\| \cdot \sup_\theta \|g_\theta(\ell)\| \cdot \|S_r^N(k, \ell)\| \\
&\quad + 2 \sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty \sup_\theta \|g_\theta(k)\| \cdot \sup_\theta \|m_\theta(\ell)\| \cdot \|\tilde{S}_r^N(k, \ell)\| \tag{2B.14}
\end{aligned}
$$

4. **Apply (2B.11)–(2B.12) and Lemma 2B.1**:
   Equation (2B.14) expresses the upper bound on $R_r^N$ as the sum of two double series of the exact form $\varphi = \sum_{k, \ell} a(k, \ell) z(k, \ell)$ from (2B.11):
   - In the first double series, the deterministic weights are $a(k, \ell) = \bar{g}(k)\bar{g}(\ell)$, whose sum over $k, \ell \ge 0$ is $\big(\sum_k \bar{g}(k)\big)\big(\sum_\ell \bar{g}(\ell)\big) = C_G^2 < \infty$, and the random variables are $z(k, \ell) = \|S_r^N(k, \ell)\|$, which satisfy $E|z(k, \ell)|^2 \le 4 C_e C_H^4 (N - r)$ by Lemma 2B.1 (2B.5).
   - In the second double series, the deterministic weights are $2\bar{g}(k)\bar{m}(\ell)$, whose sum over $k, \ell \ge 0$ is $2 C_G C_M < \infty$, and the random variables are $\|\tilde{S}_r^N(k, \ell)\|$, which satisfy $E\|\tilde{S}_r^N(k, \ell)\|^2 \le 4 C_e C_H^2 C_w^2 (N - r)$ by Lemma 2B.1 (2B.6).
   Using $(A + B)^2 \le 2A^2 + 2B^2$ together with (2B.12) gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\big[(R_r^N)^2\big] = E\left[ \sup_\theta \|R_\theta(N, r)\| \right]^2 &\le 2 \cdot C_G^4 \cdot 4 \cdot C_e \cdot C_H^4 \cdot (N - r) \\
&\quad + 8 \cdot C_G^2 \cdot C_M^2 \cdot 4 \cdot C_w^2 \cdot C_H^2 \cdot (N - r) \\
&\le C \cdot (N - r)
\end{aligned}
$$

   which completes the proof of Lemma 2B.2. $\blacksquare$

##### Step 4: Proof of Theorem 2B.1 — Part A: Convergence Along the Square Subsequence $N^2$
Now we have the bound $E[(R_1^N)^2] \le C \cdot N$. We want to prove that $\frac{1}{N} R_1^N \to 0$ **with probability 1** as $N \to \infty$.

> [!warning] Why Chebyshev + Borel-Cantelli Fails Directly on the Full Sequence $N$
> If we apply Chebyshev's inequality directly to $\frac{1}{N} R_1^N$, we get:
>
> $$
> P\left( \frac{1}{N} R_1^N > \varepsilon \right) \le \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2} E\left( \frac{R_1^N}{N} \right)^2 \le \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2} \frac{C \cdot N}{N^2} = \frac{C}{\varepsilon^2 N}
> $$
>
> This proves convergence in probability (and mean-square), **but to use the Borel-Cantelli Lemma for almost sure (w.p. 1) convergence, we need the infinite series of probabilities to converge!** Because the harmonic series $\sum_{N=1}^\infty \frac{1}{N} = \infty$ diverges, we cannot apply Borel-Cantelli directly to every integer $N$.

**The Classic Subsequence Trick ($k^2$)**:
Instead of checking every integer $N$, we first evaluate the average along the sparser **subsequence of perfect squares** $N^2$ (or $k^2$).
1. Denote $r(t, \theta) \triangleq s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t) - E s_\theta(t)s_\theta^T(t)$ (2B.15) and $R_r^N = \sup_{\theta \in D_\theta} \|R_\theta(N, r)\|$ (2B.16).
2. Replace $N$ by $N^2$ in Lemma 2B.2 (with $r = 1$):

$$
E\left( \frac{1}{N^2} R_1^{N^2} \right)^2 \le \left( \frac{1}{N^2} \right)^2 \cdot C \cdot N^2 = \frac{C}{N^2}
$$

3. By **Chebyshev's inequality** (Appendix I, (I.19)):

$$
P\left( \frac{1}{N^2} R_1^{N^2} > \varepsilon \right) \le \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2} E\left( \frac{R_1^{N^2}}{N^2} \right)^2 \le \frac{C}{\varepsilon^2 N^2}
$$

4. Summing over $k = 1, 2, \dots, \infty$ gives a **convergent $p$-series** ($\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} < \infty$):

$$
\sum_{k=1}^\infty P\left( \frac{1}{k^2} R_1^{k^2} > \varepsilon \right) \le \frac{C}{\varepsilon^2} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k^2} < \infty
$$

5. By the **Borel-Cantelli Lemma** (Appendix I, (I.18)), for any $\varepsilon > 0$, the event $\left\{ \frac{1}{k^2} R_1^{k^2} > \varepsilon \right\}$ occurs for at most finitely many $k$ with probability 1. Hence, along the square subsequence:

$$
\frac{1}{k^2} R_1^{k^2} \to 0, \quad \text{w.p. 1} \quad \text{as } k \to \infty \tag{2B.17}
$$

---

##### Step 5: Proof of Theorem 2B.1 — Part B: Bridging the Gap Between $N^2$ and $(N+1)^2$
We now know that $\frac{1}{k} R_1^k$ converges to $0$ w.p. 1 at the "milestone" points $k = N^2$. What happens for intermediate sample sizes $k$ that lie strictly between two consecutive squares, $N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2$?

1. For each block $N$, let $k_N \in [N^2, (N+1)^2]$ and $\theta_N \in D_\theta$ be the index and parameter where the maximum excursion inside the block $N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2$ is attained:

$$
\sup_{N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2} \frac{1}{k} R_1^k = \frac{1}{k_N} \left\| \sum_{t=1}^{k_N} r(t, \theta_N) \right\|
$$

2. Split the sum from $t = 1$ to $k_N$ into two pieces: the sum up to the square milestone $N^2$, plus the "remainder bridge" from $t = N^2 + 1$ to $k_N$. Using the triangle inequality:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sup_{N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2} \frac{1}{k} R_1^k &= \frac{1}{k_N} \left\| \sum_{t=1}^{k_N} r(t, \theta_N) \right\| \\
&\le \frac{1}{k_N} \left\| \sum_{t=1}^{N^2} r(t, \theta_N) \right\| + \frac{1}{k_N} \left\| \sum_{t=N^2+1}^{k_N} r(t, \theta_N) \right\| \tag{2B.18} \\
&\le \frac{1}{k_N} \cdot R_1^{N^2} + \frac{1}{k_N} \cdot R_{N^2+1}^{k_N}
\end{aligned}
$$

3. **Analyze the First Term on the RHS of (2B.18)**:
   Since $k_N \ge N^2$, we have $\frac{1}{k_N} R_1^{N^2} \le \frac{1}{N^2} R_1^{N^2}$, which converges to $0$ w.p. 1 as $N \to \infty$ directly by (2B.17)!
4. **Analyze the Second Term on the RHS of (2B.18) (the Remainder Bridge)**:
   Using $k_N \ge N^2 \implies \frac{1}{k_N^2} \le \frac{1}{N^4}$, and bounding the maximum over $k \in [N^2+1, (N+1)^2]$ by the sum of squares over all $k$ in that interval, we apply Lemma 2B.2 with $r = N^2 + 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E \left| \frac{1}{k_N} R_{N^2+1}^{k_N} \right|^2 &\le \frac{1}{N^4} \cdot E \max_{N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2} \big| R_{N^2+1}^k \big|^2 \\
&\le \frac{1}{N^4} \sum_{k=N^2+1}^{(N+1)^2} E \big| R_{N^2+1}^k \big|^2 \\
&\le \frac{1}{N^4} \sum_{k=N^2+1}^{(N+1)^2} C \cdot (k - N^2)
\end{aligned}
$$

   How big is the sum $\sum_{k=N^2+1}^{(N+1)^2} (k - N^2)$? Let $j = k - N^2$. Since $(N+1)^2 - N^2 = 2N + 1$, as $k$ runs from $N^2 + 1$ to $(N+1)^2$, $j$ runs from $1$ to $2N + 1$. The sum of the first $2N + 1$ integers is:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{2N+1} j = \frac{(2N+1)(2N+2)}{2} = (2N+1)(N+1) = O(N^2)
$$

   Dividing this $O(N^2)$ sum by $N^4$ yields:

$$
E \left| \frac{1}{k_N} R_{N^2+1}^{k_N} \right|^2 \le \frac{C}{N^2}
$$

5. Because $\sum_{N=1}^\infty \frac{C}{N^2} < \infty$, applying Chebyshev's inequality and the Borel-Cantelli Lemma one more time proves that the second term $\frac{1}{k_N} R_{N^2+1}^{k_N} \to 0$ w.p. 1 as $N \to \infty$ as well!
6. Combining the two terms of (2B.18) shows that the maximum over the entire block between consecutive squares vanishes almost surely:

$$
\sup_{N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2} \frac{1}{k} R_1^k \to 0, \quad \text{w.p. 1} \quad \text{as } N \to \infty \tag{2B.19}
$$

   which implies $\frac{1}{k} R_1^k \to 0$ w.p. 1 as $k \to \infty$ through all integers, completing the proof of Theorem 2B.1 (and Theorem 2.3). $\blacksquare$

##### Step 6: Corollary to Theorem 2B.1 — Relaxing Independence to a Martingale Difference Sequence
At the end of Appendix 2B (page 60), Ljung points out a vital relaxation of the statistical assumptions on $\{e(t)\}$ that is heavily used in recursive identification and closed-loop analysis.

> [!important] Corollary to Theorem 2B.1 (Martingale Difference Innovations)
> Suppose that the conditions of Theorem 2.3 / Theorem 2B.1 hold, EXCEPT that the strict mutual independence assumption on $\{e(t)\}$ in (2.88) is weakened to the conditional moment conditions:
>
> $$
> E\big[ e(t) \mid e(t-1), \dots, e(0) \big] = 0, \qquad E\big[ e^2(t) \mid e(t-1), \dots, e(0) \big] = \lambda \quad (\text{or } \Lambda_t)
> $$
>
> $$
> E|e(t)|^4 \le C
> $$
>
> Then Theorem 2B.1 (and Theorem 2.3) still holds! That is, $\{e(t)\}$ need not be mutually independent white noise; it is sufficient that $\{e(t)\}$ is a **martingale difference sequence** with constant (or deterministic) conditional variance and bounded fourth moments.

**Why the Proof Still Works under Martingale Differences**:
Where in the entire proof of Appendix 2B was independence of $\{e(t)\}$ actually used? **Only in Lemma 2B.1**, to show that $E[\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1)\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)] = 0$ unless the time indices $\{t - k_1, t - \ell_1\}$ and $\{s - k_2, s - \ell_2\}$ overlap!
Let us verify why this still holds when $\{e(t)\}$ is a martingale difference sequence:
- Suppose the four indices $\{t - k_1, t - \ell_1, s - k_2, s - \ell_2\}$ have no overlap between the $(t, k_1, \ell_1)$ pair and the $(s, k_2, \ell_2)$ pair.
- Let $\tau_{\max}$ be the **strictly largest** time index among $\{t - k_1, t - \ell_1, s - k_2, s - \ell_2\}$. Without loss of generality, suppose $\tau_{\max}$ belongs to the first factor $\alpha(t, k_1, \ell_1) = e(t-k_1)e^T(t-\ell_1) - \Lambda_{t-\ell_1}\delta_{k_1\ell_1}$.
- Case 1 ($k_1 \neq \ell_1$, so $\tau_{\max}$ appears only once, say $\tau_{\max} = t - k_1$): Condition on all past innovations up to $\tau_{\max} - 1$ using the Law of Iterated Expectations. Everything else in $\gamma$ is measurable with respect to the past $\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\max}-1}$ and pulls outside the conditional expectation, while $E[e(\tau_{\max}) \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\max}-1}] = 0$!
- Case 2 ($k_1 = \ell_1$, so $\tau_{\max} = t - k_1 = t - \ell_1$ is strictly larger than both $s - k_2$ and $s - \ell_2$): Condition on $\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\max}-1}$. Then $\alpha(s, k_2, \ell_2)$ is measurable with respect to $\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\max}-1}$ and pulls out, while:

$$
E\big[ \alpha(t, k_1, k_1) \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\max}-1} \big] = E\big[ e(\tau_{\max})e^T(\tau_{\max}) - \Lambda_{\tau_{\max}} \mid \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\max}-1} \big] = \Lambda_{\tau_{\max}} - \Lambda_{\tau_{\max}} = 0
$$

Thus, the 4-index coincidence counting argument in Lemma 2B.1 holds verbatim!

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Conceptual Leap 1: Why Bounded 4th Moments ($E|e(t)|^4 \le C_e$) Are Needed for 2nd-Moment Ergodicity**: At first glance, students wonder why second moments ($\lambda_t = E[e^2(t)]$) aren't enough to prove convergence of sample second moments $\frac{1}{N}\sum v(t)v(t-\tau)$. Remember that to prove a sample average converges to its expectation via Chebyshev's inequality, you must bound the **variance** (second moment) of that sample average. Since the sample average is already quadratic in $v(t)$ (and thus quadratic in $e(t)$), its variance is **quartic** (fourth-order) in $e(t)$, leading directly to the 6-fold sum and 4-index coincidence argument in Lemma 2B.1.
> - **Conceptual Leap 2: The $N^2$ Subsequence + Remainder Bridge Trick (Equations 2B.17–2B.19)**: Because the variance of the sample average decays at rate $O(1/N)$, Chebyshev's inequality gives a tail bound of $O(1/N)$, whose sum $\sum \frac{1}{N} = \infty$ diverges—blocking a direct Borel-Cantelli argument. Watch closely on pp. 59–60 how Ljung bypasses this without needing 8th moments: he proves almost sure convergence along the square subsequence $N^2$ (since $\sum \frac{1}{N^2} < \infty$) and then bounds the maximum fluctuation $\sup_{N^2 \le k \le (N+1)^2}$ inside each gap of length $2N + 1$ using a union/sum bound of order $\frac{1}{N^4} \sum_{j=1}^{2N+1} j = O(1/N^2)$, which is again summable!
> - **Conceptual Leap 3: Periodogram vs. Smoothed Periodogram**: Do not misread page 43 as saying the raw periodogram $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ converges to $\Phi_s(\omega)$. Theorem 2.3 proves that each fixed-lag sample covariance $\hat{R}_s^N(\tau)$ converges w.p. 1 to $R_s(\tau)$, which by Lemma 2.1 implies that **frequency-smoothed** versions of $|S_N(\omega)|^2$ converge to $\Phi_s(\omega)$.
> - **Notation & Typo Traps in Appendix 2B**:
>   1. On page 56, in the statement of Lemma 2B.1, $C_l = \sup_t E|e(t)|^4$ is a typographical error for $C_e = \sup_t E|e(t)|^4$ (used immediately in (2B.5) and (2B.6)).
>   2.
