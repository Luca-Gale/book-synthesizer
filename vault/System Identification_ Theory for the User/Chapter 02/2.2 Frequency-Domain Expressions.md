# 2.2 Frequency-Domain Expressions

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 2**: This section translates the discrete-time convolution and transfer-operator descriptions from Section 2.1 into the frequency domain, establishing how sinusoidal inputs, finite-sample Discrete Fourier Transforms (DFTs), and periodograms propagate through stable linear time-invariant (LTI) systems—and quantifying the exact finite-sample boundary error $R_N(\omega)$ that forms the foundation for spectral and frequency-domain identification in Section 2.3 and Chapter 6.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

In Section 2.1, a linear time-invariant (LTI) system was characterized in the time domain by its impulse response sequence $\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$, which relates the input $u(t)$ to the output $y(t)$ via an infinite convolution sum:

$$
y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k) = G(q) u(t)
$$

While convolution is physically transparent, it mixes values of the input across all past time steps. Why shift to the **frequency domain**?

1. **Sinusoids are Eigenfunctions of LTI Systems**: If you excite a stable LTI system with a pure sinusoid $u(t) = \cos(\omega t)$ that has been running since $t = -\infty$, the steady-state output is *not* a distorted waveform—it is the exact same sinusoid at the exact same frequency $\omega$, merely scaled in amplitude by $|G(e^{i\omega})|$ and shifted in phase by $\arg G(e^{i\omega})$. Thus, evaluating the transfer function $G(z)$ on the unit circle $z = e^{i\omega}$ completely characterizes the steady-state sinusoidal behavior of the system at every frequency $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$.
2. **Convolution Becomes Multiplication (Up to a Finite-Sample Transient)**: In real system identification experiments, we never observe signals from $t = -\infty$ to $+\infty$; we only collect a finite record of $N$ samples, $t = 1, 2, \dots, N$. When we take the normalized Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of a finite record, the infinite-time convolution $s(t) = G(q) w(t)$ converts into an **algebraic multiplication** in the frequency domain, plus an explicit remainder term $R_N(\omega)$:

$$
S_N(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega) + R_N(\omega)
$$

3. **Controlling the "End Effects" (Leakage / Transient Error)**: Because the finite observation window $[1, N]$ cuts off the unknown pre-experiment input ($t \le 0$) and misses the tail of the impulse response after $t = N$, the frequency-domain product $G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega)$ does not equal $S_N(\omega)$ exactly unless the input is periodic with period $N$. Theorem 2.1 proves that for any bounded input and strictly stable system with $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k |g(k)| < \infty$, this mismatch $R_N(\omega)$ decays at rate $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$. This single bound is the workhorse behind almost every asymptotic frequency-domain result in the book.

> [!note] Unit Sampling Interval Convention ($T = 1$)
> Following the convention established at the end of Section 2.1, Ljung writes all equations in Section 2.2 assuming a normalized sampling interval $T = 1$ time unit. Whenever physical sampling interval $T \neq 1$ is needed, replace $\omega$ with $\omega T$ so that $G(e^{i\omega})$ becomes $G_T(e^{i\omega T})$ and the Nyquist frequency range $[-\pi, \pi]$ becomes $[-\pi/T, \pi/T]$ radians per second.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Sinusoid Response, Transient Decay, and the Frequency Function

Suppose the deterministic LTI system $y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k)$ from Equation (2.6) is driven by a cosine input at angular frequency $\omega$:

$$
u(t) = \cos(\omega t) \tag{2.32}
$$

Using Euler's formula $e^{i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + i \sin(\omega t)$, we express $u(t)$ as the real part of a complex exponential:

$$
u(t) = \operatorname{Re} e^{i\omega t}
$$

##### Assumption 1: Eternal Sinusoidal Excitation ($t > -\infty$)
Suppose first that $u(t) = \cos(\omega t)$ has been applied since $t = -\infty$, and that the system is stable ($\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty$). Substituting $u(t - k) = \operatorname{Re} e^{i\omega(t - k)}$ into the convolution sum yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) \operatorname{Re} e^{i\omega(t - k)} \\
&= \operatorname{Re} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{i\omega(t - k)} \\
&= \operatorname{Re} \left\{ e^{i\omega t} \cdot \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i\omega k} \right\} \\
&= \operatorname{Re} \left\{ e^{i\omega t} \cdot G(e^{i\omega}) \right\} \\
&= |G(e^{i\omega})| \cos(\omega t + \varphi)
\end{aligned} \tag{2.33}
$$

where the phase angle $\varphi$ is defined as:

$$
\varphi = \arg G(e^{i\omega}) \tag{2.34}
$$

**Step-by-step justification of each equality in (2.33)**:
1. **First equality**: Direct substitution of $u(t - k) = \operatorname{Re} e^{i\omega(t - k)}$ into $y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k)$.
2. **Second equality**: Because the impulse response coefficients $g(k) \in \mathbb{R}$ are strictly real numbers (and the series converges absolutely by stability), the real-part operator $\operatorname{Re}(\cdot)$ is linear over real-weighted sums and can be pulled outside the summation: $\sum g(k) \operatorname{Re}(z_k) = \operatorname{Re}(\sum g(k) z_k)$.
3. **Third equality**: By the laws of exponents, $e^{i\omega(t - k)} = e^{i\omega t} e^{-i\omega k}$, and $e^{i\omega t}$ does not depend on the summation index $k$, so it factors out of the sum.
4. **Fourth equality**: Recall from Section 2.1 (Equations 2.16–2.17) that the transfer function is defined as $G(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) z^{-k}$. Evaluating at $z = e^{i\omega}$ gives $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i\omega k} = G(e^{i\omega})$.
5. **Fifth equality**: Write the complex number $G(e^{i\omega})$ in polar form as $G(e^{i\omega}) = |G(e^{i\omega})| e^{i\varphi}$ where $\varphi = \arg G(e^{i\omega})$. Multiplying by $e^{i\omega t}$ gives $|G(e^{i\omega})| e^{i(\omega t + \varphi)}$, whose real part is $|G(e^{i\omega})| \cos(\omega t + \varphi)$.

##### Assumption 2: Causal Start at $t = 0$ ($u(t) = 0$ for $t < 0$)
What if the cosine input was *not* applied since $t = -\infty$, but instead was switched on at $t = 0$ so that $u(t) = 0$ for all $t < 0$ and $u(t) = \cos(\omega t)$ for $t \ge 0$?
Then for any $t \ge 1$, the input terms $u(t - k)$ vanish whenever $t - k < 0 \iff k > t$. Thus the active sum only runs over $k = 1, 2, \dots, t$. Writing $\sum_{k=1}^{t} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} - \sum_{k=t+1}^{\infty}$ (or if $u(0) = 0$ so $u(t) = 0$ for $t \le 0$, then $t - k \le 0 \iff k \ge t$, so the missing tail starts at $k = t$), we get the steady-state expression (2.33) plus the additional transient term:

$$
-\operatorname{Re} \left\{ e^{i\omega t} \sum_{k=t}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i\omega k} \right\}
$$

(Note: In Ljung's text, the lower index of this subtracted tail is $k = t$, corresponding to $u(t - k)$ being zero for $t - k \le 0$; if $u(0) = \cos(0) = 1$ is included, the subtracted tail starts at $k = t + 1$. Either way, the asymptotic behavior is identical.)

By the triangle inequality and $|e^{i\theta}| = 1$, the magnitude of this additional term is bounded above ("dominated") by:

$$
\left| -\operatorname{Re} \left\{ e^{i\omega t} \sum_{k=t}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i\omega k} \right\} \right| \le \left| e^{i\omega t} \sum_{k=t}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i\omega k} \right| \le \sum_{k=t}^{\infty} |g(k)|
$$

Provided that $G(q)$ is **stable** ($\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty$), the tail of a convergent series must vanish as its lower limit goes to infinity:

$$
\lim_{t \to \infty} \sum_{k=t}^{\infty} |g(k)| = 0
$$

Therefore, the correction term is a **transient** that decays to zero as $t \to \infty$.

##### Definition of the Frequency Function & Graphical Representations
In steady state ("in stationarity"), the complex number:

$$
G(e^{i\omega}) \tag{2.35}
$$

which is the transfer function $G(z)$ evaluated on the unit circle $z = e^{i\omega}$, contains complete information about how a sinusoid of frequency $\omega$ is transformed: its modulus $|G(e^{i\omega})|$ is the amplitude gain and its argument $\arg G(e^{i\omega})$ is the phase shift in radians. Consequently, the complex-valued function:

$$
G(e^{i\omega}), \qquad -\pi \le \omega \le \pi \tag{2.36}
$$

is defined as the **frequency function** of the system.
- **Bode Plot**: Graphical display of $\log |G(e^{i\omega})|$ (amplitude curve) and $\arg G(e^{i\omega})$ (phase curve) plotted against $\log \omega$ (for $\omega > 0$).
- **Nyquist Plot**: Parametric plot of the complex trajectory $G(e^{i\omega})$ in the complex plane ($\operatorname{Im} G(e^{i\omega})$ vs. $\operatorname{Re} G(e^{i\omega})$) as $\omega$ varies over $[-\pi, \pi]$ (or $[0, \pi]$).

#### 2.2.2 Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), Inverse DFT, and the Periodogram

Consider a finite sequence of observations $u(t)$ for $t = 1, 2, \dots, N$ (often denoted compactly as $u_1^N$). We define the normalized finite-time Fourier transform $U_N(\omega)$ for any real $\omega$ by:

$$
U_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} u(t) e^{-i\omega t} \tag{2.37}
$$

> [!important] Ljung's $1/\sqrt{N}$ Normalization Convention
> Unlike many signal processing textbooks that place $1$ on the forward DFT and $1/N$ on the inverse DFT, Ljung places $1/\sqrt{N}$ symmetrically on **both** the forward transform (2.37) and the inverse transform (2.38). Why?
> 1. It makes the DFT a **unitary transformation** (preserving Euclidean norm directly without extra $1/N$ factors in Parseval's theorem).
> 2. Its squared magnitude $|U_N(\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{N} \left| \sum_{t=1}^N u(t) e^{-i\omega t} \right|^2$ is **automatically normalized by $1/N$**, meaning $|U_N(\omega)|^2$ is already the **periodogram** (average signal power per unit frequency) and converges to a finite spectral density for stochastic processes as $N \to \infty$.

When $\omega$ is restricted to the $N$ equally spaced harmonic grid points $\omega = 2\pi k / N$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots, N$, the values $U_N(2\pi k / N)$ form the **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)** of $u_1^N$. The original time-domain sequence $u(t)$ ($t = 1, \dots, N$) is recovered exactly via the **Inverse DFT**:

$$
u(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{N} U_N(2\pi k / N) e^{i 2\pi k t / N} \tag{2.38}
$$

##### Proof of the Inverse DFT (2.38) via Orthogonality of Complex Exponentials
To verify (2.38), first establish the fundamental discrete orthogonality identity for any integer $r = t - s$:

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i r k / N} = \begin{cases} 1, & r = 0 \pmod{N} \\ 0, & 1 \le |r| < N \end{cases} \tag{2.39}
$$

*Why does (2.39) hold?*
- When $r = 0$ (or any integer multiple of $N$), $e^{2\pi i r k / N} = e^0 = 1$ for every $k$, so the sum of $N$ ones divided by $N$ is $\frac{1}{N} \cdot N = 1$.
- When $1 \le |r| < N$, let $z = e^{2\pi i r / N} \neq 1$. The sum is a finite geometric series:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{N} z^k = z \frac{1 - z^N}{1 - z} = e^{2\pi i r / N} \frac{1 - e^{2\pi i r}}{1 - e^{2\pi i r / N}} = 0
$$

because $e^{2\pi i r} = 1$ for every integer $r$.

Now substitute the definition of $U_N(2\pi k / N)$ from (2.37) (using dummy time variable $s$ instead of $t$) into the right-hand side of (2.38):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{N} U_N(2\pi k / N) e^{i 2\pi k t / N}
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(s) \exp\left( -\frac{i 2\pi k s}{N} \right) \right] \cdot \exp\left( \frac{i 2\pi k t}{N} \right) \\
&= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(s) \exp\left( -\frac{i 2\pi k s}{N} \right) \cdot \exp\left( \frac{i 2\pi k t}{N} \right) \\
&= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(s) \sum_{k=1}^{N} \exp\left( \frac{2\pi i k (t - s)}{N} \right) \\
&= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(s) N \cdot \delta(t - s) \\
&= u(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\delta(t - s)$ is the Kronecker delta ($\delta(0) = 1$ and $\delta(r) = 0$ for $r \neq 0$). Since both $t$ and $s$ lie in $\{1, \dots, N\}$, their difference $r = t - s$ satisfies $|r| < N$, so $r = 0 \pmod{N}$ occurs if and only if $s = t$.

##### Symmetry, Periodicity, and Symmetric Summation Bounds
From definition (2.37), since $t$ is an integer, $e^{-i(\omega + 2\pi)t} = e^{-i\omega t} e^{-i 2\pi t} = e^{-i\omega t}$. Thus $U_N(\omega)$ is periodic with period $2\pi$:

$$
U_N(\omega + 2\pi) = U_N(\omega) \tag{2.40}
$$

Furthermore, because $u(t)$ is real-valued ($\overline{u(t)} = u(t)$), negating $\omega$ simply conjugates the complex exponential:

$$
U_N(-\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} u(t) e^{i\omega t} = \overline{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} u(t) e^{-i\omega t}} = \overline{U_N(\omega)} \tag{2.41}
$$

where the overbar denotes complex conjugation (**Hermitian symmetry**). Consequently:
- $|U_N(-\omega)| = |U_N(\omega)|$ (even magnitude) and $\arg U_N(-\omega) = -\arg U_N(\omega)$ (odd phase).
- $U_N(\omega)$ is completely determined by its values on $[0, \pi]$.
- Using periodicity (2.40), shifting the index $k \in \{N/2 + 1, \dots, N\}$ downward by $N$ maps it to $k - N \in \{-N/2 + 1, \dots, 0\}$. Thus, assuming $N$ is even (with analogous bounds for odd $N$), the inverse DFT (2.38) is symmetrically written over $[-\pi, \pi]$ as:

$$
u(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k = -N/2 + 1}^{N/2} U_N(2\pi k / N) e^{i 2\pi k t / N} \tag{2.42}
$$

Pairing $+k$ and $-k$ terms via (2.41) allows (2.42) to be written purely in terms of real $\cos(\omega t)$ and $\sin(\omega t)$ basis functions (avoiding complex numbers).

##### The Periodogram and Parseval's Relationship
In (2.42), the complex coefficient $U_N(2\pi k / N)$ measures the "weight" carried by the harmonic frequency $\omega = 2\pi k / N$ in decomposing $\{u(t)\}_{t=1}^N$. Its squared modulus:

$$
|U_N(\omega)|^2 \tag{2.43}
$$

is defined as the **periodogram** of the finite signal $u(t), t = 1, 2, \dots, N$, measuring the contribution of frequency $\omega$ to the average signal power.

This energy/power interpretation is made exact by **Parseval's relationship**:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{N} |U_N(2\pi k / N)|^2 = \sum_{t=1}^{N} u^2(t) \tag{2.44}
$$

*Proof of Parseval's Relationship (2.44)*:
Write $|U_N(2\pi k / N)|^2 = U_N(2\pi k / N) \overline{U_N(2\pi k / N)}$, expand both factors using (2.37), interchange summation order, and apply (2.39):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{N} |U_N(2\pi k / N)|^2
&= \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} u(t) e^{-i 2\pi k t / N} \right) \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(s) e^{i 2\pi k s / N} \right) \\
&= \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(t) u(s) \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i k (s - t) / N} \right) \\
&= \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} u(t) u(s) \delta(s - t) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} u^2(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Just as a prism decomposes white light into spectral energy components, Parseval's identity shows that the total time-domain energy $\sum_{t=1}^N u^2(t)$ is the exact sum of the periodogram values across the $N$ DFT frequencies $\omega_k = 2\pi k / N$.

#### 2.2.3 Worked Examples: Periodograms of Sinusoidal and Periodic Signals

##### Example 2.1: Periodogram of a Sinusoid
Suppose the input is a pure cosine wave of amplitude $A$ and period $N_0 > 1$ (an integer):

$$
u(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t), \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{N_0} \tag{2.45}
$$

**Crucial Assumption**: Assume the observation length $N$ is an exact integer multiple of the period $N_0$, i.e., $N = s \cdot N_0$ for some integer $s \ge 1$ (so the data window contains exactly $s$ full periods, eliminating spectral leakage). Note that $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{N_0} = \frac{2\pi s}{N}$ lies directly on the $s$-th DFT frequency bin!

**Step-by-step derivation**:
1. Expand the cosine using Euler's identity:

$$
\cos(\omega_0 t) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ e^{i\omega_0 t} + e^{-i\omega_0 t} \right]
$$

2. Substitute into the DFT definition (2.37):

$$
U_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{A}{2} \left[ e^{i(\omega_0 - \omega)t} + e^{-i(\omega_0 + \omega)t} \right]
$$

3. Evaluate at the DFT grid frequencies $\omega = \frac{2\pi k}{N}$ (with $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi s}{N}$):

$$
U_N\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right) = \frac{A \sqrt{N}}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i (s - k) t / N} + \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} e^{-2\pi i (s + k) t / N} \right]
$$

4. Apply the orthogonality relation (2.39) for $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$ (noting $N_0 > 2 \implies 0 < s < N/2$, so $s - k \equiv 0 \pmod{N}$ and $s + k \equiv 0 \pmod{N}$ cannot hold simultaneously):
   - When $\omega = +\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi s}{N}$ ($k = s$): the first sum is $1$ and the second sum is $0$, giving $U_N(\omega_0) = \frac{A \sqrt{N}}{2}$.
   - When $\omega = -\omega_0 = -\frac{2\pi s}{N}$ ($k = -s$): the first sum is $0$ and the second sum is $1$, giving $U_N(-\omega_0) = \frac{A \sqrt{N}}{2}$.
   - For all other DFT grid frequencies $\omega = \frac{2\pi k}{N}$ with $k \neq \pm s \pmod{N}$: both sums are $0$, so $U_N(\omega) = 0$.
5. Squaring $|U_N(\omega)|$ yields the periodogram:

$$
|U_N(\omega)|^2 = \begin{cases} N \cdot \dfrac{A^2}{4}, & \text{if } \omega = \pm \omega_0 = \pm \dfrac{2\pi}{N_0} = \pm \dfrac{2\pi s}{N} \\[8pt] 0, & \text{if } \omega = \dfrac{2\pi k}{N}, \quad k \neq \pm s \end{cases} \tag{2.46}
$$

**Sanity Check with Parseval's Theorem**: The periodogram has two spikes in $[-\pi, \pi]$, each of height $N A^2 / 4$. Their sum is $2 \cdot (N A^2 / 4) = N A^2 / 2$, which matches the time-domain energy $\sum_{t=1}^N A^2 \cos^2(\omega_0 t) = N A^2 / 2$. Notice that the spike height grows linearly with $N$ because a persistent sinusoid has finite *power* ($A^2/2$) and therefore infinite total *energy* as $N \to \infty$.

##### Example 2.2: Periodogram of a General Periodic Signal
Suppose $u(t)$ is periodic with integer period $N_0$, meaning $u(t) = u(t + N_0)$ for all $t$, and we observe $u(t)$ over the interval $[1, N]$ where $N = s \cdot N_0$ ($s \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ complete periods).

**Step-by-step derivation**:
1. Over a single period $[1, N_0]$, apply the symmetric inverse DFT representation (2.42) with $N$ replaced by $N_0$:

$$
u(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_0}} \sum_{r = -N_0/2 + 1}^{N_0/2} A_r e^{2\pi i t r / N_0} \tag{2.47}
$$

where $A_r$ are the single-period normalized DFT coefficients:

$$
A_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_0}} \sum_{t=1}^{N_0} u(t) e^{-2\pi i t r / N_0} \tag{2.48}
$$

2. Because $e^{2\pi i (t + N_0) r / N_0} = e^{2\pi i t r / N_0}$ is periodic with period $N_0$ and $u(t)$ is also periodic with period $N_0$, the representation (2.47) holds identically over the **entire** $N$-sample interval $t \in [1, N]$.
3. Substitute (2.47) into the $N$-sample DFT (2.37) evaluated at $\omega = \frac{2\pi k}{N}$ (noting that $\frac{r}{N_0} = \frac{r \cdot s}{N}$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_N\left(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_0}} \sum_{r = -N_0/2 + 1}^{N_0/2} A_r e^{2\pi i t (r s) / N} \right) e^{-2\pi i k t / N} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{s \cdot N_0 \cdot N_0}} \sum_{r = -N_0/2 + 1}^{N_0/2} A_r \sum_{t=1}^{N} e^{2\pi i (r s - k) t / N} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{s}}{N} \sum_{r = -N_0/2 + 1}^{N_0/2} A_r \cdot N \delta(k - r s) = \begin{cases} \sqrt{s} \, A_r, & \text{if } k = r \cdot s \\ 0, & \text{if } k \neq r \cdot s \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

4. Squaring the magnitude gives the periodogram across the $N$-point frequency grid:

$$
|U_N(\omega)|^2 = \begin{cases} s \cdot |A_r|^2, & \text{if } \omega = \dfrac{2\pi r}{N_0}, \quad r = 0, \pm 1, \dots, \pm \dfrac{N_0}{2} \\[8pt] 0, & \text{if } \omega = \dfrac{2\pi k}{N}, \quad k \neq r \cdot s \end{cases} \tag{2.49}
$$

> [!note] Deterministic Periodicity vs. Stochastic Realizations
> In Examples 2.1 and 2.2, the periodograms are clean line spectra (isolated spikes at harmonics of $2\pi/N_0$ and zero elsewhere). In sharp contrast, when a signal is a realization of a **stochastic process**, its periodogram $|U_N(\omega)|^2$ does *not* settle down to a smooth curve as $N \to \infty$—instead, it fluctuates wildly as a function of $\omega$ with an asymptotic variance equal to the square of its mean (explored in Section 2.3, Figure 2.8, and Lemma 6.2).

#### 2.2.4 Transformation of Periodograms & Theorem 2.1 (Finite-Sample DFT Leakage Bound)

When a signal is filtered through a linear time-invariant system, how does its finite-sample Fourier transform (and hence its periodogram) change? Theorem 2.1 provides the exact relation and bounds the finite-window transient error.

> [!important] Theorem 2.1: Transformation of Finite-Sample Fourier Transforms by an LTI System
> Let $\{s(t)\}$ and $\{w(t)\}$ be related by the strictly stable linear system $G(q)$:
>
> $$
> s(t) = G(q) w(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) w(t - k) \tag{2.50}
> $$
>
> **Assumptions**:
> 1. **Bounded Input (including unknown past)**: The input $w(t)$ for $t \le 0$ is unknown, but $w(t)$ is uniformly bounded for all $t \in \mathbb{Z}$:
>
> $$
> |w(t)| \le C_w \quad \text{for all } t
> $$
>
> 2. **Strict Stability with Finite First Lag Moment**: The impulse response satisfies:
>
> $$
> C_G = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k |g(k)| < \infty \tag{2.55}
> $$
>
> Define the normalized $N$-sample Fourier transforms over $t = 1, \dots, N$:
>
> $$
> S_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} s(t) e^{-i\omega t} \tag{2.51}
> $$
>
> $$
> W_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} w(t) e^{-i\omega t} \tag{2.52}
> $$
>
> **Conclusion**: For every $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$,
>
> $$
> S_N(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega) + R_N(\omega) \tag{2.53}
> $$
>
> where the remainder term $R_N(\omega)$ satisfies the uniform bound:
>
> $$
> |R_N(\omega)| \le 2 C_w \cdot \frac{C_G}{\sqrt{N}} \tag{2.54}
> $$
>

---

##### Complete Step-by-Step Proof of Theorem 2.1

**Step 1: Expand $S_N(\omega)$ and change the time summation variable.**
Substitute $s(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) w(t - k)$ into the definition (2.51) of $S_N(\omega)$ and interchange the finite sum over $t$ with the absolutely convergent sum over $k$:

$$
S_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} s(t) e^{-i t \omega} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \sum_{t=1}^{N} g(k) w(t - k) e^{-i t \omega}
$$

Introduce the change of variables $\tau = t - k$ (so $t = \tau + k$, and as $t$ runs from $1$ to $N$, $\tau$ runs from $1 - k$ to $N - k$). Split the exponential as $e^{-i(\tau + k)\omega} = e^{-i k \omega} e^{-i \tau \omega}$:

$$
S_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i k \omega} \cdot \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{N - k} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega}
$$

**Step 2: Compare the shifted window $\tau \in [1 - k, N - k]$ against the observed window $\tau \in [1, N]$.**
Notice that if the inner sum ran over $\tau = 1, \dots, N$ instead of $\tau = 1 - k, \dots, N - k$, then $\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau=1}^N w(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega}$ would be $W_N(\omega)$ independent of $k$, and the outer sum would immediately evaluate to $G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega)$!

Why do the two sums differ for a fixed lag $k \ge 1$?
- The shifted sum $\sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{N - k}$ **includes** $k$ pre-window terms ($\tau = 1 - k, 2 - k, \dots, 0$) that are *not* in $W_N(\omega)$.
- The shifted sum **omits** $k$ end-of-window terms ($\tau = N - k + 1, N - k + 2, \dots, N$) that *are* in $W_N(\omega)$.
- All intermediate terms ($\tau = 1, \dots, N - k$ when $k < N$) cancel identically in the difference!

Therefore, taking the difference between $W_N(\omega)$ and the shifted sum for any $k \ge 1$ leaves at most $k$ terms in each of the two non-overlapping boundary regions:

$$
W_N(\omega) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{N - k} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{0} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = N - k + 1}^{N} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega}
$$

(Note: Even if $k > N$, adding and subtracting the overlapping range $\tau \in [1 - k, 0] \cap [N - k + 1, N]$ yields the exact same identity.)

**Step 3: Bound the boundary mismatch for each lag $k$.**
Applying the triangle inequality and using $|w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega}| = |w(\tau)| \le C_w$ on each of the $k$ terms in both sums gives Equation (2.56):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left| W_N(\omega) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{N - k} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} \right|
&\le \left| \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{0} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} \right| + \left| \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = N - k + 1}^{N} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} \right| \\
&\le \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{0} C_w + \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = N - k + 1}^{N} C_w \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} (k \cdot C_w) + \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} (k \cdot C_w) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{N}} \cdot k \cdot C_w
\end{aligned} \tag{2.56}
$$

**Step 4: Sum over all lags $k = 1, \dots, \infty$ to bound $|R_N(\omega)|$.**
Since $G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i k \omega} W_N(\omega)$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|R_N(\omega)| = \left| S_N(\omega) - G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega) \right|
&= \left| \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-i k \omega} \left[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{N - k} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} - W_N(\omega) \right] \right| \\
&\le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k) e^{-i k \omega}| \cdot \left| \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^{N - k} w(\tau) e^{-i \tau \omega} - W_N(\omega) \right| \\
&\le \frac{2}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |k g(k) C_w e^{-i k \omega}| \\
&= \frac{2 C_w}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k |g(k)| = \frac{2 C_w \cdot C_G}{\sqrt{N}}
\end{aligned}
$$

which establishes (2.53)–(2.55). $\blacksquare$

---

##### Corollary: Exact Disappearance of $R_N(\omega)$ for Periodic Inputs

> [!tip] Corollary to Theorem 2.1
> Suppose $\{w(t)\}$ is periodic with period $N$ (i.e., $w(\tau + N) = w(\tau)$ for all $\tau$). Then the remainder term $R_N(\omega)$ in (2.53) is **identically zero** at all DFT grid frequencies $\omega = 2\pi m / N$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}$):
>
> $$
> R_N\left(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\right) = 0 \implies S_N\left(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\right) = G\left(e^{i 2\pi m / N}\right) W_N\left(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\right)
> $$
>

*Proof*: Look at the two boundary sums in Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 2.1 evaluated at $\omega = 2\pi m / N$. In the first boundary sum ($\tau = 1 - k, \dots, 0$), let $\tau' = \tau + N \in \{N - k + 1, \dots, N\}$. Since $w(\tau) = w(\tau + N) = w(\tau')$ by $N$-periodicity and $e^{-i (2\pi m / N) \tau} = e^{-i (2\pi m / N) (\tau' - N)} = e^{-i (2\pi m / N) \tau'}$, the pre-window sum $\sum_{\tau = 1 - k}^0 w(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega}$ is **identically equal** to the post-window sum $\sum_{\tau' = N - k + 1}^N w(\tau') e^{-i\tau'\omega}$! Thus the two boundary sums cancel each other out term-by-term for every lag $k$, making the left-hand side of (2.56) zero for all $k$, and hence $R_N(2\pi m / N) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 — Why $C_G = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k |g(k)| < \infty$ Is Required in Theorem 2.1**: Ordinary BIBO stability only requires $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty$. Why does Theorem 2.1 require the stronger condition $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k |g(k)| < \infty$? Because at lag $k$, the time shift $w(t - k)$ shifts the finite window $[1, N]$ by $k$ samples, creating $k$ missing samples at the start and $k$ missing samples at the end—so the mismatch grows linearly with $k$. Note also that $\frac{d}{d\omega} G(e^{i\omega}) = -i \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k g(k) e^{-i k \omega}$, so $C_G < \infty$ guarantees that the frequency function $G(e^{i\omega})$ is continuously differentiable (smooth) with respect to $\omega$.
> - **Leap 2 — Scaling of $|W_N(\omega)|$ vs. $|R_N(\omega)|$**: Why is an error bound of order $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ in (2.54) small enough? Because for typical persistent signals (white noise, quasi-stationary inputs, etc.), the normalized DFT $W_N(\omega)$ has magnitude of order $\mathcal{O}(1)$ (since $\sum |W_N|^2 = \sum w^2(t) \sim \mathcal{O}(N)$, so the average $|W_N(\omega)|^2$ is $\mathcal{O}(1)$). Thus, the signal term $G(e^{i\omega}) W_N(\omega)$ is $\mathcal{O}(1)$ while the transient leakage error $R_N(\omega)$ vanishes at rate $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$!
> - **Notation Trap 1 — The $1/\sqrt{N}$ Factor in $U_N(\omega)$**: Always remember that Ljung's $U_N(\omega)$ in (2.37) includes $1/\sqrt{N}$. Consequently, the periodogram is simply $|U_N(\omega)|^2$ (Equation 2.43), *not* $\frac{1}{N}|U_N(\omega)|^2$.
> - **Notation Trap 2 — Role Switching of Input/Output Letters**: In Equations (2.32)–(2.49), the input is $u(t)$ and output is $y(t)$. In Theorem 2.1 (Equations 2.50–2.56), Ljung switches to generic filter input $w(t)$ and filter output $s(t)$ so that Theorem 2.1 can later be applied both to the deterministic input channel ($w = u$) and to the stochastic disturbance channel ($w = e$, $G = H$) in Section 2.3!
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why does the periodogram of a sinusoid (Example 2.1) have spike height proportional to $N$ ($\frac{N A^2}{4}$), whereas Theorem 2.1 gives a remainder $R_N(\omega)$ proportional to $1/\sqrt{N}$?
>   2. How does the Corollary to Theorem 2.1 explain why periodic excitation signals (like multisine inputs) are prized in frequency-domain system identification?
