# 2.1 Impulse Responses, Disturbances, and Transfer Functions

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 2**: This opening section establishes the canonical time-domain mathematical framework for linear time-invariant (LTI) discrete-time systems used throughout the entire book: $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$. By bridging continuous-time physical convolution with zero-order-hold computer sampling, unifying deterministic-looking step/pulse disturbances and random measurement noise under a single filtered white-noise paradigm, and introducing the shift operator $q$ alongside BIBO and strict stability criteria, Section 2.1 lays the exact algebraic foundation for frequency-domain analysis (Section 2.2), spectral characterization (Section 2.3), ergodicity (Section 2.4), and parametric model estimation in Parts II and III.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Before we can estimate a model of a dynamical system from experimental input-output data, we must decide *what mathematical language* will describe how past inputs and unmeasured disturbances shape the current output. In engineering practice, almost every physical process exists in continuous time, yet modern data acquisition and control systems interact with the plant at discrete sampling instants $t_k = kT$. Furthermore, no real-world output $y(t)$ is purely determined by the user's known control input $u(t)$: sensors suffer from thermal noise and drift, while unmeasured environmental forces—such as wind gusts hitting an aircraft or people walking into a heated room—continually perturb the trajectory.

Ljung introduces three foundational ideas in this section to tame these realities before any identification algorithm is even formulated:

1. **Exact Discretization via Zero-Order Hold (ZOH)**: Rather than approximating continuous-time differential equations prematurely, Ljung shows that if the computer holds the actuator input constant between sampling instants ($u(t) = u_k$ for $kT \le t < (k+1)T$), the continuous-time convolution integral collapses *exactly* into a discrete-time convolution sum at the sampling instants. Thus, a discrete-time impulse response sequence $\{g_T(\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}$ is not a crude approximation—it is an exact description of the sampled-data plant.
2. **A Unified Stochastic Model for Both "Classical" and "Noisy" Disturbances**: In classical control theory, disturbances are often modeled as deterministic steps, pulses, or sinusoids, whereas in stochastic control they are modeled as random noise. Ljung bridges this divide by passing an independent white-noise sequence $\{e(t)\}$ through a linear shaping filter $H(q)$ to produce the additive output disturbance $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$. By changing only the probability density function (PDF) of $e(t)$—for instance, from a sparse impulse distribution that fires rarely to a dense Gaussian distribution that fires at every sample—the exact same second-order linear structure generates either isolated, deterministic-looking step/impulse transients or continuous, irregular random fluctuations.
3. **Operator Algebra via the Shift Operator $q$**: Writing infinite convolution sums $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)u(t-k)$ quickly becomes algebraically unwieldy. By introducing the forward shift operator $q u(t) = u(t+1)$ and backward shift operator $q^{-1} u(t) = u(t-1)$, convolution sums transform into compact operator products $G(q)u(t)$ and $H(q)e(t)$ that mirror Laplace and $z$-transform transfer functions while operating directly on time-domain sequences.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Continuous-Time Impulse Response & Fundamental System Properties

Consider a dynamical system with a scalar input signal $u(t)$ and a scalar output signal $y(t)$, as depicted in Figure 2.1.

![[assets/fig_2_1_the_system.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 2.1)
> - **Block Geometry**: The rectangular box represents the deterministic plant dynamics mapping the scalar input trajectory $u(t)$ (entering from the left arrow) to the scalar output trajectory $y(t)$ (exiting to the right arrow).
> - **Implicit Assumptions**: No external disturbances or sensor inaccuracies are shown yet; the output $y(t)$ is completely and uniquely determined by the history of the input $u(s)$ for $s \le t$.

To characterize this block mathematically, Ljung invokes three foundational structural assumptions:

1. **Time Invariance**: The system's response to a given input signal does not depend on absolute time (shifting the input in time by $t_0$ merely shifts the output by the same $t_0$).
2. **Linearity**: The output response to any linear combination of inputs $\alpha_1 u_1(t) + \alpha_2 u_2(t)$ is the exact same linear combination of the individual output responses $\alpha_1 y_1(t) + \alpha_2 y_2(t)$.
3. **Causality**: The output at a certain time $t$ depends *only* on the input up to that time, $\{u(s) : s \le t\}$, and never on future inputs ($s > t$).

Under **linearity**, **time invariance**, and **causality**, the system is completely characterized by its **impulse response** (also called its **weighting function**) $g(\tau)$ via the convolution integral:

$$
y(t) = \int_{\tau=0}^{\infty} g(\tau) u(t - \tau) \, d\tau \tag{2.1}
$$

- **Why the lower limit is $\tau = 0$**: Causality dictates that inputs at future times $t - \tau > t$ (which correspond to $\tau < 0$) cannot influence $y(t)$, meaning $g(\tau) = 0$ for all $\tau < 0$.
- **Physical Interpretation of $g(\tau)$**: The function value $g(\tau)$ measures the weight with which an input applied $\tau$ time units ago, $u(t - \tau)$, contributes to the present output $y(t)$. Knowing $\{g(\tau)\}_{\tau=0}^{\infty}$ and the input history $u(s)$ for $s \le t$ allows us to compute the corresponding output $y(s)$ for all $s \le t$ for any arbitrary input. *The impulse response is thus a complete characterization of the system.*

#### 2.2.2 Zero-Order Hold Sampling & Exact Sampled-Data Convolution

In modern system identification, data acquisition is performed almost exclusively in discrete time. Suppose the continuous output $y(t)$ is observed at equidistant **sampling instants** $t_k = kT$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$, where $T > 0$ is the **sampling interval**. Evaluating Equation (2.1) at $t = kT$ yields:

$$
y(kT) = \int_{\tau=0}^{\infty} g(\tau) u(kT - \tau) \, d\tau \tag{2.2}
$$

In computer-controlled systems, the digital-to-analog converter (DAC) typically holds the actuator command constant across each sampling period (a **zero-order hold**, or ZOH):

$$
u(t) = u_k, \quad kT \le t < (k + 1)T \tag{2.3}
$$

Let us substitute the piecewise-constant input condition (2.3) into the integral (2.2) without skipping any steps:

1. **Partition the integration domain $[0, \infty)$ into intervals of length $T$**:
   We break the improper integral $\int_{\tau=0}^{\infty}$ into an infinite sum of definite integrals over the sub-intervals $[(\ell - 1)T, \ell T]$ for $\ell = 1, 2, \dots$:

$$
y(kT) = \int_{\tau=0}^{\infty} g(\tau) u(kT - \tau) \, d\tau = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} \int_{\tau=(\ell-1)T}^{\ell T} g(\tau) u(kT - \tau) \, d\tau
$$

2. **Evaluate $u(kT - \tau)$ inside each sub-interval**:
   When the integration variable $\tau$ lies in the open interval $(\ell - 1)T < \tau \le \ell T$, the shifted time argument $t' = kT - \tau$ satisfies:

$$
(k - \ell)T \le kT - \tau < (k - \ell + 1)T
$$

   By the zero-order hold definition (2.3), throughout this entire interval of width $T$, the input $u(kT - \tau)$ is identically equal to the constant discrete sample $u_{k-\ell}$!

3. **Factor $u_{k-\ell}$ out of each sub-integral**:
   Because $u_{k-\ell}$ does not depend on $\tau$ inside $[(\ell-1)T, \ell T]$, we factor it outside the integral:

$$
y(kT) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} \left[ \int_{\tau=(\ell-1)T}^{\ell T} g(\tau) \, d\tau \right] u_{k-\ell} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g_T(\ell) u_{k-\ell} \tag{2.4}
$$

   where we define the discrete-time **sampled-data impulse response** sequence $\{g_T(\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}$ as:

$$
g_T(\ell) = \int_{\tau=(\ell-1)T}^{\ell T} g(\tau) \, d\tau \tag{2.5}
$$

> [!important] Crucial Insight: Zero Approximation Error Under ZOH
> Equation (2.4) involves **no approximation whatsoever** provided the continuous-time input satisfies the zero-order hold condition (2.3). Even if $u(t)$ is not strictly piecewise constant, (2.4) remains a good approximation as long as $u(t)$ does not vary significantly within a single sampling interval $T$. Notice also why the sum starts at $\ell = 1$: because $u(t) = u_k$ only takes effect at $t = kT$, the integral over $[0, 0]$ at $t = kT$ has measure zero, so $u_k$ (at lag $\ell = 0$) does not instantaneously affect $y(kT)$ unless $g(\tau)$ contains a Dirac delta at $\tau = 0$ (a direct feedthrough term).

**Normalized Time-Unit Convention ($T = 1$)**:
Whenever the explicit numerical value of $T$ is not essential to the discussion, Ljung normalizes the time scale by setting $T = 1$ time unit and uses the symbol $t$ to enumerate discrete sampling instants ($t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$). Equation (2.4) is then written cleanly as:

$$
y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k), \quad t = 0, 1, 2, \dots \tag{2.6}
$$

To denote finite segments of a discrete-time signal sequence, Ljung introduces the vector history notation:

$$
y_s^t = (y(s), y(s + 1), \dots, y(t)) \tag{2.7}
$$

and, when starting from the initial observation $s = 1$, the shorthand:

$$
y_1^t = y^t
$$

#### 2.2.3 Additive Disturbances & Real-World Perturbation Sources

Equation (2.6) claims that the output $y(t)$ can be calculated *exactly* once the input sequence $\{u(t)\}$ is known. In real engineering systems, this is unrealistic: signals beyond our control invariably perturb the output. Within a linear framework, all such effects are lumped into an **additive output disturbance** term $v(t)$, as illustrated in Figure 2.2:

$$
y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k) + v(t) \tag{2.8}
$$

![[assets/fig_2_1_system_with_disturbance.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 2.2)
> - **Signal Flow**: The controllable input $u(t)$ passes through the plant block $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t-k)$, producing the undisturbed plant output. At the summing junction ($+$), the lumped disturbance $v(t)$ enters additively from above to form the measured output $y(t)$.
> - **Handling Input Noise ("Errors-in-Variables")**: What if the input measurements themselves are corrupted by noise, or disturbances enter at the plant input rather than the output? Ljung adopts a pragmatic convention: we treat the measured (or commanded) input values as the *actual* signal $u(t)$, and any deviation from the true physical stimulus is propagated through the linear plant dynamics and absorbed into the lumped output disturbance $v(t)$.

Ljung categorizes the physical origins of $v(t)$ into two primary classes:

1. **Measurement Noise**: Sensor imperfections, thermal/electronic noise, quantization, and calibration drift in the instruments measuring the output.
2. **Uncontrollable Inputs (Process Load Disturbances)**: Physical signals that act on the plant just like inputs, but are not controllable by the user:
   - *Aerospace Example*: An airplane's trajectory is governed by controllable rudder and aileron deflections $u(t)$, but also by unpredictable wind gusts and atmospheric turbulence.
   - *Thermal Example*: The temperature of a room is controlled by radiators $u(t)$, but is simultaneously perturbed by people moving in and out unpredictably (each person acts as an unmeasured heat source of $\approx 100\text{ W}$).

Depending on the physical mechanism, realizations of $v(t)$ can look drastically different, as contrasted in Figure 2.3 and Figure 2.4:

![[assets/fig_2_1_room_temperature.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 2.3 — Room Temperature)
> - **Axes**: The horizontal axis plots discrete sample time $t \in [0, 200]$, and the vertical axis plots room temperature (in $^{\circ}\text{C}$, ranging from $18^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $25^{\circ}\text{C}$).
> - **Physical Signature**: Around a nominal baseline of $22^{\circ}\text{C}$, we see small high-frequency sensor fluctuations punctuated by two large, structured, "classical-looking" deterministic transients: a sharp downward drop to $\approx 18.7^{\circ}\text{C}$ near $t = 15$ (e.g., a window briefly opened) followed by exponential thermal recovery, and a sustained upward step plateau to $\approx 24^{\circ}\text{C}$ between $t = 100$ and $t = 160$ (e.g., several people entering the room for a meeting and then leaving).

![[assets/fig_2_1_moisture_content_paper.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 2.4 — Moisture Content in Paper)
> - **Axes**: The horizontal axis plots sample index $t \in [0, 100]$, and the vertical axis plots percentage moisture content in paper during a paper-making process (ranging from $2.8\%$ to $3.1\%$).
> - **Physical Signature**: Unlike the isolated step/pulse events of Figure 2.3, this disturbance exhibits continuous, erratic, correlated stochastic fluctuations across the entire record, typical of turbulent pulp flow variation and sensor noise.

#### 2.2.4 Characterization of Disturbances as Filtered White Noise

The defining characteristic of any disturbance $v(t)$ is that **its value is not known beforehand**. However, past disturbance values $\{v(s), s \le t\}$ often carry information that allows us to make qualified predictions about future values $\{v(t+k), k \ge 1\}$.

While a complete probabilistic description would require specifying the full conditional joint probability density function of $\{v(t+k), k \ge 1\}$ given $\{v(s), s \le t\}$, doing so is far too cumbersome in practice. Instead, Ljung models $v(t)$ as the output of a causal linear time-invariant shaping filter driven by **white noise**:

$$
v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) e(t - k) \tag{2.9}
$$

**Underlying Assumptions on $\{e(t)\}$ and $\{h(k)\}$**:
- **White Noise $\{e(t)\}$**: A sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with a specified probability density function (PDF), zero mean ($E[e(t)] = 0$), and variance $\lambda$.
- **Monic Normalization ($h(0) = 1$)**: Without loss of generality, we normalize the leading filter coefficient to $h(0) = 1$, because any overall scale factor can be absorbed by adjusting the variance $\lambda$ of $e(t)$.

**How One Linear Equation Captures Both Classical Pulses and Continuous Noise**:
By varying the PDF of the driving white noise $e(t)$ in (2.9), we can synthesize both Figure 2.3-style and Figure 2.4-style disturbances:

1. **Sparse Impulse Noise (Compound Poisson / Bernoulli-Gaussian Model)**:
   Suppose $e(t)$ is zero almost all the time, and only occasionally takes a non-zero Gaussian value $r \in N(0, \gamma)$ with small probability $\mu \ll 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e(t) &= 0, \quad \text{with probability } 1 - \mu \\
e(t) &= r, \quad \text{with probability } \mu
\end{aligned} \tag{2.10}
$$

   Because $r \sim N(0, \gamma)$ has zero mean and variance $\gamma$, let us compute the mean and variance of $e(t)$ under (2.10):
   - **Mean**: $E[e(t)] = (1 - \mu) \cdot 0 + \mu \cdot E[r] = 0$.
   - **Variance**: $\lambda = E[e(t)^2] = (1 - \mu) \cdot 0^2 + \mu \cdot E[r^2] = \mu \gamma$.

   When $\mu$ is small, non-zero impulses occur at isolated random instants. Each impulse triggers a scaled copy $r \cdot h(t - t_0)$ of the shaping filter's impulse response! Thus, (2.10) generates "classical" disturbance patterns—steps, pulses, damped sinusoids, and ramps—occurring at random instants (compare Figure 2.5 with the room temperature in Figure 2.3).

![[assets/fig_2_1_realization_sparse_impulses.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 2.5 — Sparse Impulse Realization)
> - **Axes**: Sample time $t \in [0, 100]$ on the horizontal axis; disturbance amplitude $v(t) \in [-15, 15]$ on the vertical axis.
> - **Connection to Equations (2.9)–(2.10)**: A damped oscillatory filter $\{h(k)\}$ is driven by the sparse distribution (2.10). Only two non-zero innovations $e(t)$ occur in the 100-sample window (one positive impulse near $t \approx 7$, one negative impulse near $t \approx 62$). Between these rare events, $e(t) = 0$, so $v(t)$ simply traces out the deterministic impulse response $r \cdot h(t - t_0)$ and decays back to zero.

2. **Gaussian White Noise**:
   Conversely, suppose $e(t)$ is drawn at every sample from a normal distribution with zero mean and variance $\lambda$:

$$
e(t) \in N(0, \lambda) \tag{2.11}
$$

   Now, every single time step $t$ injects a random innovation, causing the overlapping impulse responses $h(k)e(t-k)$ to superpose continuously (Figure 2.6), which closely matches irregular measurement and process noise like the paper moisture record in Figure 2.4.

![[assets/fig_2_1_realization_gaussian_noise.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 2.6 — Gaussian White Noise Realization)
> - **Axes**: Sample time $t \in [0, 100]$ on the horizontal axis; disturbance amplitude $v(t) \in [-15, 15]$ on the vertical axis.
> - **Crucial Comparison with Figure 2.5**: Figure 2.6 uses the **exact same linear filter $\{h(k)\}$** and the **exact same second-order innovation variance $\lambda = \mu \gamma$** as Figure 2.5! Because second-order properties depend *only* on the mean and variance of $e(t)$, both Figure 2.5 and Figure 2.6 are realizations of stochastic processes with identical means and covariance functions, despite their dramatically different visual appearances.

> [!warning] Stochastic Process vs. Realization Notation
> Strictly speaking, $\{e(t)\}$ and $\{v(t)\}$ are **stochastic processes** (sequences of random variables defined on a probability space), whereas the specific time trajectory observed in an experiment and added to the plant output in Figure 2.2 is a single **realization** (sample path) of $\{v(t)\}$. Following standard system identification convention, Ljung uses the same symbol $v(t)$ for both the random variable and its observed realization whenever the meaning is clear from context.

#### 2.2.5 Mean, Covariance Function, and Stationarity of $v(t)$

Assume $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random variables with zero mean ($E[e(t)] = 0$) and constant variance $E[e(t)^2] = \lambda$. Because $e(t)$ and $e(s)$ are independent for $t \ne s$, their expectation factors as $E[e(t)e(s)] = E[e(t)]E[e(s)] = 0$. Using the Kronecker delta $\delta(k - s)$ (which equals $1$ when $k = s$ and $0$ otherwise), we can write the covariance of the white-noise source compactly as:

$$
E[e(t - k) e(t - \tau - s)] = \lambda \, \delta(k - \tau - s)
$$

**Step 1: Compute the Mean $E[v(t)]$**:
Taking the expectation of (2.9) and using linearity of expectation:

$$
E v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) E e(t - k) = 0 \tag{2.12}
$$

**Step 2: Derive the Auto-Covariance Function $R_v(\tau) = E[v(t) v(t - \tau)]$**:
Multiplying $v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) e(t - k)$ by $v(t - \tau) = \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(s) e(t - \tau - s)$ and taking expectations term-by-term yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E v(t) v(t - \tau) &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(k) h(s) E e(t - k) e(t - \tau - s) \\
&= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(k) h(s) \delta(k - \tau - s) \lambda \\
&= \lambda \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) h(k - \tau)
\end{aligned} \tag{2.13}
$$

- **Why the double sum collapses to a single sum**: For each fixed $k \ge 0$, the Kronecker delta $\delta(k - \tau - s)$ is non-zero *only* when $s = k - \tau$. Adopting the causality convention that $h(r) = 0$ for all negative lags $r < 0$, the inner sum over $s \ge 0$ picks out the single term $h(k - \tau)$ (which automatically vanishes if $k - \tau < 0$).
- **Stationarity**: Notice that the right-hand side of (2.13), $\lambda \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) h(k - \tau)$, depends *only* on the time lag $\tau$ and is completely independent of absolute time $t$. We therefore define the **covariance function** of the process $v$:

$$
R_v(\tau) = E v(t) v(t - \tau) \tag{2.14}
$$

Because both the mean $E v(t) = 0$ and the covariance $E v(t)v(t-\tau) = R_v(\tau)$ are independent of $t$, the stochastic process $\{v(t)\}$ is said to be (wide-sense or second-order) **stationary**. Its second-order properties are uniquely determined by the filter sequence $\{h(k)\}$ and the white-noise variance $\lambda$.

#### 2.2.6 Shift Operators ($q, q^{-1}$) and Transfer Operators ($G(q), H(q)$)

To write convolution sums like (2.8) and (2.9) in compact algebraic form, Ljung defines the **forward shift operator** $q$:

$$
q u(t) = u(t + 1)
$$

and its inverse, the **backward shift operator** (or unit delay operator) $q^{-1}$:

$$
q^{-1} u(t) = u(t - 1)
$$

Repeated application $k$ times yields $q^{-k} u(t) = u(t - k)$. Substituting this operator action into the discrete-time plant convolution (2.6) gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) \left( q^{-k} u(t) \right) \\
&= \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) q^{-k} \right] u(t) = G(q) u(t)
\end{aligned} \tag{2.15}
$$

where we define the **transfer operator** (or **transfer function**) of the linear deterministic system:

$$
G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) q^{-k} \tag{2.16}
$$

> [!tip] Why Write $G(q)$ Instead of $G(q^{-1})$?
> Although the series $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) q^{-k}$ is a power series in $q^{-1}$, Ljung writes $q$ as the argument of $G(q)$ so that time-domain operator expressions are in **formal agreement with $z$-transform and Fourier-transform definitions**. Strictly speaking, the term *transfer function* refers to the complex-valued $z$-transform of $\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$:
>
> $$
> G(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) z^{-k} \tag{2.17}
> $$
>
> where $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is a complex variable, whereas $G(q)$ is a time-domain operator mapping the input sequence $u^t$ to the output sequence $y^t$. In practice, Ljung uses the term "transfer function" interchangeably for both $G(z)$ and $G(q)$.

Similarly, defining the disturbance transfer operator:

$$
H(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) q^{-k} \tag{2.18}
$$

allows us to write the filtered white-noise disturbance model (2.9) as:

$$
v(t) = H(q) e(t) \tag{2.19}
$$

Combining (2.15) and (2.19) inside the additive disturbance relation (2.8) yields the **canonical basic description for a linear time-invariant system with additive disturbance**:

$$
\boxed{y(t) = G(q) u(t) + H(q) e(t)} \tag{2.20}
$$

where $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random variables with zero mean values and variances $\lambda$.

#### 2.2.7 Continuous-Time Representations & Approximate Discretization Formulas

In many physical modeling applications, the underlying dynamics are derived from Newton's laws or conservation principles as continuous-time differential equations. Let $G_c(s)$ denote the Laplace transform of the continuous-time impulse response $\{g(\tau)\}$ in (2.1). Then the Laplace transforms $Y(s)$ and $U(s)$ of the output and input satisfy:

$$
Y(s) = G_c(s) U(s) \tag{2.21}
$$

Introducing the **differentiation operator** $p$ defined by $p u(t) = \frac{d}{dt} u(t)$, we can write the time-domain differential equation in operator shorthand as:

$$
y(t) = G_c(p) u(t) \tag{2.22}
$$

If the continuous input trajectory $\{u(t)\}$ is known, (2.22) describes $y(t)$ at all continuous times $t \in \mathbb{R}$, and therefore also at the discrete sampling instants $t = 1, 2, \dots$ (by solving the differential equation numerically). Combining the continuous-time plant description (2.22) evaluated at the sampling instants with the discrete-time disturbance model (2.19) yields the hybrid representation:

$$
y(t) = G_c(p) u(t) + H(q) e(t), \quad t = 1, 2, \dots \tag{2.23}
$$

More commonly, we transform the continuous-time transfer function $G_c(p)$ directly into a discrete-time transfer operator $G_T(q)$ parameterized by the sampling interval $T$:

$$
G_c(p) \rightarrow G_T(q) \tag{2.24}
$$

- **Exact Zero-Order Hold Transformation**: When $u(t)$ is piecewise constant over $[kT, (k+1)T)$, $G_T(q) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g_T(\ell) q^{-\ell}$ is obtained *exactly* from (2.5) without approximation.
- **Approximate Difference Replacements**: Alternatively, one can approximate the differentiation operator $p u(t) = \dot{u}(t)$ by a finite difference:
  1. **Euler's Forward Difference Approximation**: Approximating $\dot{u}(t) \approx \frac{u(t+T) - u(t)}{T} = \frac{q - 1}{T} u(t)$ substitutes $p \approx \frac{q - 1}{T}$ into $G_c(p)$:

$$
G_T(q) \approx G_c\!\left(\frac{q - 1}{T}\right) \tag{2.25}
$$

  2. **Tustin's Formula (Bilinear Transform / Trapezoidal Rule)**: Approximating the integral trapezoidal rule between samples substitutes $p \approx \frac{2}{T} \frac{q - 1}{1 + q}$:

$$
G_T(q) \approx G_c\!\left(\frac{2}{T} \frac{q - 1}{1 + q}\right) \tag{2.26}
$$

#### 2.2.8 Poles, Zeros, Stability Hierarchy, and Monic Filters

Considering $G(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) z^{-k}$ in (2.17) as a complex-valued function of $z \in \mathbb{C}$:
- **Zeros ($\beta_i$)**: Complex numbers $\beta_i \in \mathbb{C}$ such that $G(\beta_i) = 0$ are called the **zeros** of the transfer function (or of the system).
- **Poles ($\alpha_i$)**: Complex numbers $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{C}$ at which $|G(z)| \to \infty$ are called the **poles** of the system. When $G(z)$ is a rational function of $z$ (a ratio of polynomials), the poles are precisely the roots (zeros) of the denominator polynomial.

Ljung establishes four rigorous structural definitions for discrete-time filters that are invoked repeatedly in convergence and asymptotic proofs throughout the book:

1. **Stability (BIBO Stability)**:
   The transfer function $G(q)$ (or "the system $G$" or "the filter $G$") is **stable** if its impulse response is absolutely summable ($\ell_1$-summable):

$$
G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) q^{-k}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty \tag{2.27}
$$

   - **Proof of Bounded-Input Bounded-Output (BIBO) Equivalence**: Suppose the input sequence $\{u(t)\}$ is bounded by $|u(t)| \le C$ for all $t$. Then the output $z(t) = G(q) u(t)$ satisfies:

$$
|z(t)| = \left| \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) u(t - k) \right| \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| \cdot |u(t - k)| \le C \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| = C' < \infty
$$

   - **Complex-Analytic Consequence (Region of Convergence)**: For any complex number $z \in \mathbb{C}$ on or outside the unit circle ($|z| \ge 1$), we have $|z|^{-k} \le 1$ for all $k \ge 1$, so:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left| g(k) z^{-k} \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| \cdot |z|^{-k} \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty
$$

     By the Weierstrass M-test, the Laurent expansion $G(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) z^{-k}$ converges absolutely and uniformly for all $|z| \ge 1$. Consequently, **$G(z)$ is analytic on and outside the unit circle**, which implies that **a stable causal discrete-time transfer function has no poles on or outside the unit circle** (all poles satisfy $|\alpha_i| < 1$).

2. **Uniformly Stable Family of Filters**:
   When analyzing parameterized model sets (Chapter 4), we consider families of filters indexed by a parameter $\alpha$ in an index set $\mathcal{A}$:

$$
G_\alpha(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g_\alpha(k) q^{-k}, \quad \alpha \in \mathcal{A} \tag{2.28}
$$

   Such a family is **uniformly stable** if all member impulse responses are dominated pointwise by a single common summable sequence $\{g(k)\}$:

$$
|g_\alpha(k)| \le g(k), \ \forall \alpha \in \mathcal{A}, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) < \infty \tag{2.29}
$$

3. **Strict Stability**:
   To bound the rate at which initial-condition transients decay (such as in Section 2.2), a slightly stronger condition than (2.27) is often required. The filter $G(q)$ is **strictly stable** if its lag-weighted impulse response is summable:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k |g(k)| < \infty \tag{2.30}
$$

   - **Why Rational Stability Implies Strict Stability**: If $G(q)$ is a *rational* transfer function, its impulse response decays geometrically as $|g(k)| \le M \rho^k$ where $\rho = \max_i |\alpha_i|$ is the magnitude of the dominant pole. If $G(q)$ is stable, then $\rho < 1$, and because the arithmetico-geometric series $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k \rho^k = \frac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2} < \infty$ converges whenever $\rho < 1$, **stability and strict stability are completely equivalent for all rational transfer functions**.

4. **Monic Filter**:
   A filter $H(q)$ is **monic** if its zeroth-lag coefficient (instantaneous feedthrough term) is equal to $1$ (or the identity matrix in the multivariable case):

$$
H(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k) q^{-k}, \quad h(0) = 1 \tag{2.31}
$$

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: Identical Second-Order Statistics vs. Dramatically Different Sample Paths**: Equations (2.10) and (2.11) demonstrate a profound limitation of second-order statistical descriptions (mean and covariance). A sparse Poisson-like impulse process (Figure 2.5) and a dense Gaussian process (Figure 2.6) can share the *exact same* mean ($0$), white-noise variance ($\lambda = \mu \gamma$), shaping filter $H(q)$, and auto-covariance function $R_v(\tau)$! Because standard least-squares and prediction-error methods rely primarily on second-order properties (or equivalently the power spectrum), they treat both processes identically using $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$.
> - **Notation Trap 1: Time Index $t$ vs. Continuous Time $t$**: From Equation (2.6) onward, Ljung normalizes the sampling interval to $T = 1$ and reuses the symbol $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ for the discrete sample index. Be careful when reading Section 2.1's optional subsection on continuous-time representations (Equations (2.21)–(2.26)), where $t$ briefly reverts to continuous time before being evaluated at discrete instants $t = 1, 2, \dots$ in (2.23).
> - **Notation Trap 2: Summation Lower Limits ($k = 1$ vs. $k = 0$)**: Notice that the plant transfer function $G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)q^{-k}$ starts at $k = 1$ (representing at least a one-sample delay from zero-order hold sampling, $g(0) = 0$), whereas the disturbance filter $H(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k)q^{-k}$ starts at $k = 0$ with $h(0) = 1$ (a **monic** filter). This distinction will become critical in Chapter 3 when computing optimal one-step-ahead predictors!
> - **Notation Trap 3: Argument of $G(q)$**: Even though $G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)q^{-k}$ is a series in negative powers of $q$ (i.e., positive powers of the backward shift $q^{-1}$), Ljung writes $G(q)$ rather than $G(q^{-1})$ so that replacing $q$ by the complex variable $z$ directly yields the standard $z$-transform $G(z)$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *Why is normalizing $h(0) = 1$ in $H(q)$ essential for unique model parameterization when $\lambda = \text{Var}(e(t))$ is also an unknown parameter?*
>   2. *Where do the poles of the Euler approximation (2.25) and Tustin's approximation (2.26) map the left half of the continuous $s$-plane ($\text{Re}(s) < 0$)?*
