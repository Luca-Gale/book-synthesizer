# 2.5 Multivariable Systems

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 2**: This section generalizes the time- and frequency-domain framework of Sections 2.1–2.4 from scalar single-input, single-output (SISO) systems to multivariable systems with $m$ inputs and $p$ outputs (MIMO), establishing how transfer matrices, outer-product covariance matrices, Hermitian spectral transformations, and multivariable spectral factorization behave when scalars become non-commuting matrices.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Real-world industrial and aerospace systems rarely operate as isolated single-loop channels. A chemical distillation column has multiple manipulated valves (reflux rate, reboiler heat duty, feed flow) that simultaneously affect multiple measured outputs (top and bottom product compositions, column pressure, tray temperatures). Similarly, an aircraft's elevator, aileron, and rudder inputs dynamically couple into pitch, roll, and yaw rates. When we excite one actuator, energy bleeds across multiple sensor channels—and random disturbances entering the plant are themselves spatially correlated across the outputs.

Ljung opens Section 2.5 with a crucial pedagogical insight that splits the transition from scalar (SISO) to multivariable ($p$-output, $m$-input MIMO) system identification into two fundamentally different levels of difficulty:

1. **The "Easy Part" (Linear Algebra & Bookkeeping)**: Extending time-domain impulse responses, covariance functions, and frequency-domain spectra from scalars to matrices is mostly a matter of disciplined matrix bookkeeping. Products of signals become **outer products** ($s(t)s^T(t-\tau)$), squared magnitudes $|G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_w(\omega)$ become **quadratic Hermitian forms** $G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_w(\omega)G^T(e^{-i\omega})$ because matrices do not commute, and scalar absolute values in stability definitions are replaced by matrix norms.
2. **The "Difficult Part" (Internal Structure & Parametrization of Multi-Output Models)**: While multiple-input, single-output (MISO) models ($p = 1, m > 1$) merely sum $m$ scalar transfer functions at a single output node and pose no deep structural obstacles, **multi-output models ($p > 1$)** possess a much richer internal dynamical structure. For instance, different output channels may share common poles (internal states) in ways that cannot be uniquely or parsimoniously represented by naively writing down independent rational transfer functions for every $(i, j)$ entry. Choosing canonical or overlapping state-space/polynomial parametrizations that are identifiable without redundancy is highly nontrivial (deferred to Chapter 4 and Appendix 4A).

Before tackling those deep parametrization challenges in Chapter 4, Section 2.5 completes the non-parametric foundation of Chapter 2 by showing how the foundational system equation, quasi-stationary covariances, power spectral densities, and spectral factorization carry over to $p$-dimensional output vectors and $m$-dimensional input vectors—and how the multivariable spectral formula naturally unlocks the scalar cross-spectrum formula when inputs and disturbances are correlated (e.g., under closed-loop feedback).

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 The Multivariable Linear System Description
We collect the $p$ scalar output signals into a $p$-dimensional column vector $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times 1}$ and the $m$ scalar input signals into an $m$-dimensional column vector $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 1}$. Similarly, the driving white-noise source is represented as a $p$-dimensional column vector $e(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times 1}$. The basic time-invariant linear system description looks formally identical to the scalar equation (2.20):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t) \tag{2.90}
$$

**Matrix Structure and Innovations Assumptions**:
1. **Input-to-Output Transfer Matrix $G(q)$**: $G(q)$ is a $p \times m$ transfer function matrix:

$$
G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) q^{-k}, \quad g(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}
$$

   Its $(i, j)$ entry, denoted $G_{ij}(q)$, is the scalar transfer function from **input number $j$** to **output number $i$**. Writing out the $i$-th component of $G(q)u(t)$ explicitly shows how all $m$ inputs superpose at output $i$:

$$
[G(q)u(t)]_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} G_{ij}(q) u_j(t), \quad i = 1, \dots, p
$$

2. **Disturbance Transfer Matrix $H(q)$**: $H(q)$ is a $p \times p$ square transfer function matrix that is **monic** (normalized so its zero-lag impulse response is the $p \times p$ identity matrix $I_p$):

$$
H(q) = I_p + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k) q^{-k}, \quad h(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}
$$

3. **Vector White Noise $\{e(t)\}$**: $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random $p$-dimensional vectors with zero mean ($E e(t) = 0$) and instantaneous spatial covariance matrix $\Lambda$:

$$
E e(t)e^T(t) = \Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}, \quad E e(t)e^T(s) = 0 \quad (t \neq s)
$$

> [!note] Why is $e(t)$ Chosen to Have Dimension $p$?
> Even if physical disturbances originate from many internal or external mechanisms, the observed disturbance vector $v(t) = y(t) - G(q)u(t)$ has $p$ components. By multivariable spectral factorization (Section 2.2.5 below), any full-rank $p$-dimensional stationary stochastic process $v(t)$ can be canonically generated by filtering a $p$-dimensional white-noise innovation vector $e(t)$ through a square $p \times p$ monic filter $H(q)$.

#### 2.2.2 Impulse Response Matrix Norms and Multivariable Stability
In the scalar case, stability and convergence bounds relied on the absolute values $|g(k)|$ and $|h(k)|$ of the impulse response coefficients (e.g., $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g(k)| < \infty$). For multivariable systems, $g(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}$ and $h(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ are matrices. All scalar stability definitions carry over verbatim once scalar absolute values are replaced by the **Frobenius matrix norm** (the Euclidean norm of all matrix entries):

$$
\|g(k)\| = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{p} \sum_{j=1}^{m} |g_{ij}(k)|^2 \right)^{1/2} \tag{2.91}
$$

Accordingly, the multivariable filter $G(q)$ is BIBO stable if and only if $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \|g(k)\| < \infty$, which is equivalent to requiring that every individual scalar channel $G_{ij}(q)$ be stable ($\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |g_{ij}(k)| < \infty$ for all $i, j$).

---

#### 2.2.3 Matrix Covariance and Cross-Covariance Functions
For quasi-stationary vector signals $s(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times 1}$ and $w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 1}$, multiplying two column vectors requires taking an **outer product** (post-multiplying the first column vector by the transpose of the second). The scalar covariance definitions (cf. (2.59)) generalize to:

$$
R_s(\tau) = \overline{E} s(t)s^T(t - \tau) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p} \tag{2.92}
$$

$$
R_{sw}(\tau) = \overline{E} s(t)w^T(t - \tau) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m} \tag{2.93}
$$

where the $(i, j)$ entry of $R_{sw}(\tau)$ is the scalar cross-covariance $\overline{E} s_i(t) w_j(t - \tau)$, and the size of these covariance matrices is measured using the Frobenius norm (2.91).

**Crucial Asymmetry of Matrix Covariances under Lag Reversal**:
Unlike scalar autocovariances where $R_s(-\tau) = R_s(\tau)$, shifting the time index $t' = t - \tau$ in the matrix definition reverses the order of the outer product and therefore introduces a matrix transpose:

$$
R_s(-\tau) = \overline{E} s(t)s^T(t + \tau) = \left[ \overline{E} s(t + \tau)s^T(t) \right]^T = R_s^T(\tau)
$$

$$
R_{sw}(-\tau) = \overline{E} s(t)w^T(t + \tau) = \left[ \overline{E} w(t + \tau)s^T(t) \right]^T = R_{ws}^T(\tau)
$$

#### 2.2.4 Spectral Density Matrices and Multivariable Linear Filtering (Theorem 2.2 Counterpart)
The Fourier transform definitions of spectra and cross-spectra remain formally unchanged from (2.65) and (2.68), operating entry-by-entry on the covariance matrices:

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega} \in \mathbb{C}^{p \times p}, \quad \Phi_{sw}(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty} R_{sw}(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega} \in \mathbb{C}^{p \times m}
$$

Because $R_s(-\tau) = R_s^T(\tau)$ and $R_{sw}(-\tau) = R_{ws}^T(\tau)$, the spectral density matrix $\Phi_s(\omega)$ is **Hermitian** ($\Phi_s^T(-\omega) = \Phi_s^*(\omega) = \Phi_s(\omega)$), and the cross-spectral matrix satisfies $\Phi_{sw}(\omega) = \Phi_{ws}^T(-\omega)$.

**Derivation of the Multivariable Spectral Transformation (2.94)**:
Suppose a quasi-stationary $m$-vector signal $w(t)$ with spectrum $\Phi_w(\omega) \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times m}$ is passed through a stable $p \times m$ linear system $G(q)$ to produce the $p$-vector signal $s(t) = G(q)w(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) w(t-k)$. Let us compute the auto-covariance matrix $R_s(\tau)$ step by step, paying strict attention to matrix multiplication order and transposes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_s(\tau) &= \overline{E} s(t) s^T(t - \tau) \\
&= \overline{E} \left( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) w(t - k) \right) \left( \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g(\ell) w(t - \tau - \ell) \right)^T \\
&= \overline{E} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g(k) w(t - k) w^T(t - \tau - \ell) g^T(\ell)
\end{aligned}
$$

where we used the matrix transpose identity $(g(\ell) w(t - \tau - \ell))^T = w^T(t - \tau - \ell) g^T(\ell)$. Moving the deterministic impulse response matrices $g(k)$ and $g^T(\ell)$ to the left and right of the expectation operator $\overline{E}$ yields:

$$
R_s(\tau) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g(k) \left[ \overline{E} w(t - k) w^T(t - \tau - \ell) \right] g^T(\ell) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g(k) R_w(\tau + \ell - k) g^T(\ell)
$$

Now take the discrete-time Fourier transform $\Phi_s(\omega) = \sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty} R_s(\tau) e^{-i\tau\omega}$ and factor the complex exponential into three parts via $\tau = (\tau + \ell - k) + k - \ell$, so $e^{-i\tau\omega} = e^{-ik\omega} e^{-i(\tau + \ell - k)\omega} e^{i\ell\omega}$:

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) = \left( \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-ik\omega} \right) \left( \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} R_w(m) e^{-im\omega} \right) \left( \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g^T(\ell) e^{i\ell\omega} \right)
$$

Recognizing the frequency response $G(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k) e^{-ik\omega}$ on the left and its conjugate transpose $G^T(e^{-i\omega}) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g^T(\ell) e^{i\ell\omega}$ on the right gives the multivariable counterpart of Theorem 2.2:

$$
\Phi_s(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_w(\omega) G^T(e^{-i\omega}) \tag{2.94}
$$

**Dimension Check**: $\underbrace{G(e^{i\omega})}_{p \times m} \underbrace{\Phi_w(\omega)}_{m \times m} \underbrace{G^T(e^{-i\omega})}_{m \times p} = \underbrace{\Phi_s(\omega)}_{p \times p}$.

#### 2.2.5 Correlated Input and Disturbance: The Block-Signal Trick and Equations (2.95)–(2.96)
A major payoff of the multivariable spectral transformation (2.94) is that it immediately determines how **cross-spectra** transform when two signals are summed—even in a scalar system where the input $u(t)$ and disturbance $w(t)$ are mutually correlated (such as when data is collected in closed-loop feedback, where controller action feeds $w(t)$ back into $u(t)$).

**Case 1: Jointly Quasi-Stationary Scalar Sequences $u(t)$ and $w(t)$ (Equation 2.95)**
Suppose we have a scalar output $y(t)$ generated by two **jointly quasi-stationary** scalar sequences $u(t)$ and $w(t)$:

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)w(t) = \begin{bmatrix} G(q) & H(q) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ w(t) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Here we have reformulated the sum of two scalar channels as a $1 \times 2$ MISO transfer matrix $\tilde{G}(q) = \begin{bmatrix} G(q) & H(q) \end{bmatrix}$ acting on the $2 \times 1$ stacked input vector $\tilde{w}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t) & w(t) \end{bmatrix}^T$.
The covariance matrix of the stacked vector $\tilde{w}(t)$ at lag $\tau$ is the $2 \times 2$ block matrix:

$$
R_{\tilde{w}}(\tau) = \overline{E} \begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ w(t) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u(t-\tau) & w(t-\tau) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R_u(\tau) & R_{uw}(\tau) \\ R_{wu}(\tau) & R_w(\tau) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Taking the Fourier transform yields the $2 \times 2$ joint spectral density matrix, where we use $\Phi_{wu}(\omega) = \Phi_{uw}(-\omega)$ for scalar real signals:

$$
\Phi_{\tilde{w}}(\omega) = \begin{bmatrix} \Phi_u(\omega) & \Phi_{uw}(\omega) \\ \Phi_{uw}(-\omega) & \Phi_w(\omega) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Applying the multivariable formula (2.94) with $\tilde{G}(e^{i\omega}) = \begin{bmatrix} G(e^{i\omega}) & H(e^{i\omega}) \end{bmatrix}$ and $\tilde{G}^T(e^{-i\omega}) = \begin{bmatrix} G(e^{-i\omega}) \\ H(e^{-i\omega}) \end{bmatrix}$ gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_y(\omega) &= \begin{bmatrix} G(e^{i\omega}) & H(e^{i\omega}) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Phi_u(\omega) & \Phi_{uw}(\omega) \\ \Phi_{uw}(-\omega) & \Phi_w(\omega) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} G(e^{-i\omega}) \\ H(e^{-i\omega}) \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \left|G(e^{i\omega})\right|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \left|H(e^{i\omega})\right|^2 \Phi_w(\omega) \\
&\quad + G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uw}(\omega)H(e^{-i\omega}) + H(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uw}(-\omega)G(e^{-i\omega}) \\
&= \left|G(e^{i\omega})\right|^2 \Phi_u(\omega) + \left|H(e^{i\omega})\right|^2 \Phi_w(\omega) \\
&\quad + 2\operatorname{Re}\left( G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uw}(\omega)H(e^{-i\omega}) \right)
\end{aligned} \tag{2.95}
$$

**Why the Cross-Terms Combine into $2\operatorname{Re}(\cdot)$**:
Because $g(k)$, $h(k)$, and $R_{uw}(\tau)$ are real-valued sequences, evaluating their Fourier transforms at $-\omega$ is identical to taking their complex conjugates: $G(e^{-i\omega}) = \overline{G(e^{i\omega})}$, $H(e^{-i\omega}) = \overline{H(e^{i\omega})}$, and $\Phi_{uw}(-\omega) = \overline{\Phi_{uw}(\omega)}$. Consequently, the second cross-term $H(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uw}(-\omega)G(e^{-i\omega})$ is the exact complex conjugate of the first cross-term $G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uw}(\omega)H(e^{-i\omega})$, and for any complex number $z$, $z + \bar{z} = 2\operatorname{Re}(z)$.

**Case 2: Uncorrelated Multivariable Input $u(t)$ and White Noise $e(t)$ (Equation 2.96)**
When $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ is the full $p$-output, $m$-input MIMO system (2.90) and the input $\{u(t)\}$ is independent of (uncorrelated with) the white noise $\{e(t)\}$ ($\Phi_{ue}(\omega) = 0$), the cross-terms vanish and the white-noise spectrum is $\Phi_e(\omega) = \Lambda$. The multivariable counterpart of the Corollary to Theorem 2.2 (cf. (2.72)) then reads:

$$
\Phi_y(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega}) \Phi_u(\omega) G^T(e^{-i\omega}) + H(e^{i\omega}) \Lambda H^T(e^{-i\omega}) \tag{2.96}
$$

#### 2.2.6 Multivariable Spectral Factorization and Ergodicity (Theorem 2.3)
Finally, Ljung states how the two remaining theoretical pillars of Chapter 2—Spectral Factorization (Theorem 2.1) and Ergodicity of sample covariances (Theorem 2.3)—extend to the $p$-output multivariable setting:

1. **Multivariable Spectral Factorization (Rozanov, 1967, Theorem 10.1)**:
   - **Assumptions**: Suppose $\Phi_v(\omega)$ is a $p \times p$ spectral density matrix that is **strictly positive definite** ($\Phi_v(\omega) > 0$) for all $\omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$ and whose entries are rational functions of $\cos\omega$ (or $e^{i\omega}$).
   - **Conclusion**: Then there exists a $p \times p$ **monic** matrix function $H(z)$ ($H(\infty) = I_p$) whose entries are rational functions of $z$ (or $z^{-1}$), and a $p \times p$ positive definite covariance matrix $\Lambda$, such that:

$$
\Phi_v(\omega) = H(e^{i\omega}) \Lambda H^T(e^{-i\omega})
$$

     and the scalar rational function $\det H(z)$ has **no poles and no zeros on or outside the unit circle** ($|z| \ge 1$).
   - **Significance of the Determinant Condition**: In a scalar transfer function $H(z)$, stability and minimum-phase invertibility mean the poles and zeros of $H(z)$ lie strictly inside the unit circle ($|z| < 1$). For a $p \times p$ matrix $H(z)$, the inverse is given by Cramer's rule as $H^{-1}(z) = \frac{1}{\det H(z)} \operatorname{adj}(H(z))$. Requiring that $\det H(z)$ has neither poles nor zeros in $|z| \ge 1$ guarantees that **both** the forward shaping filter $H(q)$ and the whitening (prediction-error) inverse filter $H^{-1}(q)$ are causal and exponentially stable!

2. **Multivariable Ergodicity (Theorem 2.3)**:
   The formulation of Theorem 2.3 (convergence of finite-sample empirical covariances $\hat{R}_s^N(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N s(t)s^T(t-\tau)$ to the theoretical ensemble/quasi-stationary covariance matrix $R_s(\tau)$ as $N \to \infty$) carries over to multivariable signals without any changes—in fact, Ljung's proof in Appendix 2B is written directly for the multivariable case.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 (Why MISO Is "Easy" While Multi-Output MIMO Is "Hard")**: Pay close attention to Ljung's distinction at the top of page 44. Adding multiple inputs to a single output ($p = 1, m > 1$) only turns $G(q)$ into a $1 \times m$ row vector $G(q) = \begin{bmatrix} G_1(q) & \cdots & G_m(q) \end{bmatrix}$, which means each input channel can still be parametrized straightforwardly. The true structural complexity arises only when $p > 1$ (multiple outputs), because multiple outputs observe the *same* underlying state vector, forcing the rows of $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ to share common dynamics in subtle ways (explored in Appendix 4A).
> - **Leap 2 (The Stacked Block-Signal Trick for Cross-Spectra)**: Notice how equation (2.95) is derived on page 45: instead of doing a separate time-domain derivation for correlated scalar signals $u(t)$ and $w(t)$, Ljung stacks them into a $2 \times 1$ vector $\begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ w(t) \end{bmatrix}$ passed through the $1 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix} G(q) & H(q) \end{bmatrix}$ and applies the multivariable Theorem 2.2 formula (2.94) in one line! This cross-spectral term $2\operatorname{Re}(G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_{uw}(\omega)H(e^{-i\omega}))$ will become crucial in later chapters whenever we analyze **closed-loop identification**, where feedback correlates $u(t)$ with the disturbance $w(t)$.
> - **Notation Trap 1 (Matrix Ordering in Covariances and Spectra)**: Never commute matrices! In covariance definitions $R_{sw}(\tau) = \overline{E}s(t)w^T(t-\tau)$, the first subscript $s$ is the column vector on the left and the second subscript $w$ is the transposed row vector on the right. Similarly, in the spectral transformation $\Phi_s(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_w(\omega)G^T(e^{-i\omega})$, the transfer matrix $G(e^{i\omega})$ always pre-multiplies $\Phi_w(\omega)$ on the left, while its conjugate transpose $G^T(e^{-i\omega})$ post-multiplies on the right.
> - **Notation Trap 2 ($G_{ij}(q)$ Indexing Convention)**: Consistent with standard linear algebra ($y_i = \sum_j G_{ij} u_j$), the first index $i$ of $G_{ij}(q)$ denotes the **output** channel ($i \in \{1, \dots, p\}$) and the second index $j$ denotes the **input** channel ($j \in \{1, \dots, m\}$). Do not confuse "from input $j$ to output $i$" with $G_{ji}(q)$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. If $y(t)$ is $p \times 1$ and $u(t)$ is $m \times 1$, why must $G^T(e^{-i\omega})$ appear on the right-hand side of $\Phi_s(\omega) = G(e^{i\omega})\Phi_w(\omega)G^T(e^{-i\omega})$ even just to make the matrix dimensions compatible?
>   2. Why does the multivariable spectral factorization theorem specify the pole/zero condition on the scalar determinant $\det H(z)$ rather than on the individual matrix entries $H_{ij}(z)$?
