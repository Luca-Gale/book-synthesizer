# 5.5 Nonlinear Black-Box Models: Neural Networks, Wavelets and Classical Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: Having established the universal coordinate-expansion framework $g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k g_k(\varphi)$ with dilated and translated "mother basis functions" $\kappa(\cdot)$ in Section 5.4, Section 5.5 demonstrates that almost every celebrated nonlinear black-box architecture across machine learning, signal processing, and classical statistics—**Feedforward Sigmoid Neural Networks**, **Radial Basis Function (RBF) Networks**, **Wavelet Networks & Orthonormal Wavelets**, **Nadaraya-Watson Kernel Estimators**, **Nearest-Neighbor Interpolators**, and **B-Splines**—is simply a specific combination of a multivariable construction rule (ridge vs. radial vs. tensor-product) and a mother function choice (global sigmoid vs. local bell/indicator/polynomial). This demystifies competing disciplinary vocabularies before Section 5.6 extends the exact same basis-expansion lens to rule-based **Fuzzy Models**.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

### 1.1 One Equation to Rule Them All: Cutting Through Disciplinary Jargon
When an engineer enters the literature on nonlinear system identification, they are immediately confronted by what appear to be radically different paradigms originating from separate research communities:
- **Connectionist / Machine Learning Community**: Talks about *artificial neurons*, *synaptic weights*, *activation thresholds*, and *hidden layers* (Sigmoid Neural Networks and Radial Basis Function Networks).
- **Harmonic Analysis & Signal Processing Community**: Talks about *time-scale multiresolution analysis*, *dyadic dilations*, and *mother wavelets* (Wavelet Decompositions and Wavelet Networks).
- **Nonparametric Statistics & Classical Approximation Community**: Talks about *Parzen-Rosenblatt / Nadaraya-Watson kernels*, *Epanechnikov windows*, *Voronoi tessellations / nearest-neighbor lookups*, and *piecewise polynomial B-splines*.

Ljung's central pedagogical message in Section 5.5 is that **mathematically and algorithmically, all of these models are variations of the exact same function expansion** introduced in Equations (5.32) and (5.43) of Section 5.4:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k g_k(\varphi, \beta_k, \gamma_k)
$$

Every model family reviewed in Section 5.5 is completely specified by answering just **three structural design questions** from Section 5.4:
1. **What is the scalar "mother basis function" $\kappa(x)$?** Is it a *global* step/sigmoid function $\sigma(x)$, a *local* smooth bell function $e^{-x^2}$, a *local* compactly supported parabolic window $(1 - x^2)_+$, a discontinuous boxcar indicator $\mathbf{1}_{\{|x| \le 1\}}$, or a piecewise polynomial spline?
2. **How is the scalar mother function lifted to the $d$-dimensional regressor vector $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}^d$?** Does it project $\varphi$ onto a direction vector via the **ridge construction** (5.42), measure weighted Euclidean/hypercube distance from a center via the **radial construction** (5.40), or take products of 1D functions along each coordinate axis via **tensor products**?
3. **How are the scale ($\beta_k$) and location ($\gamma_k$) parameters determined?** Are they *adaptively tuned* alongside the linear output weights $\alpha_k$ via nonlinear optimization (as in neural networks and wavelet networks), *fixed on a deterministic dyadic/regular grid* (as in orthonormal wavelets and B-splines), or *anchored directly at the observed historical regressor data points* $\gamma_k = \varphi(k)$ (as in kernel estimators and nearest-neighbor interpolation)?

| Nonlinear Black-Box Family | Mother Function $\kappa(x)$ | Multivariable Lifting ($d > 1$) | Scale $\beta_k$ & Location $\gamma_k$ Policy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sigmoid Neural Network** | Global sigmoid $\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ (5.38) | **Ridge** (5.42): $\kappa(\beta_k^T \varphi - \gamma_k)$ | **Adaptively optimized** in $\theta$ |
| **RBF / Wavelet Network** | Local Gaussian bell $e^{-x^2}$ (5.36) or wavelet $\psi(x)$ | **Radial** (5.40): $\kappa(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k})$ | **Adaptively optimized** in $\theta$ |
| **Orthonormal Wavelets** | Local mother wavelet $\psi(x)$ | **Tensor products** of scalar wavelets | **Fixed dyadic grid**: $\beta_j = 2^j$, $\gamma_{j,k} = 2^{-j}k$ |
| **Kernel Estimators** | Local bell / Epanechnikov $(1-x^2)_+$ (5.50) | **Radial** with uniform bandwidth $h = 1/\beta$ | **Fixed at data points** $\gamma_k$, tuned global scalar $h$ |
| **Nearest Neighbor** | Local hypercube indicator (5.35) | **Radial** hypercube $\kappa(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k})$ | **Anchored at all past data** $\gamma_k = \varphi(k) \in Z^N$ |
| **B-Splines** | Local piecewise polynomial of degree $m$ | **Tensor products** / local grid cells | **Fixed knot sequence** with $C^{m-1}$ continuity |

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

| Symbol | Meaning / Physical Interpretation | Space / Dimensions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\varphi$ or $\varphi(t)$ | Regression vector containing past inputs, outputs, or internal model states | $\mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $d$ | Dimension of the regression vector $\varphi$ (number of regressors) | $\mathbb{Z}^+$ |
| $g(\varphi, \theta)$ | Nonlinear predictor function mapping regressor $\varphi$ to predicted output $\hat{y}(t \mid \theta)$ | $\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ |
| $g_k(\varphi)$ | The $k$-th parameterized basis function in the expansion (5.32) | $\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ |
| $\kappa(\cdot)$ | Scalar "mother basis function" generating the basis family via dilation and translation | $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ |
| $\psi(\cdot)$ | Standard notation in the wavelet literature for a zero-mean oscillatory mother wavelet (corresponding to Ljung's $\kappa$) | $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ |
| $\alpha_k$ or $\alpha_{j,k}$ | Linear coordinate (output weight) scaling the $k$-th (or $(j,k)$-th) basis function | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\beta_k$ | Scale / dilation parameter (scalar bandwidth, vector of directional weights, or positive-definite metric matrix) | $\mathbb{R}^+$, $\mathbb{R}^d$, or $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ |
| $\gamma_k$ | Location / translation parameter (scalar threshold or center point in regressor space) | $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $j, k$ | Dyadic scale index ($j$) and integer translation index ($k$) in wavelet decompositions | $\mathbb{Z}^+$ (or $\mathbb{Z}$) |
| $g_{j,k}(\varphi)$ | Doubly-indexed wavelet basis function at resolution level $j$ and spatial shift $k$ | $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ |
| $h$ | Bandwidth (smoothing window width) parameter in nonparametric kernel estimators ($h = 1/\beta$) | $\mathbb{R}^+$ |
| $Z^N$ | Historical estimation dataset $\{y(t), \varphi(t)\}_{t=1}^N$ of $N$ observed input-output samples | $(\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^d)^N$ |
| $\varphi^*$ | Newly encountered query regression vector at which we wish to evaluate the predictor | $\mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $\varphi^\dagger$ | The historical regression vector $\varphi(t) \in Z^N$ that lies closest in distance to the query point $\varphi^*$ | $\mathbb{R}^d$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Neural Networks: Sigmoid Feedforward & Radial Basis Architectures
Although inspired historically by biological neural synapses in animals, an **Artificial Neural Network (ANN)** in system identification is strictly a specific parameterization of the general function expansion (5.32):

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k g_k(\varphi)
$$

Depending on how the scalar mother function $\kappa(\cdot)$ and the multivariable construction are paired, two primary single-hidden-layer feedforward network classes emerge:

1. **One-Hidden-Layer Feedforward Sigmoid Neural Networks**:
   Combining the linear expansion (5.32) with:
   - The **ridge basis construction** (5.42), which projects $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}^d$ onto a weight vector $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and shifts by scalar threshold $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = \kappa(\beta_k^T \varphi - \gamma_k)
$$

   - And the **sigmoid mother function** (5.38):

$$
\kappa(x) = \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}
$$

   yields the classical **one-hidden-layer feedforward sigmoid neural network**:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k \frac{1}{1 + \exp\bigl(-(\beta_k^T \varphi - \gamma_k)\bigr)}
$$

   Here, each basis term $g_k(\varphi)$ represents a single hidden "neuron" whose activation is constant along $(d-1)$-dimensional hyperplanes orthogonal to $\beta_k$ and transitions smoothly from $0$ to $1$ across the hyperplane $\beta_k^T \varphi = \gamma_k$.

2. **Wavelet Networks and Radial Basis Function (RBF) Neural Networks**:
   Combining the linear expansion (5.32) with:
   - The **radial basis construction** (5.40), which measures the $\beta_k$-weighted distance from a center vector $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = \kappa\bigl(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k}\bigr)
$$

   - And the **Gaussian bell-type local mother function** (5.36), $\kappa(x) = e^{-x^2}$ (or a localized radial wavelet function),
   produces both **Wavelet Networks** (Zhang and Benveniste, 1992) and **Radial Basis Neural Networks** (Poggio and Girosi, 1990). Unlike ridge networks, each hidden unit here acts as a localized receptive field centered at $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with ellipsoidal shape governed by the metric $\beta_k$.

#### 2.2.2 Wavelets & Multiresolution Orthonormal Expansions
While wavelet networks treat the scales $\beta_k$ and centers $\gamma_k$ as continuous free parameters optimized via gradient descent, classical **Wavelet Decomposition** fixes the scales and locations on a structured **dyadic grid** to achieve a *multiresolution local basis*.

##### Derivation of the Dyadic Wavelet Basis — Equation (5.48)
In the wavelet literature, the localized oscillatory mother basis function $\kappa(\cdot)$ is called the **mother wavelet** and is conventionally denoted by $\psi(\cdot)$ rather than $\kappa(\cdot)$.
1. Start from the general one-dimensional scale-and-location parameterization (5.34) from Section 5.4:

$$
g_i(\varphi) = \kappa\bigl(\beta_i(\varphi - \gamma_i)\bigr)
$$

2. Instead of using a single flat index $i = 1, \dots, n$, **doubly index** the basis expansion (5.32) by a **scale (resolution) index $j$** and a **spatial translation index $k$**, where $j, k$ are positive integers (or integers spanning the domain of interest).
3. Restrict the dilation parameter $\beta_j$ to powers of two (**dyadic scaling**) and the translation parameter $\gamma_k$ to an integer grid scaled inversely by $2^j$:

$$
\beta_j = 2^j, \qquad \gamma_k = 2^{-j} k
$$

4. Substituting $\beta_j = 2^j$ and $\gamma_k = 2^{-j} k$ directly into $\kappa\bigl(\beta_j(\varphi - \gamma_k)\bigr)$ gives:

$$
\kappa\bigl(2^j(\varphi - 2^{-j} k)\bigr) = \kappa(2^j \varphi - k)
$$

This yields Ljung's **Equation (5.48)** for the scalar wavelet basis functions:

$$
g_{j,k}(\varphi) = \kappa(2^j \varphi - k), \qquad j, k \text{ positive integers} \tag{5.48}
$$

With this doubly-indexed family, the function expansion (5.32) takes the multiresolution form:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{j} \sum_{k} \alpha_{j,k} g_{j,k}(\varphi) = \sum_{j} \sum_{k} \alpha_{j,k} \kappa(2^j \varphi - k)
$$

##### Structural Advantages & The Curse of Dimensionality in Wavelets
- **Multiresolution Capability**: Compared to the simple piecewise-constant boxcar approximation in (5.35) (which tiles the domain only once at a single fixed grid width), the dyadic wavelet expansion (5.48) **multiply covers** the regressor intervals using basis functions of different resolutions ($2^j$). Coarse scales (small $j$) capture broad, low-frequency trends across wide intervals, while fine scales (large $j$) zoom in on sharp local transients of width $\propto 2^{-j}$ centered at $\varphi = 2^{-j}k$.
- **Orthonormality & Fast Coordinate Computation**: Under **Assumption 5.5.1 (Admissible Orthonormal Mother Wavelet)**—that the mother wavelet $\kappa$ (or $\psi$) is specifically constructed (e.g., Haar, Daubechies, Meyer, or Battle-Lemarié wavelets) so that the dyadic family $\{2^{j/2}\kappa(2^j\varphi - k)\}_{j,k}$ forms an **orthonormal basis** in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$—the coordinates $\alpha_{j,k}$ decouple completely and can be computed directly via inner products (or the Fast Wavelet Transform) without solving ill-conditioned nonlinear optimization problems.
- **Multivariable Extension & Dimensionality Limit ($d \lesssim 3$)**: To extend scalar wavelets (5.48) to a $d$-dimensional regression vector $\varphi = [\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_d]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$, one typically constructs **tensor products** of scalar wavelet functions along each coordinate axis:

$$
g_{\mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}}(\varphi) = \prod_{m=1}^{d} \kappa(2^{j_m} \varphi_m - k_m)
$$

  (or separable multi-dimensional wavelet constructions). However, if each coordinate requires $M$ basis functions, a tensor-product grid requires $M^d$ basis functions. Because the computational and memory storage burden grows exponentially with the regressor dimension $d$ (the **curse of dimensionality**), Ljung emphasizes that **in practice, orthonormal wavelet expansions are limited to systems with very few regressors ($d \le 3$)**.

#### 2.2.3 "Classical" Nonlinear Black-Box Models

Before the rise of neural networks and wavelets, statistics and numerical analysis developed several foundational local basis methods that fit seamlessly into the exact same expansion (5.32)/(5.43).

##### 1. Kernel Estimators (Nadaraya-Watson Nonparametric Regression) — Equations (5.49) & (5.50)
Originally introduced by Nadaraya (1964) and Watson (1969), **Kernel Estimators** construct a local basis expansion using a bell-shaped **kernel function** $\kappa(\cdot)$ centered at given points $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ in the space of the regression vector $\varphi$, sharing a **single uniform scale (bandwidth) parameter $h > 0$**:

$$
g(\varphi) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k \kappa\left( \left\| \frac{\varphi - \gamma_k}{h} \right\| \right) \tag{5.49}
$$

- **Connection to Section 5.4**: Comparing (5.49) with the general expansion (5.32), (5.34), and (5.40), a kernel estimator is strictly the radial local basis expansion with **one fixed scalar scale parameter** $\beta_k = \beta = 1/h$ shared across all $n$ basis functions, where $h > 0$ is a small positive bandwidth number tuned to the smoothness of the problem and $\gamma_k$ are prescribed anchor points in regressor space (typically the observed historical regressors $\varphi(k)$).
- **The Epanechnikov Kernel — Equation (5.50)**: While Gaussian bells $\kappa(x) = e^{-x^2}$ are often used, a celebrated compactly supported choice in nonparametric statistics (optimal in a minimum asymptotic integrated squared error sense) is the **Epanechnikov kernel**:

$$
\kappa(x) = \begin{cases} 1 - x^2 & \text{for } |x| < 1 \\ 0 & \text{for } |x| \ge 1 \end{cases} \tag{5.50}
$$

  Because $\kappa(x) = 0$ whenever $|x| \ge 1$, the $k$-th term in (5.49) vanishes completely whenever $\|\varphi - \gamma_k\| \ge h$. Thus, evaluating $g(\varphi)$ only averages over data centers $\gamma_k$ lying strictly inside a ball of radius $h$ around $\varphi$.

##### 2. Nearest Neighbors or Interpolation as a Hypercube Basis Expansion
The **Nearest Neighbor** approach to system identification has immediate intuitive appeal:
- Given $N$ historical input-output pairs $Z^N = \{\bigl(y(1), \varphi(1)\bigr), \dots, \bigl(y(N), \varphi(N)\bigr)\}$ and a newly encountered query regressor $\varphi^*$, we search the past dataset $Z^N$ to find the historical regression vector $\varphi(t) = \varphi^\dagger$ that is **closest** to $\varphi^*$:

$$
\varphi^\dagger = \arg\min_{\varphi(t) \in Z^N} \|\varphi^* - \varphi(t)\|
$$

- We then predict the output for $\varphi^*$ by directly associating it with the historical measurement $y(t)$ corresponding to $\varphi(t) = \varphi^\dagger$:

$$
\hat{y}(\varphi^*) = y(t) \quad \text{where } \varphi(t) = \varphi^\dagger
$$

> [!tip] How Nearest-Neighbor Lookup Casts Exactly into Equation (5.43)
> At first glance, searching a table of past data for the closest point $\varphi^\dagger$ looks like a procedural algorithm rather than a basis function expansion $g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k \kappa(\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k))$. Ljung shows that nearest-neighbor lookup is **mathematically identical** to (5.43) under four exact choices:
> 1. **Number of basis terms ($n = N$)**: Set the number of terms in the expansion equal to the total number of historical data points in $Z^N$.
> 2. **Mother function $\kappa$**: Choose $\kappa$ as the **unit indicator (boxcar) function** (5.35), generalized to a $d$-dimensional hypercube (or Voronoi cell) via the radial construction (5.40): $\kappa\bigl(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k}\bigr) \in \{0, 1\}$.
> 3. **Centers ($\gamma_k$) & Scales ($\beta_k$)**: Place the center of the $k$-th cube directly at the $k$-th observed data point, $\gamma_k = \varphi(k) \in Z^N$, and choose the local scale parameters $\beta_k$ so that the cells $\kappa\bigl(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k}\bigr)$ partition the regressor space tightly—meaning every query point $\varphi^*$ falls inside the support of **exactly one** cell (the cell of its closest historical neighbor $\varphi(t) = \varphi^\dagger$, where $\kappa(\beta_t(\varphi^* - \gamma_t)) = 1$, while $\kappa(\beta_k(\varphi^* - \gamma_k)) = 0$ for all $k \ne t$).
> 4. **Linear Coordinates ($\alpha_k$)**: Set the coordinate weight directly to the observed output at that data point: $\alpha_k = y(k)$.
>
> Substituting these four choices into (5.43) evaluates the predictor at $\varphi^*$ as:
>
> $$
> g(\varphi^*, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \alpha_k \kappa\bigl(\beta_k(\varphi^* - \gamma_k)\bigr) = \alpha_t \cdot 1 + \sum_{k \ne t} \alpha_k \cdot 0 = \alpha_t = y(t)
> $$
>
> which reproduces the exact output $y(t)$ of the closest historical regressor $\varphi(t) = \varphi^\dagger$!

##### 3. B-Splines (Smooth Piecewise Polynomial Local Bases)
Whereas nearest-neighbor interpolation uses discontinuous $0$-th degree piecewise-constant indicator functions (5.35), **B-Splines** (De Boor, 1978; Schumaker, 1981) are **local basis functions consisting of piecewise polynomials** defined over a grid of knots:
- **Order of Smoothness**: At the knot boundaries where adjacent polynomial pieces meet, B-splines enforce **continuous derivatives up to a prescribed order** determined by the polynomial degree (specifically, a B-spline of polynomial degree $m$ has continuous derivatives up to order $m - 1$, i.e., $C^{m-1}$ continuity, at simple knots).
- **Computational Efficiency**: Because each B-spline basis function $g_k(\varphi)$ has **compact local support** (vanishing outside a small number of adjacent knot intervals) and is evaluated via simple polynomial arithmetic (Cox–de Boor recursion), B-splines can be made as smooth as desired while remaining computationally very fast—explaining their widespread adoption in classical interpolation and low-dimensional nonlinear identification.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Conceptual Leap 1 — Parametric vs. Nonparametric Models Share the Same Expansion (5.32)**: It is easy to think of "neural networks" (parametric models with a fixed number of hidden units $n \ll N$) and "kernel estimators / nearest-neighbor lookup" (nonparametric memory-based methods where $n = N$ grows with sample size) as opposite worlds. In Section 5.5, Ljung shows they obey the *exact same algebraic expansion* (5.32): the only difference is whether $\gamma_k, \beta_k$ are treated as a small set of estimated parameters $\theta$ or anchored directly to the historical data points $\varphi(k) \in Z^N$.
> - **Conceptual Leap 2 — Why Orthonormal Wavelets Hit a Wall at $d \approx 3$ While Neural Networks Do Not**: Orthonormal wavelets (5.48) enjoy linear least-squares / inner-product coordinate computation because their dilations $\beta_j = 2^j$ and translations $\gamma_k = 2^{-j}k$ sit on a fixed grid. However, tiling a $d$-dimensional space with a fixed tensor-product grid requires $O(M^d)$ basis functions, making standard orthonormal wavelets impractical for $d > 3$. Feedforward ridge neural networks and adaptive wavelet networks avoid this exponential grid explosion by *optimizing* the directions/centers $\beta_k, \gamma_k$ in $\theta$ so that basis functions are placed only where the data actually live—at the price of turning parameter estimation into a non-convex optimization problem.
> - **Notation Trap 1 — Wavelet Literature $\psi$ vs. Ljung's Universal Mother Function $\kappa$**: In standard wavelet textbooks, $\varphi(x)$ denotes the "father wavelet" (scaling function) and $\psi(x)$ denotes the "mother wavelet." Because Ljung already uses $\varphi$ for the **regression vector** $\varphi(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, he retains $\kappa(\cdot)$ for the mother wavelet in Equation (5.48): $g_{j,k}(\varphi) = \kappa(2^j \varphi - k)$. Do not confuse the regressor argument $\varphi$ in Ljung's $\kappa(2^j \varphi - k)$ with the scaling function $\varphi(x)$ found in wavelet papers!
> - **Notation Trap 2 — Bandwidth $h$ vs. Scale $\beta$**: In Section 5.4, $\beta$ multiplies $(\varphi - \gamma_k)$, so a **larger $\beta$** means a **narrower, more localized** basis function. In Equation (5.49) for kernel estimators, statistical convention places the bandwidth $h = 1/\beta$ in the **denominator**: $\kappa(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\| / h)$. Thus, a **smaller $h$** corresponds to a **larger $\beta$** (sharper localization, lower bias, higher variance).
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. How does changing the mother function in a radial expansion from an indicator boxcar (5.35) to an Epanechnikov kernel (5.50) smooth the discontinuous Voronoi jumps of a Nearest-Neighbor predictor into a Nadaraya-Watson kernel smoother?
>   2. When we move to Section 5.6 (Fuzzy Models), how will linguistic "IF–THEN" rules and fuzzy membership functions map onto the exact same basis expansion $\sum_k \alpha_k g_k(\varphi)$?
