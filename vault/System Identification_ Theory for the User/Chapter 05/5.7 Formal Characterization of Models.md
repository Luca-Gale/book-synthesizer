# 5.7 Formal Characterization of Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: This section (*) provides the rigorous mathematical counterparty to Section 4.5 for general, possibly time-varying and nonlinear models. It establishes that any dynamical model is fundamentally a sequence of one-step-ahead predictor mappings $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ (optionally augmented with a prediction-error probability distribution or covariance), generalizes model sets $\mathcal{M}^*$ and parameterized model structures $\mathcal{M}$, and proves via **Lemma 5.1** that specifying a predictor plus a conditional error density is *no more and no less general* than specifying the full unstructured joint probability density of the observed data record.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Throughout Chapter 5, we have encountered a seemingly disparate zoo of nonlinear and time-varying models: LPV models, nonlinear ARX/NARMAX, Hammerstein-Wiener block-oriented structures, neural networks, wavelet networks, and fuzzy rule-based models. Before moving to parameter estimation in Chapter 7, Ljung asks a unifying foundational question: **What *is* a model of a dynamical system at its most fundamental level, stripped of specific parameterizations or linearity assumptions?**

1. **The Pragmatic View — A Model as a Predictor Rule**:
   Regardless of whether a model is built from differential equations, transfer functions, or neural networks, its operational job at time $t$ is always the same: take all past observed input-output data $Z^{t-1}$ and produce a guess (prediction) $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ for the next output $y(t)$. If we stop here, we have a **predictor model**. If we also specify how the remaining unpredictable part (the prediction error $\varepsilon_m(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1)$) behaves statistically—either through its full conditional probability density function (CPDF) $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ or just its second moment (covariance matrix $\Lambda_m(t)$)—we obtain a **complete** or **partial probabilistic model**.
2. **Bridging Specific Architectures to General Identification Theory**:
   Once models are defined as predictor functions $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$, all the structural machinery developed for LTI models in Section 4.5 carries over seamlessly:
   - Properties like **linearity**, **time-invariance**, **$k$-step-ahead prediction**, and **simulation (output-error)** become simple structural restrictions on how $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ depends on $t$, $y^{t-1}$, and $u^{t-1}$.
   - A **model set** $\mathcal{M}^*$ is a collection of such predictor models, and a **model structure** $\mathcal{M}$ is a differentiable mapping from a parameter vector $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbf{R}^d$ to a specific predictor $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta) \in \mathcal{M}^*$.
3. **The Abstract View — Why Predictors Do Not Lose Generality (Lemma 5.1)**:
   A pure statistician might object that defining a model as a one-step-ahead predictor seems restrictive or overly pragmatic: since we interact with the system solely through the data sequence $Z^t = (y^t, u^t)$ under random disturbances, shouldn't the most general model be a joint probability distribution $P_m$ or joint PDF $\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t)$ (or conditional joint PDF $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$) over the entire trajectory?
   - Specifying a high-dimensional joint PDF $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$ directly is intractable and obscures causality and the arrow of time.
   - By repeated application of Bayes's rule (chain rule of conditional probabilities), **Lemma 5.1** shows a remarkable equivalence: any joint PDF $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$ induces a conditional mean predictor $g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) = E_m[y(t) | y^{t-1}, u^{t-1}]$ and a conditional error PDF $f_e$; conversely, any predictor $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ paired with an error PDF $f_e$ uniquely reconstructs the full joint PDF of the output sequence $y^t$ as a product of one-step-ahead error densities:
     $$\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t) = \prod_{k=1}^t f_e\big(y(k) - g_m(k, Z^{k-1}), k\big)$$
   - This proves that the **predictor formulation is 100% equivalent in generality to the full joint-PDF formulation**, while naturally encoding causality, sequential time flow, and directly giving us the likelihood function for Maximum Likelihood estimation!

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

| $u(t)$ | System input vector at discrete time $t$ | $\mathbf{R}^m$ ($m$-dimensional column vector) |
| $y^t, u^t$ | Output and input sequences up to and including time $t$: $y^t = (y(1), \dots, y(t))$, $u^t = (u(1), \dots, u(t))$ | $\mathbf{R}^{p t}$ and $\mathbf{R}^{m t}$ |
| $Z^t$ | Complete input-output data record up to and including time $t$: $Z^t = (y^t, u^t)$ | $\mathbf{R}^{p t} \times \mathbf{R}^{m t}$ |

| $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ | One-step-ahead predicted output at time $t$ based on past data $Z^{t-1}$ | $\mathbf{R}^p$ |

| $f_e(x, t)$ | PDF of the prediction error $y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1)$ under the assumption that prediction errors are independent of past data $Z^{t-1}$ | $\mathbf{R}^p \times \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}_+$ |
| $\Lambda_m(t)$ | Covariance matrix (second moment) of the prediction error $y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1)$ at time $t$ | $\mathbf{R}^{p \times p}$ (symmetric positive semidefinite) |
| $W_t^y(q), W_t^u(q)$ | Time-varying linear shift-operator filters operating on $y(t)$ and $u(t)$ | $p \times p$ and $p \times m$ rational/causal operators in $q^{-1}$ |
| $\mathcal{M}^*$ | Model set: a collection of candidate models $m$ | Set of models |
| $\mathcal{M}$ | Model structure: a differentiable mapping from parameter space $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ into model set $\mathcal{M}^*$ | Mapping $\theta \mapsto \mathcal{M}(\theta) \in \mathcal{M}^*$ |
| $\theta$ | Parameter vector indexing a specific model in $\mathcal{M}$ | $D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbf{R}^d$ |
| $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ | Set of admissible parameter values for model structure $\mathcal{M}$ | Subset of $\mathbf{R}^d$ |
| $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ | Parameterized predictor function corresponding to parameter vector $\theta$ | $\mathbf{R}^p$ |
| $\varphi(t, Z^{t-1})$ | Regression vector/matrix (known nonlinear function of past data) in a linear regression structure | $\mathbf{R}^{d \times p}$ (so $\varphi^T(t, Z^{t-1}) \in \mathbf{R}^{p \times d}$) |
| $\mu(t, Z^{t-1})$ | Known offset/bias vector in an affine linear regression predictor | $\mathbf{R}^p$ |

| $\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t)$ | Joint probability density function (PDF) for the entire data sequence $Z^t$ up to time $t$ | $\mathbf{R}^{(p+m)t} \to \mathbf{R}_+$ |
| $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$ | Conditional joint PDF of the output sequence $y^t$ given a deterministic input sequence $u^t$ | $\mathbf{R}^{p t} \to \mathbf{R}_+$ |
| $E_m[\cdot | \cdot]$ | Conditional mathematical expectation evaluated with respect to the probability law of model $m$ | Expectation operator |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Predictor Models, Complete Probabilistic Models, and Partial Probabilistic Models

Assume a dynamical system with a $p$-dimensional output $y(t) \in \mathbf{R}^p$ and an $m$-dimensional input $u(t) \in \mathbf{R}^m$. As in previous chapters, $Z^t = (y^t, u^t)$ denotes the complete input-output data record up to and including time $t$.

##### Definition of a Predictor Model — Eq. (5.61)
A **model $m$ of a dynamical system** is formally defined as a sequence of deterministic functions $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ for $t = 1, 2, \dots$, mapping:

$$
g_m : \mathbf{R} \times \mathbf{R}^{p(t-1)} \times \mathbf{R}^{m(t-1)} \to \mathbf{R}^p$$

which represents a rule for guessing or predicting the current output $y(t)$ from past observed data $Z^{t-1}$:
$$

\hat{y}(t|t-1) = g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) \tag{5.61}$$

A model that specifies *only* the predictor sequence (5.61) is called a **predictor model**.

##### Complete Probabilistic Model — Eqs. (5.62)–(5.63)
When the predictor (5.61) is complemented with the **conditional probability density function (CPDF)** (given past data $Z^{t-1}$) of the associated prediction errors $\varepsilon_m(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1)$:

>
> $$
> f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1}) : \text{CPDF of } y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1), \quad \text{given } Z^{t-1} \tag{5.62}$$
>
> the pair $\big\{ g_m(t, Z^{t-1}),\, f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1}) \big\}$ is called a **complete probabilistic model**.
>
> [!important] Standard Independence Assumption on Prediction Errors — Eq. (5.63)
> A typical modeling assumption is that the prediction errors $\varepsilon_m(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1)$ are **statistically independent** across time (and hence independent of past data $Z^{t-1}$). Under this assumption, the conditional density $f_e$ does not depend on $Z^{t-1}$ and simplifies to a marginal time-indexed density:
> $$
>
>f_e(x, t) : \text{PDF of } y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1), \quad \text{these errors independent} \tag{5.63}$$

##### Partial Probabilistic Model — Eq. (5.64)
In many practical applications (such as weighted least-squares or Gauss-Newton estimation), one prefers not to specify the entire functional shape of the PDF $f_e$, specifying instead only its **second moment (covariance matrix)** alongside the independence assumption:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Lambda_m(t) &: \text{covariance matrix of } y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1) \\
&\phantom{:} \text{these errors independent}
\end{aligned} \tag{5.64}$$

A predictor model (5.61) paired with the covariance specification (5.64) is called a **partial probabilistic model**.

#### 2.2.2 Structural Classification of General Models

A general model $m$ can be classified into familiar structural families according to four functional properties of $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ (and $\Lambda_m$ or $f_e$ when applicable):

1. **Linear Models — Eq. (5.65)**:
   The model $m$ is said to be **linear** if $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is a linear function of the past outputs $y^{t-1}$ and past inputs $u^{t-1}$. Using shift-operator notation, this means there exist time-indexed linear causal filters $W_t^y(q)$ and $W_t^u(q)$ such that:

   $$g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) = W_t^y(q) y(t) + W_t^u(q) u(t) \tag{5.65}$$

   *(Note: Since $g_m$ depends only on $Z^{t-1}$, $W_t^y(q)$ and $W_t^u(q)$ contain only strictly causal delays $q^{-1}, q^{-2}, \dots$)*
2. **Time-Invariant Models**:
   A model $m$ is said to be **time invariant** if the predictor mapping $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is invariant under a shift of absolute time $t$ (i.e., given the same relative sequence of past inputs and outputs, the prediction does not depend on the clock index $t$). If $\Lambda_m(t)$ or $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ is specified, time invariance further requires that $\Lambda_m$ and $f_e$ be independent of absolute time $t$.
3. **$k$-Step-Ahead Predictor Models**:
   A model $m$ is said to be a **$k$-step-ahead predictor** if $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is a function of $y^{t-k}$ and $u^{t-1}$ only (meaning output measurements from times $t-k+1, \dots, t-1$ are not used by the predictor).
4. **Simulation Models (Output-Error Models)**:
   A model $m$ is said to be a **simulation model** or an **output error (OE) model** if $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is a function of the past input sequence $u^{t-1}$ only (completely independent of past measured outputs $y^{t-1}$, which corresponds to $k = t$).

*Note on Stability and Model Equality*: Analogously to the linear time-invariant discussion in Section 4.5 (specifically Eq. (4.116)), one can formally define the stability of the nonlinear predictor function $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ (sensitivity to initial conditions in $Z^{t-1}$ decaying over time) and exact/almost-sure equality between two models $m_1$ and $m_2$. Ljung notes that this follows the exact same conceptual blueprint as Section 4.5 and refrains from repeating the technical details.

#### 2.2.3 Model Sets, Model Structures, and Linear Regressions

Just as in Definition 4.3 for linear systems, we organize candidate models into sets and parameterized structures:

##### Model Sets $\mathcal{M}^*$ and Model Structures $\mathcal{M}$ — Eq. (5.66)
A **model set** $\mathcal{M}^*$ is a collection of models (predictor functions, optionally with $\Lambda$ or $f_e$). A **model structure** $\mathcal{M}$ is a differentiable mapping from a parameter domain $D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbf{R}^d$ into a model set $\mathcal{M}^*$:
$$

\mathcal{M} : \theta \to g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta) \in \mathcal{M}^*; \quad \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbf{R}^d \tag{5.66}$$

(together with $\Lambda(t; \theta)$ or $f_e(x, t; \theta)$, if applicable). Once equality between models in $\mathcal{M}^*$ is defined, **parameter identifiability** ($\mathcal{M}(\theta_1) = \mathcal{M}(\theta_2) \implies \theta_1 = \theta_2$) and **system identifiability** are defined identically to Section 4.5.

##### General Linear Regression Structure — Eq. (5.67)
A model structure $\mathcal{M}$ is called a **linear regression** if its parameter domain is the entire Euclidean space $D_{\mathcal{M}} = \mathbf{R}^d$ and the parameterized predictor function $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ is a linear (or affine) function of the parameter vector $\theta$:

>
> $$
> g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta) = \varphi^T(t, Z^{t-1})\theta + \mu(t, Z^{t-1}) \tag{5.67}$$
>
> [!tip] Crucial Distinction: "Linear Model" vs. "Linear Regression"
> Compare Eq. (5.65) and Eq. (5.67) carefully:
> - **Linear Model (5.65)**: $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is linear in the **data** $(y^{t-1}, u^{t-1})$, but may be highly nonlinear in the parameters $\theta$ (e.g., an LTI Box-Jenkins or ARMAX model).
> - **Linear Regression (5.67)**: $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ is linear (or affine) in the **parameters** $\theta$, while the regressor $\varphi(t, Z^{t-1})$ and offset $\mu(t, Z^{t-1})$ can be arbitrarily **nonlinear functions of the past data** $Z^{t-1}$ (e.g., polynomial NARX, radial basis networks with fixed centers, or fuzzy models with fixed membership functions!).
>
> ---
>
> #### 2.2.4 Another View of Models: The Joint Probability Density Formulation
>
> Defining a model as a predictor (5.61) is pragmatic and operational. Ljung now develops a more abstract, purely statistical viewpoint and proves its equivalence to the predictor viewpoint.
>
> ##### Abstract Relational and Probabilistic View — Eqs. (5.68)–(5.71)
> Since an external observer communicates with the system solely through the input-output sequence $Z^t = (y^t, u^t)$, any assumption about the system is ultimately an assumption about $Z^t$:
> $$
>
>\text{A model of a system is an assumed relationship for } Z^t, \quad t = 1, 2, \dots \tag{5.68}$$

Because real experiments are affected by unmeasurable disturbances, applying the exact same input sequence $u^N$ in different experiments yields different output realizations $y^N$. Thus, it is natural to treat $y^t$ (or $Z^t$) as a random variable and define a model $m$ as a specification of the probabilistic properties of $Z^t$ via a **probability measure** $P_m$ or a **joint probability density function (PDF)** for $Z^t$:

$$
\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t) \tag{5.69}$$

such that for any measurable subset (event) $B \subset \mathbf{R}^{(p+m)t}$, the probability that $Z^t$ falls in $B$ is:
$$

P_m(Z^t \in B) = \int_{x^t \in B} \bar{f}_m(t, x^t) \, dx^t \tag{5.70}$$

When the input sequence $u^t$ is treated as a known, deterministic sequence, we focus instead on the **conditional joint PDF** of the output sequence $y^t$ given $u^t$:

>
> $$
> \bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t) \tag{5.71}$$
>
> ##### Why Direct Joint PDFs Are Awkward—and How They Connect to Predictors — Eq. (5.72)
> Constructing a high-dimensional joint density $\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t)$ or $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$ directly is awkward because it treats the entire $t$-step data record as a static vector, hiding the natural arrow of time and causality. (Indeed, introducing unmeasurable stochastic processes $\{w(t)\}$ and $\{e(t)\}$ in Sections 4.2–4.3 was simply a convenient, physically intuitive device for implicitly defining $\bar{f}_m$.)
>
> However, we can move back and forth between the joint PDF view (5.69)/(5.71) and the predictor view (5.61)–(5.62):
> 1. **From Joint PDF $\bar{f}_m$ to Predictor $g_m$ and Error CPDF $f_e$**:
> Given the joint PDF $\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t)$ in (5.69), we can always marginalize and condition on $Z^{t-1} = (y^{t-1}, u^{t-1})$ to compute the conditional mean of $y(t)$ given $y^{t-1}, u^{t-1}$:
> $$
>\hat{y}(t|t-1) = E_m\big[ y(t) \big| y^{t-1}, u^{t-1} \big] = g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) \tag{5.72}$$
>
>    as well as the conditional distribution of the residual $y(t) - g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ given $Z^{t-1}$, which yields the CPDF $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ in (5.62).
> 2. **From Predictor $g_m$ and Error PDF $f_e$ to Joint PDF $\bar{f}_m$ (Lemma 5.1)**:
>    Conversely, given a predictor $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ and an error density $f_e(x, t)$, we can reconstruct the complete joint density $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$ via **Lemma 5.1**.
>
> ---
>
> #### 2.2.5 Equivalence of Predictor and Joint PDF Formulations (Lemma 5.1 & Proof)
>
> ##### Statement of Lemma 5.1 — Eqs. (5.73)–(5.74)
> [!important] Lemma 5.1 (Reconstruction of the Joint Output PDF from the Predictor and Error Density)
> **Assumptions**:
> 1. Suppose that $u^t$ is a given, **deterministic sequence**.
> 2. Assume that the generation of the output sequence $y^t$ is described by the model:
>
> $$
> y(t) = g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) + \varepsilon_m(t) \tag{5.73}$$
>
> 3. Assume that the conditional PDF of the prediction error $\varepsilon_m(t)$ (given past data $y^{t-1}, u^{t-1}$) is $f_e(x, t)$ (independent of $Z^{t-1}$).
>
> **Conclusion**:
> Then the joint probability density function for the entire output sequence $y^t = (y(1), \dots, y(t))$, given $u^t$, is given by the product:
> $$
>\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t) = \prod_{k=1}^t f_e\big( y(k) - g_m(k, Z^{k-1}), \, k \big) \tag{5.74}$$
> 
> *(Notation note: In (5.74), for convenience, the dummy evaluation variable $x_k$ for the density of $y(k)$ is denoted by $y(k)$ itself.)*

##### Step-by-Step Proof of Lemma 5.1 — Eq. (5.75)
1. **Single-Step Conditional Density of $y(t)$ given $Z^{t-1}$**:
   Since $y(t) = g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) + \varepsilon_m(t)$ by (5.73), and $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is completely fixed (deterministic) once $Z^{t-1} = (y^{t-1}, u^{t-1})$ is given, $y(t)$ is simply a deterministic shift of the random variable $\varepsilon_m(t)$ by $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ (with unit Jacobian determinant $\det(I) = 1$). Therefore, the conditional PDF of $y(t)$ evaluated at dummy variable $x_t$, given $Z^{t-1}$, is:

   $$p(x_t | Z^{t-1}) = f_e\big( x_t - g_m(t, Z^{t-1}), \, t \big) \tag{5.75}$$

2. **Two-Step Joint Conditional Density via Bayes's Rule**:
   Using Bayes's rule / the multiplication rule of conditional probabilities ($P(A \cap B | C) = P(A | B, C) P(B | C)$, see Appendix Eq. (I.10)), the joint CPDF of the last two outputs $y(t)$ and $y(t-1)$ given $Z^{t-2}$ (and deterministic $u^t$) can be factored as:

   $$\begin{aligned}
   p(x_t, x_{t-1} | Z^{t-2}) &= p\big(x_t \big| y(t-1) = x_{t-1}, \, Z^{t-2}\big) \cdot p(x_{t-1} | Z^{t-2}) \\
   &= f_e\big( x_t - g_m(t, Z^{t-1}), \, t \big) \cdot f_e\big( x_{t-1} - g_m(t-1, Z^{t-2}), \, t-1 \big)
   \end{aligned}$$

   where $y(t-1)$ inside $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is evaluated at the dummy variable $x_{t-1}$, and $u^t$ is treated as a given deterministic sequence throughout.
3. **Recursive Iteration down to $t = 1$**:
   Iterating this exact conditioning factorization step-by-step backward down to $k = 1$ yields the joint probability density function of the complete sequence $y(t), y(t-1), \dots, y(1)$ given $u^t$, which is $\bar{f}_m(t, y^t | u^t)$ in Eq. (5.74). $\blacksquare$

#### 2.2.6 Martingale Difference Property & Relaxing Error Independence

Ljung concludes Section 5.7 with a crucial **Remark** contrasting the general joint PDF (5.69) with the independent-error predictor model (5.73)–(5.74):

- **The Fundamental Equivalence**:
  A predictor model (5.61) complemented with an assumed conditional PDF for the associated prediction errors is *no more and no less general* than the general, unstructured joint PDF model (5.69).
- **When Errors Depend on Past Data — Martingale Difference Property — Eq. (5.76)**:
  If we start from an arbitrary joint PDF $\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t)$ in (5.69) and define the predictor as the conditional expectation $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = E_m[y(t) | y^{t-1}, u^{t-1}] = g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ as in (5.72), the resulting prediction error $\varepsilon_m(t) = y(t) - g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ has a conditional PDF $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ that may in general still depend on past data $Z^{t-1}$ (for example, conditional heteroskedasticity where the error variance depends on past inputs/outputs).
  - Consequently, the prediction errors $\varepsilon_m(t)$ are **not necessarily statistically independent**, *even though* they always form a **martingale difference sequence** (their conditional mean given all past errors/data is identically zero):

    $$E\big[ \varepsilon_m(t) \big| \varepsilon_m(t-1), \dots, \varepsilon_m(1) \big] = 0 \tag{5.76}$$

  - In Lemma 5.1 (Eqs. (5.73)–(5.74)), Ljung assumed for simplicity that $f_e(x, t)$ did not depend on $Z^{t-1}$ (implying independence of $\varepsilon_m(t)$ from past data). If that assumption is relaxed back to the general CPDF $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ of (5.62), the proof of Lemma 5.1 still holds identically with $f_e(y(k) - g_m(k, Z^{k-1}), k, Z^{k-1})$ inside the product (5.74)!

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: Why Lemma 5.1 is the Cornerstone of Maximum Likelihood Identification**:
>   Equation (5.74) looks like a purely theoretical identity in Chapter 5, but it is actually the **exact likelihood function** used in Chapter 7! When you substitute a parameterized predictor $g(k, Z^{k-1}; \theta)$ into (5.74) and plug in the actual measured data $Z^N$, $\bar{f}_m(N, y^N | u^N; \theta)$ becomes the likelihood function $L(\theta)$, and taking the negative logarithm turns the product $\prod_{k=1}^N$ into the sum $-\sum_{k=1}^N \ln f_e(\varepsilon(k, \theta), k)$ minimized by Prediction Error and Maximum Likelihood methods.
> - **Leap 2: Uncorrelated / Zero Conditional Mean $\neq$ Statistically Independent**:
>   Pay close attention to the Remark around Eq. (5.76). When $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = E_m[y(t) | Z^{t-1}]$, the law of iterated expectations guarantees that $\varepsilon_m(t)$ has zero conditional mean given the past: $E[\varepsilon_m(t) | \varepsilon_m(t-1), \dots, \varepsilon_m(1)] = 0$ (a **martingale difference sequence**). However, higher-order moments (like the conditional variance $\Lambda_m(t, Z^{t-1})$) can still depend on $Z^{t-1}$ unless we explicitly assume $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1}) = f_e(x, t)$ as in (5.63) and (5.73).
> - **Notation Trap 1: Dummy Variables vs. Random Variables in Eq. (5.74)**:
>   In Eq. (5.75), Ljung writes $p(x_t | Z^{t-1})$ using $x_t$ as the dummy evaluation argument of the density of the random variable $y(t)$. In Eq. (5.74), he drops $x_k$ and writes $y(k)$ directly inside $f_e(y(k) - g_m(k, Z^{k-1}), k)$ to avoid clutter. Remember that $y(k)$ in (5.74) plays two roles simultaneously: it is the evaluation point of the density at time $k$, and it is part of the conditioning history $Z^{k-1}, Z^k, \dots$ for all subsequent factors $j > k$.
> - **Notation Trap 2: $\bar{f}_m$ vs. $f_e$**:
>   Do not confuse $\bar{f}_m(t, Z^t)$ (with an overbar and subscript $m$, denoting the **joint PDF** of the entire $t$-step trajectory $Z^t \in \mathbf{R}^{(p+m)t}$) with $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ (subscript $e$, denoting the **single-step conditional PDF** of the $p$-dimensional prediction error $\varepsilon_m(t) \in \mathbf{R}^p$ at time $t$).
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *How does a neural network or NARX model fit into the hierarchy of Predictor Model (5.61), Partial Probabilistic Model (5.64), and Complete Probabilistic Model (5.62)?*
>   2. *Why does transforming $y(t) = g_m(t, Z^{t-1}) + \varepsilon_m(t)$ into the conditional density $p(x_t | Z^{t-1}) = f_e(x_t - g_m(t, Z^{t-1}), t)$ in Eq. (5.75) not require a Jacobian determinant factor $\left|\det \frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial y}\right|$?* (Because given $Z^{t-1}$, $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ is a constant offset, so $\frac{\partial \varepsilon_m(t)}{\partial y(t)} = I_p$, whose determinant is $1$!)
