# 5.8 Summary

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: Section 5.8 (along with the bibliographic context in Section 5.9) synthesizes the entire Chapter 5 taxonomy of time-varying and nonlinear system models—spanning physical state-space structures, semi-physical regressor transformations, look-up tables, and universal black-box basis expansions—into the single formal predictor framework $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ . It contrasts the formal mathematical continuity from linear to nonlinear identification with the profound practical necessity of injecting structural prior knowledge to tame the curse of dimensionality.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

From a purely formal standpoint, developing models for nonlinear and time-varying systems mirrors the linear time-invariant (LTI) paradigm of Chapter 4: in both cases, **a dynamical model is fundamentally a predictor function** that maps past input-output data $Z^{t-1}$ to a one-step-ahead prediction $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ . The sole mathematical difference is that $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ is no longer restricted to be a linear, time-invariant filter of $Z^{t-1}$ .

From a **practical engineering standpoint**, however, moving from linear to nonlinear models introduces a massive leap in complexity:
- In the LTI world, unstructured black-box models (ARX, ARMAX, OE, BJ, state-space) are compact, canonical, and low-dimensional; a handful of lag orders fully specify the structure.
- In the nonlinear world, the sheer richness of possible nonlinear dynamic behaviors makes purely unstructured black-box modeling far more demanding due to the curse of dimensionality and non-convex optimization landscapes.
- Consequently, **building prior knowledge about the character of the nonlinearities into the model structure** becomes the central strategic decision in nonlinear system identification.

Chapter 5 organizes this structural prior knowledge along a continuous spectrum—from full physical laws to completely data-driven basis expansions—all converging into the formal predictor architecture of Section 5.7:

| Model Family (Chapter 5 Section) | Predictor Structure $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ | Role of Physical / Domain Insight | Key Engineering Trade-offs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Linear Time-Varying (LTV)** (Sec. 5.1) | $\varphi^T(t)\theta(t)$ or time-varying filters $G(q, t), H(q, t)$ | Known scheduling variables (LPV) or assumed smooth parameter evolution | Simple extension of LTI tools, but cannot capture amplitude-dependent static/dynamic nonlinearities unless reformulated. |
| **Nonlinear White-Box / Grey-Box State-Space** (Sec. 5.3) | Simulation/innovations of $\dot{x} = f(x, u; \theta)$ , $y = h(x, u; \theta) + v$ (Eq. 5.25–5.26) | **High ("Ambitious Physical Modeling")**: First-principles balance equations & kinematics | Physically interpretable parameters $\theta$ and valid extrapolation across regimes; requires deep domain effort and exact numerical integration/filtering. |
| **Semi-Physical / Block-Oriented & Look-Up Tables** (Sec. 5.2) | Linear regression $\theta^T \varphi(t)$ on nonlinearly transformed regressors (Eq. 5.13), Hammerstein-Wiener cascades, or parameterized look-up tables | **Medium ("Leisurely Semi-Physical Modeling")**: Insight into how raw inputs/outputs combine (e.g., power $u^2$ , ratios, valves) or piecewise tabular curves | Retains linear-in-parameters estimation convenience or compact static curves; look-up tables avoid analytic formulas entirely by treating table entries as $\theta$ . |
| **Nonlinear Black-Box Basis Expansions** (Sec. 5.4–5.5) | $\sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k g_k(\varphi(t), \beta_k, \gamma_k)$ (ANNs, Radial Basis Functions, Wavelets, NARMAX polynomials) | **Low (Pure Black-Box)**: Only requires choosing regressor lag vector $\varphi(t)$ and basis family (ridge, radial, local) | Universal approximation power when physics is unknown; suffers from high parameter dimension, local minima, and poor extrapolation outside training data. |
| **Fuzzy & Operating-Regime Models** (Sec. 5.6) | Weighted blend of local rules/models via membership functions $\mu_k(\varphi(t))$ | **Rule-Based / Linguistic**: Expert qualitative rules or local linear operating regimes | Bridges human expert heuristics and Takagi-Sugeno local linear models with radial/local basis function expansions. |
| **Formal Probabilistic / Predictor Models** (Sec. 5.7) | $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ + error PDF $f_e(\varepsilon(t); \theta)$ | **Foundational Unification**: Separates deterministic prediction from stochastic innovation distribution | Proves that predictor + error PDF is *no more and no less general* than specifying the full joint PDF of all observations $Z^N$ . |

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

| Symbol | Meaning / Physical Interpretation | Space / Dimensions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $Z^{t-1}$ | Past input-output data sequence $\{y(t-1), u(t-1), \dots, y(1), u(1)\}$ | $\mathbb{R}^{(t-1)(p+m)}$ |
| $\theta$ | Parameter vector indexing a specific candidate model within a model structure $\mathcal{M}$ | $D_\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $\varphi(t)$ | Nonlinear regressor vector constructed from $Z^{t-1}$ (and possibly past predictions) | $\mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ | Deterministic one-step-ahead predictor mapping past data to next output | $\mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$ | Prediction error (innovation) $y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$ | $\mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $f_e(x; \theta)$ | Probability density function (PDF) postulated for the prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$ | $\mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ |
| $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$ | Complete formal model (predictor $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ optionally paired with error variance/PDF) | Model manifold $\mathcal{M}$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

Section 5.8 and Section 5.9 crystallize four unifying mathematical and structural principles that connect all model families in Chapter 5 to the estimation chapters that follow (Chapters 7–10):

#### 1. The Structural Hierarchy of Nonlinear Incorporation
Rather than treating nonlinear models as disconnected ad-hoc tricks, Ljung establishes a formal hierarchy of how nonlinearity enters the predictor $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ :

1. **White-Box / Grey-Box State-Space Parameterization (Eq. 5.25–5.26)**:
   When physical mechanisms are well understood (e.g., aircraft flight dynamics, chemical reactors), the system is formulated in continuous- or discrete-time state space:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t+1) &= f(t, x(t), u(t), w(t); \theta) \\
y(t) &= h(t, x(t), u(t), v(t); \theta)
\end{aligned}
$$

   In the deterministic simulation case ( $w(t) \equiv 0$ , Eq. 5.26), the predictor depends only on the initial state and the input history $u^{t-1}$ . When process noise $w(t)$ is present, computing the exact conditional mean predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = E[y(t) | Z^{t-1}; \theta]$ requires nonlinear filtering (such as the Extended Kalman Filter).

2. **Semi-Physical Regressor Construction & Look-Up Tables (Eq. 5.13)**:
   When full state-space physics is too complex, "leisurely" semi-physical insight guides the formation of nonlinear regressors $\varphi(t) = \varphi(Z^{t-1})$ so that the predictor remains a linear regression:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \theta^T \varphi(t)
$$

   Crucially, Ljung emphasizes in Section 5.8 that **physical insight does not need to take an analytic algebraic form**: static nonlinearities (such as valve characteristics, aerodynamic coefficients, or magnetic saturation) can be defined via piecewise **look-up tables** (Section 5.2), where the unknown model parameters $\theta$ are directly the breakpoint levels or table entries themselves.

3. **Universal Black-Box Function Approximation (Sections 5.4–5.6)**:
   When physical insight is absent, the predictor is decomposed into a coordinate projection onto a finite regressor vector $\varphi(t)$ followed by a nonlinear basis function expansion:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(\varphi(t); \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k g_k(\varphi(t), \beta_k, \gamma_k)
$$

   This single mathematical template encompasses **Artificial Neural Networks** (ridge basis functions $\sigma(\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k)$ ), **Radial Basis Function / Kernel networks** (local radial basis functions), **Wavelet networks** (multiresolution dilated/translated bases), **NARMAX polynomial/Volterra expansions** (global polynomial bases), and **Fuzzy / Operating-Regime models** (normalized local basis functions blended across operating zones).

#### 2. The Predictor-Centric Unification (Section 5.7 Recap in Section 5.8)
At the top of page 165, Ljung summarizes the foundational takeaway of Section 5.7:
- **Primary Definition of a Model**: A model of a dynamical system is, in the first place, a **predictor function** from past observations $Z^{t-1}$ to the future output $y(t)$ :

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)
$$

- **Probabilistic Augmentation**: To turn a deterministic predictor into a full probabilistic description of the stochastic process $y(t)$ , one complements the predictor function with an assumed statistical characterization of the associated prediction error:

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)
$$

  such as specifying its conditional variance $\Lambda(t, \theta)$ or its full conditional probability density function (CPDF) $f_e(\cdot; \theta)$ .
- **Equivalence to Joint PDF Specification**: By recursively applying Bayes' rule (Eq. 5.74–5.75 on p. 164), specifying $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ together with the innovation density $f_e(x, t)$ uniquely generates the complete joint probability density function $\bar{f}_m(x_N, \dots, x_1 | u^N)$ of the entire observed trajectory. Even if $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ depends on past data $Z^{t-1}$ , the resulting prediction errors still form a **martingale difference sequence** (Eq. 5.76):

$$
E\big[\varepsilon_m(t) \,\big|\, \varepsilon_m(t-1), \dots, \varepsilon_m(1)\big] = 0
$$

> [!important] Why Unifying Everything as $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ Matters
> Because every model in Chapter 4 (LTI) and Chapter 5 (LTV, semi-physical, state-space, neural, wavelet, fuzzy) reduces to a parameterized predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ producing a prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$ , the parameter estimation theory in **Chapter 7 (Prediction Error Methods)** and **Chapter 8–9 (Convergence and Asymptotic Distribution)** can be developed **once** in complete generality for all linear and nonlinear models alike!

#### 3. Historical & Bibliographic Lineage (Section 5.9 Highlights)
Section 5.9 provides essential context linking Chapter 5's concepts to the broader system identification literature:
- **NARMAX & Parametric Nonlinear Models**: Introduced and systematically studied by Leontaritis and Billings (1985) ("Nonlinear ARMAX"; also illustrated immediately in Problem 5G.1, Eq. 5.77), extending ARMAX by replacing linear lag polynomials with a nonlinear function $f(x(t-1), \dots, u(t-n); \theta)$ .
- **Block-Oriented Cascades**: The **Hammerstein model** (static input nonlinearity followed by linear dynamics) dates back to Narendra and Gallman (1966); the **Wiener model** (linear dynamics followed by static output nonlinearity) is treated by Wigren (1993) and Kalafatis, Wang, and Cluett (1997), while general **Volterra and Wiener functional series** are covered in Rugh (1981) and Korenberg (1991).
- **Semi-Physical & Grey-Box Modeling**: Automated algebraic and physical insight tools for constructing nonlinear regressors are formalized in Lindskog and Ljung (1995), while nonlinear simulation models (Eq. 5.25–5.26) in aerospace are exemplified by Grübel (1985).
- **Unified Black-Box & Operating-Regime Framework**: Ljung's unified treatment of ANNs, wavelets, radial basis functions, and fuzzy models in Sections 5.4–5.6 is directly grounded in the landmark survey by **Sjöberg et al. (1995)**. Related **operating-regime-based identification** (blending local linear models across operating regions, closely tied to Takagi-Sugeno fuzzy models) is developed in Johansen and Foss (1995) and Skeppstedt, Ljung, and Millnert (1992).
- **Foundational Model Concept**: The philosophical and mathematical nature of what constitutes a dynamical model (Section 5.7) connects to Willems' behavioral and model-theoretic framework (Willems, 1985).

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 — Formal Similarity vs. Practical Chasm**: Do not confuse *formal mathematical equivalence* with *practical modeling ease*. Formally, replacing $\varphi^T(t)\theta$ with $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ barely changes the notation. Practically, the space of nonlinear functions is infinite-dimensional, meaning unstructured black-box models require vastly more informative data and careful regularization than LTI models unless you constrain the structure using physical or semi-physical insight.
> - **Leap 2 — "Physical Insight" Includes Non-Analytic Look-Up Tables**: Readers often assume "physical modeling" means writing down differential equations with analytic formulas. Ljung explicitly stresses in Section 5.8 that knowing *which* variables interact nonlinearly allows you to represent that nonlinearity as a **look-up table** whose grid values are the estimated parameters $\theta$ . This is a powerful industrial middle ground between white-box physics and neural networks.
> - **Notation Trap — Deterministic Independence vs. Martingale Difference Errors (Page 164)**: At the top of page 164 (concluding Section 5.7 right before Section 5.8), note the subtle distinction between assuming the innovation density $f_e(x, t)$ is independent of past data $Z^{t-1}$ (Eq. 5.73, which implies independent prediction errors) versus the most general conditional density $f_e(x, t, Z^{t-1})$ (Eq. 5.62). Even when the error variance or shape depends on past data (heteroscedasticity), choosing $g_m(t, Z^{t-1})$ as the conditional mean guarantees that the prediction errors $\varepsilon_m(t)$ form a **martingale difference sequence**:
>
> $$
> E\big[\varepsilon_m(t) \,\big|\, \varepsilon_m(t-1), \dots, \varepsilon_m(1)\big] = 0
> $$
>
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *Given a real engineering system with mild nonlinearities, where on the Chapter 5 spectrum should you start—semi-physical regressor transformations (5.2), look-up tables (5.2), full nonlinear state-space simulation (5.3), or neural/wavelet black-box expansions (5.4–5.5)?*
>   2. *How does reducing every Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 model to $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ set the stage for defining a single criterion function to estimate $\theta$ in Chapter 7?*
