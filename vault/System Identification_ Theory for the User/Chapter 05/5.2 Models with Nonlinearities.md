# 5.2 Models with Nonlinearities

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: After Section 5.1 showed how small perturbations around a nominal trajectory reduce a nonlinear system to a linear time-varying (LTV) model, Section 5.2 addresses systems whose nonlinearities cannot be linearized away—demonstrating how **block-oriented structures** (Hammerstein and Wiener models) and **semi-physical modeling** allow essential physical nonlinearities to be embedded directly into tractable predictor and linear-regression frameworks before tackling general nonlinear state-space models in Section 5.3 and black-box function expansions in Section 5.4.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Allowing a general nonlinear relationship between the input sequence and the output sequence—such as the nonlinear state-space description in Equation (5.8)—vastly expands the class of physical systems we can describe. However, this flexibility comes at a severe price when inferring models from finite data records:

- **The Curse of Infinite-Dimensional Function Spaces**: Even a deterministic first-order nonlinear state-space model ($\dim x = 1$) without disturbances requires specifying two unknown functions, $f(\cdot, \cdot)$ and $h(\cdot)$, which live in an **infinite-dimensional function space**. By contrast, the corresponding first-order linear model is completely characterized by just **two real numbers**.
- **Why Semi-Physical Modeling Comes Before Black-Box Expansions**: While general black-box parameterizations of nonlinear predictors exist (and are deferred to Section 5.4), attempting to fit arbitrary nonlinear combinations of past inputs and outputs blindly causes a combinatorial explosion in the number of parameters. Ljung emphasizes a crucial engineering principle: *always try to use basic physical insight first to identify the character and location of the dominant nonlinearities*.
- **Two Practical Paradigms for Known or Structured Nonlinearities**:
  1. **Block-Oriented Static Nonlinearities (Hammerstein & Wiener Models)**: In many industrial processes, the core energy- or mass-storage dynamics behave linearly, while the nonlinearity is isolated at the boundaries—either in the **actuator** at the input (e.g., valve saturation, deadzone, or square-law heating) giving a **Hammerstein model**, or in the **sensor** at the output (e.g., nonlinear calibration curve or saturating transducer) giving a **Wiener model**.
  2. **Semi-Physical Linear Regressions**: Using high-school physics (conservation of energy, mass, or power $P = V \cdot I$), we can often deduce specific nonlinear combinations of measured signals (such as products or ratios of delayed inputs and outputs) that act as composite "virtual inputs" or **nonlinear regressors** $\varphi_i(u^t, y^{t-1})$. Even though the regressors are nonlinear functions of the raw data, the predictor remains **linear in the unknown parameters** $\theta$, preserving all the closed-form least-squares estimation machinery of Chapter 4.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Wiener, Hammerstein, and Wiener-Hammerstein Models

**Physical Assumption**: The underlying dynamic subsystem is accurately described by a linear transfer function $G(q, \theta)$, while static (memoryless) nonlinearities occur strictly at the input actuator, the output sensor, or both.

- **Hammerstein Model (Input Nonlinearity)**: A static nonlinearity $f(\cdot)$ precedes the linear dynamic system. The raw input $u(t)$ is first transformed into an effective internal input $f(u(t))$, which then drives the linear dynamics.
- **Wiener Model (Output Nonlinearity)**: The input $u(t)$ first drives a linear dynamic block to produce an intermediate signal $z(t)$, which is then passed through a static output nonlinearity $y(t) = f(z(t))$.
- **Wiener-Hammerstein Model**: A cascade combining both an input static nonlinearity and an output static nonlinearity around a linear dynamic core.

![[assets/fig_5_2_hammerstein_wiener_models.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 5.1)
> - **Top Cascade (Hammerstein Model)**: Follow the signal flow from left to right: the measured actuator command $u(t)$ enters the memoryless block $f$ to yield the distorted intermediate signal $f(u(t))$, which then excites the **Linear Model** block to produce the output $y(t)$. Physically, this represents actuator saturation or nonlinear valve flow characteristics.
> - **Bottom Cascade (Wiener Model)**: The order of operations is reversed: $u(t)$ directly excites the **Linear Model** block to generate an internal linear state/output $z(t)$, which is subsequently distorted by the memoryless sensor block $f$ to yield the measured output $y(t) = f(z(t))$. Because $f$ does not commute with dynamic operators $G(q)$, the two cascades exhibit fundamentally different dynamic responses to step and sinusoidal inputs.

**Parameterizing the Hammerstein Predictor**:
Suppose the static input nonlinearity $f(\cdot)$ is parameterized by a parameter vector $\eta$—either via **physical parameters** (such as saturation breakpoint and saturation level) or via **black-box basis functions** (such as spline coefficients)—writing $f(u(t), \eta)$. If the linear subsystem is modeled by the transfer function $G(q, \theta)$ (implicitly assuming an output-error noise structure $H(q) = 1$, or absorbing noise dynamics separately), the one-step-ahead predictor takes the form of Equation (5.11):

$$
\hat{y}(t \mid \theta, \eta) = G(q, \theta) f(u(t), \eta) \tag{5.11}
$$

This predictor is only mildly more complicated than the linear transfer-function predictors of Chapter 4; prediction gradients with respect to $\theta$ and $\eta$ can be computed directly via the chain rule.

#### 2.2.2 Nonlinear Predictors via Linear Regression Models

Recall from Equation (4.12) in Chapter 4 that a **linear regression model structure** expresses the predictor as a linear inner product in the parameter vector $\theta$:

$$
\hat{y}(t \mid \theta) = \varphi^T(t) \theta \tag{5.12}
$$

In Chapter 4 (Equation 4.11), the components of the regressor vector $\varphi(t)$ were simply lagged inputs and outputs ($-y(t-1), \dots, u(t-1), \dots$).

**Crucial Mathematical Insight**: For the estimation properties of Equation (5.12) to hold, *it is completely immaterial how $\varphi(t)$ is computed from past data*, provided that $\varphi(t)$ is a **known, computable quantity at time $t$** that does not depend on the unknown parameter vector $\theta$. Letting $u^t$ and $y^{t-1}$ denote the observed input and output sequences up to times $t$ and $t-1$, we can choose $d$ arbitrary nonlinear basis functions $\varphi_i(u^t, y^{t-1})$ and write Equation (5.13):

$$
\hat{y}(t \mid \theta) = \theta_1 \varphi_1(u^t, y^{t-1}) + \dots + \theta_d \varphi_d(u^t, y^{t-1}) = \varphi^T(t) \theta \tag{5.13}
$$

Equation (5.13) serves as a finite-dimensional linear-in-parameters expansion of an arbitrary nonlinear predictor. Ljung contrasts two strategies for selecting the nonlinear regressor functions $\varphi_i(u^t, y^{t-1})$:

1. **Black-Box Polynomial Expansions (GMDH & Polynomial Hammerstein ARX)**:
   - One can form polynomial combinations of past inputs and outputs (explored systematically in Section 5.4 and associated with the Group Method of Data Handling, **GMDH**, of Ivakhnenko, 1968). In general, cross-products of multiple lags create a huge number of candidate regressors.
   - However, for a **Hammerstein model** whose static nonlinearity is approximated by an $m$-th degree polynomial in $u$,

$$
f(u) = \alpha_1 u + \alpha_2 u^2 + \dots + \alpha_m u^m \tag{5.14}
$$

   - Feeding $f(u(t))$ into an ARX-type equation allows each power $u^k(t)$ to pass through its own numerator polynomial $B_k(q) = \alpha_k B(q)$ (or even distinct numerator dynamics $B_k(q)$), yielding the generalized polynomial Hammerstein ARX model of Equation (5.15):

$$
A(q) y(t) = B_1(q) u(t) + B_2(q) u^2(t) + \dots + B_m(q) u^m(t) \tag{5.15}
$$

   - Because $u(t), u^2(t), \dots, u^m(t)$ and their time lags can be precomputed directly from the input record, Equation (5.15) is strictly a **linear regression model structure** in the coefficients of $A(q)$ and $B_1(q), \dots, B_m(q)$.

2. **Semi-Physical Modeling (Using Physical Insight)**:
   - Rather than blindly expanding polynomials, a brief analysis using elementary physics reveals the exact functional form of the dominant nonlinearities (e.g., multiplying voltage $V(t)$ and current $I(t)$ to form electrical power $P(t) = V(t)I(t)$, as in Example 1.5).

#### 2.2.3 Example 5.1: Semi-Physical Modeling of a Solar-Heated House

Consider the solar-heated house introduced in Example 1.1. The system has two measured exogenous inputs—**pump velocity** $u(t)$ (which circulates air through the solar collector into the heat storage pebble bed) and **solar radiation intensity** $I(t)$—and one measured output, the **storage temperature** $y(t)$.

##### Step 1: Failure of the Naive Ad-Hoc Linear ARX Model
If we ignore physics and write down a standard two-input linear ARX model of the type in Equation (4.7), we obtain Equation (5.16):

$$
y(t) + a_1 y(t-1) + a_2 y(t-2) = b_1 u(t-1) + b_2 u(t-2) + c_1 I(t-1) + c_2 I(t-2) \tag{5.16}
$$

**Why Equation (5.16) fails physically**: Equation (5.16) assumes the effects of pump velocity $u$ and solar intensity $I$ are **additive**. In reality, if the fan/pump is turned off ($u(t) = 0$), *no heated air is transported from the collector to the storage tank*, so solar radiation $I(t)$ cannot heat the storage tank at all! Conversely, running the pump generates convective heat transfer proportional to the product of the collector temperature and the flow rate.

##### Step 2: Discrete-Time Heat Balance Equations with Unmeasured State $x(t)$
Let $x(t)$ denote the **temperature of the air inside the solar panel collector** at time $t$. Crucially, **$x(t)$ is not measured**. Under simple lumped-parameter thermal assumptions (measuring temperatures relative to ambient environment temperature):

1. **Collector Heat Balance (Equation 5.17)**: Over one sampling interval, the temperature rise in the collector $[= x(t+1) - x(t)]$ equals the solar heat absorbed $[= d_2 \cdot I(t)]$ minus conductive/convective heat loss from the collector to the environment $[= d_3 \cdot x(t)]$ minus the heat transported out of the collector into storage by the air flow $[= d_0 \cdot x(t) \cdot u(t)]$:

$$
x(t+1) - x(t) = d_2 I(t) - d_3 x(t) - d_0 x(t) \cdot u(t) \tag{5.17}
$$

2. **Storage Tank Heat Balance (Equation 5.18)**: Similarly, the temperature rise of the storage tank $[= y(t+1) - y(t)]$ equals the heat transported from the collector $[= d_0 x(t) \cdot u(t)]$ minus heat loss from the storage tank to the environment $[= d_1 y(t)]$:

$$
y(t+1) - y(t) = d_0 x(t) u(t) - d_1 y(t) \tag{5.18}
$$

Here $d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3$ are unknown physical heat-transfer coefficients. Notice the **bilinear (product) nonlinearity** $x(t) u(t)$ coupling the unmeasured collector temperature and the pump velocity.

##### Step 3: Algebraic Elimination of the Unmeasured Collector Temperature $x(t)$
Because $x(t)$ is unmeasured, we must eliminate it between Equations (5.17) and (5.18) to obtain an input-output relation involving only the measured signals $y, u, I$.

> [!tip] Complete Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation of Equation (5.19)
> **Assumptions**: Assume the pump velocity is non-zero at the relevant lagged time step ($u(t-2) \neq 0$) so we can solve Equation (5.18) for the unmeasured state.
> 
> 1. **Solve Equation (5.18) for $d_0 x(t)$**: Rearranging (5.18) gives:
>
> $$
> d_0 x(t) u(t) = y(t+1) - (1 - d_1) y(t) \implies d_0 x(t) = \frac{y(t+1) - (1 - d_1) y(t)}{u(t)}
> $$
>
> 2. **Shift time indices backward**: Evaluating this expression at $t-1$ and $t-2$ yields:
>
> $$
> d_0 x(t-1) u(t-1) = y(t) - (1 - d_1) y(t-1)
> $$
>
> $$
> d_0 x(t-2) = \frac{y(t-1) - (1 - d_1) y(t-2)}{u(t-2)}
> $$
>
> 3. **Rewrite the Collector Balance (5.17) at time $t-2$ and multiply by $d_0 u(t-1)$**:
>    First, collect $x(t)$ terms in (5.17):
>
> $$
> x(t+1) = (1 - d_3) x(t) + d_2 I(t) - d_0 x(t) u(t)
> $$
>
>    Shift time by $t \leftarrow t-2$:
>
> $$
> x(t-1) = (1 - d_3) x(t-2) + d_2 I(t-2) - d_0 x(t-2) u(t-2)
> $$
>
>    Multiply both sides by $d_0 u(t-1)$:
>
> $$
> d_0 x(t-1) u(t-1) = (1 - d_3) u(t-1) \big[ d_0 x(t-2) \big] + d_0 d_2 u(t-1) I(t-2) - d_0 u(t-1) \big[ d_0 x(t-2) u(t-2) \big]
> $$
>
> 4. **Substitute the storage-tank expressions for $d_0 x(t-1) u(t-1)$ and $d_0 x(t-2)$**:
>    - On the left-hand side, replace $d_0 x(t-1) u(t-1)$ with $y(t) - (1 - d_1) y(t-1)$.
>    - In the first right-hand term, replace $d_0 x(t-2)$ with $\frac{y(t-1) - (1 - d_1) y(t-2)}{u(t-2)}$.
>    - In the last right-hand term, replace $d_0 x(t-2) u(t-2)$ directly with $y(t-1) - (1 - d_1) y(t-2)$.
> 
>    Combining these and moving $(1 - d_1) y(t-1)$ to the right-hand side produces Equation (5.19) term-for-term!

Writing out the resulting input-output equation gives Equation (5.19):

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= (1 - d_1) y(t-1) + (1 - d_3) \frac{y(t-1) u(t-1)}{u(t-2)} \\
&\quad + (d_3 - 1)(1 - d_1) \frac{y(t-2) u(t-1)}{u(t-2)} + d_0 d_2 u(t-1) I(t-2) \\
&\quad - d_0 u(t-1) y(t-1) + d_0 (1 - d_1) u(t-1) y(t-2)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.19}
$$

##### Step 4: Reparameterization into a True Linear Regression
In Equation (5.19), the four underlying physical constants $(d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3)$ enter nonlinearly (via products like $(d_3 - 1)(1 - d_1)$, $d_0 d_2$, and $d_0(1 - d_1)$). However, if we define **six composite parameters** $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_6$ and **six corresponding nonlinear regressors** $\varphi_1(t), \dots, \varphi_6(t)$ as in Equation (5.20):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_1 &= (1 - d_1) & \varphi_1(t) &= y(t-1) \\
\theta_2 &= (1 - d_3) & \varphi_2(t) &= \frac{y(t-1) u(t-1)}{u(t-2)} \\
\theta_3 &= (d_3 - 1)(1 - d_1) & \varphi_3(t) &= \frac{y(t-2) u(t-1)}{u(t-2)} \\
\theta_4 &= d_0 d_2 & \varphi_4(t) &= u(t-1) I(t-2) \\
\theta_5 &= -d_0 & \varphi_5(t) &= u(t-1) y(t-1) \\
\theta_6 &= d_0 (1 - d_1) & \varphi_6(t) &= u(t-1) y(t-2)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.20}
$$

with stacked parameter and regressor vectors

$$
\theta^T = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_1 & \theta_2 & \dots & \theta_6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \varphi^T(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \varphi_1(t) & \varphi_2(t) & \dots & \varphi_6(t) \end{bmatrix}
$$

then Equation (5.19) reduces to a **true linear regression** (Equation 5.21):

$$
y(t) = \hat{y}(t \mid \theta) = \varphi^T(t) \theta \tag{5.21}
$$

**The Engineering Trade-Off of Reparameterization**:
- **What We Gained**: Notice that $\varphi(t)$ depends *only* on past measured signals $(y, u, I)$ and *not* on $\theta$. Thus, $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^6$ can be estimated globally in closed form via standard linear least squares!
- **What We Paid**: By estimating $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_6$ as six unconstrained, independent parameters, we discard the **exact algebraic constraints** linking them in Equation (5.20)—specifically, that $\theta_3 = -\theta_1 \theta_2$ and $\theta_6 = -\theta_1 \theta_5$ (since 4 physical parameters $d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3$ mapped into 6 regression coefficients). Despite ignoring these two nonlinear equality constraints, Ljung notes that the unconstrained 6-parameter linear regression (5.21) yields a remarkably accurate predictive model in practice (Ljung and Glad, 1994a).

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 — "Linear Regression" Does NOT Mean "Linear System"**: In system identification terminology, a *linear regression* $\hat{y}(t \mid \theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$ means the predictor is **linear in the parameter vector $\theta$**, NOT that the system dynamics are linear in $u(t)$ and $y(t)$. As Equations (5.15) and (5.20)–(5.21) prove, a highly nonlinear dynamical system (containing products $u(t-1)I(t-2)$ and ratios $y(t-1)u(t-1)/u(t-2)$) is still a linear regression as long as the nonlinear functions of past data contain no unknown parameters.
> - **Leap 2 — Why Hammerstein is Easier to Turn into a Linear Regression than Wiener**: In a Hammerstein model, the static nonlinearity $f(u(t))$ acts directly on the **measured input** $u(t)$. If $f(u) = \sum_{k=1}^m \alpha_k u^k$, the signals $u^k(t)$ are known and pass linearly through $B_k(q)$, yielding the linear regression (5.15). In a Wiener model, $y(t) = f(z(t))$ acts on an **unmeasured internal state** $z(t) = G(q,\theta)u(t)$, so the parameters of $G$ are trapped inside the nonlinearity $f(\cdot)$ unless $f$ is invertible.
> - **Notation Trap — Singularity When Dividing by $u(t-2)$ in Equation (5.19)**: Eliminating the unmeasured collector temperature $x(t)$ via Equation (5.18) required dividing by the delayed pump velocity $u(t-2)$ to form regressors $\varphi_2(t)$ and $\varphi_3(t)$. If the pump is ever completely shut off ($u(t-2) = 0$), these rational regressors blow up ($0/0$ or division by zero) because when the pump is off, the storage tank temperature $y(t)$ is completely decoupled from (and provides zero observability of) the collector temperature $x(t)$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. How many degrees of freedom are lost when reparameterizing the 4 physical parameters $(d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3)$ into the 6 unconstrained regression parameters $(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_6)$ in Equation (5.20), and what happens to parameter variance vs. computational simplicity?
>   2. When is it preferable to keep the physical parameterization $\hat{y}(t \mid d_0, d_1, d_2, d_3)$ (requiring iterative nonlinear optimization) versus lifting to the overparameterized linear regression $\hat{y}(t \mid \theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$?
