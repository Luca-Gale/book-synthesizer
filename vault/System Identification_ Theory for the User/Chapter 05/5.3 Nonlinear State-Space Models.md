# 5.3 Nonlinear State-Space Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: Having seen in Section 5.2 how specific physical nonlinearities can sometimes be cast into linear-in-parameters regressions via clever signal transformations, Section 5.3 addresses the general case where first-principles physical laws yield arbitrary **nonlinear state-space descriptions** (mechanistic or "white/grey-box" models). Ljung shows why exact probabilistic state prediction fails in the general nonlinear stochastic setting, re-anchors system identification around the universal predictor mapping $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$, and highlights the **nonlinear simulation model** (the nonlinear counterpart to the Output-Error structure) before transitioning to flexible black-box nonlinear structures in Section 5.4.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

When engineers model a physical, chemical, or mechanical process from first principles (Newton's laws, mass/energy balances, chemical reaction kinetics), the resulting equations almost always take the form of continuous-time or discrete-time **nonlinear state-space equations** parameterized by physical constants $\theta$ (such as activation energies, reaction orders, or friction coefficients).

However, moving from a generative stochastic nonlinear state-space model to a **one-step-ahead predictor** $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$—which is what we actually need to compute prediction errors $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$ for parameter estimation—hits a fundamental theoretical wall:
1. **The Curse of Nonlinear Stochastic Filtering**: In linear state-space models with Gaussian process noise $w(t)$ and measurement noise $v(t)$ (Chapter 4, Section 4.3), a Gaussian distribution mapped through a linear transformation remains Gaussian. Consequently, the conditional probability distribution of the hidden state $x(t)$ given past data $Z^{t-1}$ is completely characterized by just two finite-dimensional moments (its mean $\hat{x}(t)$ and covariance matrix $P(t)$), which are propagated exactly by the **Kalman filter**. Once the state transition $f(\cdot)$ or output map $h(\cdot)$ becomes *nonlinear*, a Gaussian distribution pushed through a nonlinear function ceases to be Gaussian. Tracking the exact conditional density $p(x(t) \mid Z^{t-1})$ requires propagating an entire infinite-dimensional probability density function at every time step (the nonlinear filtering problem), which has no finite-dimensional closed-form solution except in isolated special cases.
2. **Models as Predictors (The Pragmatic Escape)**: Rather than getting bogged down in intractable exact probabilistic filtering, Ljung reinforces the central philosophy introduced in Chapter 4: *for system identification purposes, a model is simply its predictor mapping* $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$. Whether we construct $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ via an approximate nonlinear filter (such as the Extended Kalman Filter) or via an *ad hoc* construction, once the mapping from past data $Z^{t-1}$ to the predicted output $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is specified, we can treat $(5.23)$ as the **basic model** and evaluate it directly via its prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$.
3. **Nonlinear Simulation Models (Output-Error Philosophy)**: The simplest and most widely used way to turn a physical nonlinear state-space model into a predictor is to **ignore the process noise** ($w(t) \equiv 0$) and simulate the noise-free state trajectory $x(t, \theta)$ driven purely by the measured inputs $u(t)$. Because this approach never requires feeding back noisy output measurements $y(t)$ through a nonlinear state observer, it works just as naturally for **continuous-time differential equations** $\dot{x}(t, \theta) = f(t, x(t, \theta), u(t), 0; \theta)$ solved numerically by an ODE integrator as it does for discrete-time state equations.

| $x(t)$ | True internal state vector of the system at time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ |
| $x(t, \theta)$ | Simulated (noise-free) state vector at time $t$ driven solely by $u(\cdot)$ for parameter vector $\theta$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ |
| $u(t)$ | Measured input vector at time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ |
| $y(t)$ | Measured output vector at time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}^p$ |

| $v(t)$ | Measurement disturbance (output noise) sequence of independent random variables | $\mathbb{R}^{n_v}$ |
| $\theta$ | Vector of unknown model parameters | $\mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $f(t, x, u, w; \theta)$ | Nonlinear state transition (or continuous-time state derivative) function | $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^{n_w} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ |
| $h(t, x, u, v; \theta)$ | Nonlinear output (measurement) function | $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^{n_v} \to \mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $Z^t$ | Complete history of input-output measurements up to time $t$: $(y^t, u^t) = (y(1), u(1), \dots, y(t), u(t))$ | $\mathbb{R}^{t(p+m)}$ |
| $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ | One-step-ahead predicted output at time $t$ given past data $Z^{t-1}$ and parameter vector $\theta$ | $\mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ | General nonlinear predictor mapping from past data $Z^{t-1}$ to the predicted output $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ | $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^{(t-1)(p+m)} \to \mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$ | Prediction error vector at time $t$: $y(t) - g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ | $\mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $\Lambda(t; \theta)$ | Parameterized covariance matrix of the prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$ | $\mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ (symmetric positive semidefinite) |
| $f_e(x, t; \theta)$ | Parameterized probability density function (PDF) of the prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$ evaluated at $x$ | $\mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ |
| $E_L$ | Arrhenius activation energy constant (divided by the gas constant $R$) in Example 5.2 | $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (Units: $\text{K}$) |
| $k_1, m, \alpha, \beta$ | Reaction rate constant ($k_1$) and reaction order exponents ($m, \alpha, \beta$) in Example 5.2 | $\mathbb{R}$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 A General Nonlinear State-Space Model Set and the Predictor Philosophy
The most general discrete-time description of a finite-dimensional stochastic dynamical system is the parameterized **nonlinear state-space model** (Eq. $5.22$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t + 1) &= f(t, x(t), u(t), w(t); \theta) \\
y(t) &= h(t, x(t), u(t), v(t); \theta)
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.22}
$$

- **Stochastic Assumptions on $(5.22)$**:
  1. $w(t)$ and $v(t)$ are sequences of **independent random variables** (white process noise and white measurement noise) with specified probability distributions.
  2. $\theta$ denotes a finite-dimensional vector of unknown parameters characterizing the system dynamics $f$, observation mechanism $h$, and/or noise statistics.

- **Why Exact Probabilistic Prediction Fails for $(5.22)$**:
  To identify $\theta$ within the prediction-error framework, we need to derive a predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ from $(5.22)$ based on the past input-output record:

$$
Z^t = (y^t, u^t) = \bigl(y(1), u(1), \dots, y(t), u(t)\bigr)
$$

  On formal probabilistic grounds (e.g., computing the conditional expectation $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \mathbb{E}[y(t) \mid Z^{t-1}; \theta]$), this nonlinear prediction problem is substantial: **it is known to have no finite-dimensional solution except in some isolated special cases**. Because nonlinear functions $f(\cdot)$ and $h(\cdot)$ distort probability distributions arbitrarily, exact recursive updating of the state distribution given $Z^{t-1}$ requires propagating an infinite number of moments (or solving partial differential/integral equations for the conditional density), as documented in the nonlinear filtering literature (e.g., Jazwinski, 1970; Anderson and Moore, 1979).

- **The Predictor as the Basic Model (Eqs. $5.23\text{--}5.24$)**:
  Despite the lack of an exact finite-dimensional optimal filter, practical predictors for $(5.22)$ can always be constructed—either via **approximations to the unrealizable optimal filter** (such as the Extended Kalman Filter, which linearizes $f$ and $h$ around the current state estimate) or via **ad hoc approaches** (such as deterministic simulation). Regardless of which route is taken, any resulting realizable predictor is a deterministic mapping from the available data history $Z^{t-1}$ to the predicted output at time $t$ (Eq. $5.23$):

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)
\tag{5.23}
$$

> [!important] Ljung's "Models as Predictors" Paradigm Extended to Nonlinear Systems
> Equation $(5.23)$ is the exact form in which the model is put to use for system identification. Therefore, Ljung emphasizes that we may view $(5.23)$ as the **basic model**, and *disregard the underlying state-space route* (like $(5.22)$) that led to $(5.23)$. This directly extends the philosophy of Chapter 4 to nonlinear systems: the only difference is that $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ in $(5.23)$ is a **nonlinear function of past data** $Z^{t-1}$, rather than a linear filter.

  Just as in Chapter 4 (cf. Eq. $4.7$), to form a complete probabilistic model structure, the deterministic predictor $(5.23)$ can be complemented with statistical assumptions about the associated **prediction error** (Eq. $5.24$):

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)
\tag{5.24}
$$

  such as specifying its parameterized covariance matrix $\Lambda(t; \theta)$ or its full probability density function (PDF) $f_e(x, t; \theta)$.

#### 2.2.2 Nonlinear Simulation Models (Discrete and Continuous Time)
A particularly simple, practical way to construct a predictor $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$ from the general state-space description $(5.22)$ is to **disregard the process noise** by setting $w(t) = 0$ and setting the measurement noise to zero ($v(t) = 0$) in the output equation (Eq. $5.25$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t + 1, \theta) &= f\bigl(t, x(t, \theta), u(t), 0; \theta\bigr) \\
\hat{y}(t|\theta) &= h\bigl(t, x(t, \theta), u(t), 0; \theta\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.25}
$$

- **Why It Is Called a Simulation Model**:
  Notice how $x(t, \theta)$ in $(5.25)$ is generated: starting from an initial state $x(0, \theta)$, the state trajectory is computed recursively using **only the actual measured input sequence** $\{u(s)\}_{s=1}^{t}$ and the candidate parameter vector $\theta$. Past measured outputs $\{y(s)\}$ are *never* fed back to correct the state $x(t, \theta)$. Thus, $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is constructed purely by **simulating a noise-free model** $(5.22)$ driven by the true input:
  - In the terminology of Chapter 4, $(5.25)$ is the exact nonlinear counterpart of an **Output-Error (OE) model** (where all disturbances are implicitly attributed to additive output/measurement error between $y(t)$ and the noise-free simulated output $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$).
  - *Notation Note*: In $(5.22)$ and $(5.25)$, Ljung includes $u(t)$ inside $h(t, x(t), u(t), \dots)$ to allow for a direct feedthrough term (or equivalently, in purely causal one-step-ahead prediction $g(t, Z^{t-1}; \theta)$, either $h$ does not depend on $u(t)$ or $u(t)$ is known at the time $y(t)$ is evaluated in simulation).

- **Continuous-Time Nonlinear Simulation Model (Eq. $5.26$)**:
  Because a simulation model does not require stochastic state filtering or discrete-time innovation feedback, it is almost as easy to use when starting directly from a **continuous-time physical representation** (Eq. $5.26$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt} x(t, \theta) &= f\bigl(t, x(t, \theta), u(t), 0; \theta\bigr) \\
\hat{y}(t|\theta) &= h\bigl(t, x(t, \theta), u(t), 0; \theta\bigr)
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.26}
$$

  Here, for any candidate parameter vector $\theta$, an ordinary differential equation (ODE) solver integrates $\frac{d}{dt}x(t, \theta)$ forward in time using the measured input trajectory $u(t)$, and $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is evaluated at the discrete sampling instants where measurements $y(t)$ are taken.

#### 2.2.3 Walkthrough of Example 5.2: Delignification (Cellulose Cooking in Paper Pulp Production)
To illustrate how a continuous-time nonlinear simulation model $(5.26)$ arises naturally in chemical engineering, Ljung presents the industrial process of **delignification** (originally studied by Hagberg and Schöön, 1974)—reducing the lignin content of wood chips in a chemical mixture during cellulose cooking to produce paper pulp.

1. **Physical State and Input Variables**:
   - **State / Output variable**:
     - $x(t)$: lignin concentration in the wood chips at time $t$. Because the lignin concentration is also the measured system output, $y(t)$ measures $x(t)$ directly.
   - **Three Measured Inputs** $\{u_i(t), \, i = 1, 2, 3\}$:
     - $u_1(t)$: absolute temperature of the chemical mixture at time $t$.
     - $u_2(t)$: concentration of hydrogen sulfite ions, $[\text{HSO}_3^-]$, at time $t$.
     - $u_3(t)$: concentration of hydrogen ions, $[\text{H}^+]$, at time $t$.

2. **First-Principles Chemical Kinetics Law (Eq. $5.27$)**:
   Basic chemical reaction kinetics (combining the Arrhenius temperature dependence $e^{-E_L / u_1(t)}$ with power-law reaction orders for the reactants) govern the rate of lignin breakdown:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} x(t) = -k_1 e^{-E_L / u_1(t)} [x(t)]^m \cdot [u_2(t)]^\alpha \cdot [u_3(t)]^\beta
\tag{5.27}
$$

   where:
   - $E_L$ is the Arrhenius activation energy constant,
   - $k_1$ is the pre-exponential reaction rate constant,
   - $m, \alpha, \beta$ are the reaction order exponents associated with lignin concentration $x(t)$, $[\text{HSO}_3^-]$, and $[\text{H}^+]$, respectively.

3. **Casting $(5.27)$ as a Continuous-Time Nonlinear Simulation Model $(5.26)$**:
   - Collect the five unknown physical constants into the parameter vector:

$$
\theta^T = \begin{bmatrix} E_L & k_1 & m & \alpha & \beta \end{bmatrix}
$$

   - For any trial parameter vector $\theta$, numerically integrating the differential equation $(5.27)$ driven by the measured input trajectories $\{u_i(t), \, i = 1, 2, 3\}$ produces a simulated trajectory of lignin concentrations $\{x(t, \theta)\}$.
   - Since the system output is the lignin concentration itself, the output map $h(\cdot)$ is simply the identity:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = x(t, \theta)
$$

   - Comparing these simulated values $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = x(t, \theta)$ against the actually measured lignin concentrations $y(t)$ yields the prediction errors $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - x(t, \theta)$, allowing any candidate parameter vector $\theta$ to be evaluated and optimized (e.g., via nonlinear least squares).

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Conceptual Leap — Why Disregarding Process Noise $w(t)$ Is Such a Big Deal**: In $(5.22)$, if $w(t) \neq 0$, the true state $x(t)$ undergoes random shocks at every step. If you do not feed back past outputs $y(s)$ to track those shocks, an open-loop simulation $x(t, \theta)$ will drift away from $x(t)$ (or even fail if the open-loop dynamics are unstable). However, if the physical system is open-loop stable and disturbances act mainly as measurement errors on $y(t)$ rather than driving the chemical/physical state $x(t)$, setting $w(t) = 0$ in $(5.25)\text{--}(5.26)$ completely sidesteps the infinite-dimensional nonlinear filtering problem!
> - **Notation Trap 1 — Overloaded $x(t)$ vs. $x(t, \theta)$**: In $(5.22)$, $x(t)$ is the **true stochastic state** of the system (a random variable driven by $w(t)$). In $(5.25)$ and $(5.26)$, $x(t, \theta)$ is a **deterministic simulated state** that depends strictly on the input history $u^t$ and the candidate parameter vector $\theta$. Similarly, in $f_e(x, t; \theta)$ right below $(5.24)$, $x$ is just a dummy integration/evaluation variable for the PDF of $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$, *not* the state vector!
> - **Notation Trap 2 — Discrete-Time vs. Continuous-Time $f(\cdot)$**: Ljung uses the same symbol $f(t, x, u, 0; \theta)$ for the next-state map $x(t+1, \theta)$ in discrete time $(5.25)$ and for the time-derivative $\frac{d}{dt}x(t, \theta)$ in continuous time $(5.26)$. Always check the left-hand side ($x(t+1, \theta)$ vs. $\frac{d}{dt}x(t, \theta)$) to know whether $f$ represents a discrete transition or a vector field.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *Can Example 5.2 $(5.27)$ be turned into a linear regression like Example 5.1 by taking logarithms?* Notice that taking $\ln(-\dot{x}(t))$ would linearize $\ln k_1 - E_L/u_1(t) + m\ln x(t) + \alpha\ln u_2(t) + \beta\ln u_3(t)$, **except** that $\dot{x}(t)$ is not directly measured and lignin concentration $x(t)$ is usually sampled sparsely and noisily during a batch cook—making numerical differentiation $\dot{x}(t)$ wildly inaccurate compared to integrating $(5.27)$ as a simulation model!
>   2. *What if we have no physical insight to write down $(5.26)$ or $(5.27)$?* That question motivates Section 5.4: how to parameterize $g(Z^{t-1}, \theta)$ as a flexible **nonlinear black-box model**.
