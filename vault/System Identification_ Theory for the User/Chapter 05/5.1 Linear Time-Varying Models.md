# 5.1 Linear Time-Varying Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: After Chapter 4 established model structures for linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, Chapter 5 expands the modeling framework to **time-varying** and **nonlinear** dynamical systems. Section 5.1 bridges LTI theory and nonlinear reality by showing how the LTI impulse-response and state-space models generalize to **Linear Time-Varying (LTV)** forms—and proves why LTV models naturally arise even in time-invariant or nonlinear physical plants via **non-uniform/multirate sampling** and **first-order Taylor linearization around a nominal trajectory**.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

### 1.1 The Chapter 5 Roadmap: Beyond Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) Models
While linear, time-invariant (LTI) models form the backbone of system identification, physical engineering systems frequently violate time-invariance or linearity. Chapter 5 organizes the progression from structured, physically motivated extensions to general black-box nonlinear predictors:
1. **Section 5.1 (Linear Time-Varying Models)**: Preserves *linearity* (superposition of responses to scaled inputs) while dropping *time-invariance*, allowing impulse responses and state-space matrices to evolve explicitly with time index $t$.
2. **Section 5.2 (Models with Nonlinearities)**: Introduces *semi-physical* static nonlinearities at the input and/or output (Hammerstein, Wiener, and Wiener-Hammerstein block-oriented models) and nonlinear transformations of raw data.
3. **Section 5.3 (Nonlinear State-Space Models)**: Incorporates physical insight into nonlinear state transitions and output equations.
4. **Section 5.4–5.6 (Black-Box Nonlinear Models)**: Moves to universal function approximators (neural networks, wavelets, radial basis functions in Section 5.5, and fuzzy models in Section 5.6).
5. **Section 5.7 (Formal Characterization of Models)**: Generalizes the LTI predictor framework of Section 4.5 to arbitrary nonlinear predictors.

### 1.2 Why Do Linear Time-Varying (LTV) Models Arise in Practice?
Before diving into general nonlinearities, Ljung introduces LTV models because they retain the tractable algebraic structure of linear predictors (closed-form state recursion and linear impulse-response superposition) while capturing three ubiquitous engineering scenarios:
- **Slowly Varying Operating Conditions or Aging Plants**: A chemical reactor whose catalyst activity degrades over time, or an aircraft whose mass decreases as fuel burns, behaves linearly for small perturbations at any instant $t$, but with impulse response weights $g_t(k)$ that depend on the current time $t$.
- **Non-Uniform Sampling, Multirate Sensing, and Missing Data**: Even if an underlying continuous-time physical system is strictly *time-invariant*, sampling it at unequal time intervals $t_k$ (so the sampling period $T_k = t_{k+1} - t_k$ varies with $k$), reading different sensors at different clock rates (multirate systems), or dropping intermittent output measurements transforms the discrete-time state-space matrices into explicit functions of $t$.
- **Linearization of a Nonlinear System Around a Trajectory**: If a nonlinear spacecraft, robotic arm, or batch chemical process tracks a time-varying reference input $u^*(t)$ and nominal state trajectory $x^*(t)$, small deviations $\Delta x(t) = x(t) - x^*(t)$ and $\Delta u(t) = u(t) - u^*(t)$ obey a **linear time-varying** state-space model whose Jacobian matrices $F(t), G(t), H(t)$ change at every time step along the trajectory.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Weighting Function & Time-Varying Transfer Operator
In Chapter 2, a **linear system** was defined fundamentally by the principle of superposition: a linear combination of input sequences produces the exact same linear combination of corresponding output responses (up to additive disturbances). Dropping the assumption of time-invariance while preserving causality and linearity yields the general linear input-output description `(5.1)`:

$$
y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g_t(k) u(t - k) + v(t) \tag{5.1}
$$

Here, the impulse response sequence $g_t(k)$ carries an explicit subscript $t$ because the system's sensitivity to an input applied $k$ steps ago depends on the *current observation time* $t$.

To express this in terms of the **absolute time $s$ at which the input pulse was applied** (where $s = t - k$), Ljung defines the **weighting function** $\tilde{g}(t, s)$ via the relation:

$$
g_t(k) = \tilde{g}(t, t - k)
$$

Substituting $s = t - k$ (so as $k$ runs from $1$ to $\infty$, $s$ runs from $t - 1$ down to $-\infty$) transforms `(5.1)` into `(5.2)`:

$$
y(t) = \sum_{s = -\infty}^{t - 1} \tilde{g}(t, s) u(s) + v(t) \tag{5.2}
$$

- **Physical Interpretation of the Weighting Function**: $\tilde{g}(t, s)$ for $t = s, s+1, \dots$ is the output response observed at time $t$ generated by a unit impulse input applied at time $s$. It is called the *weighting function* because it specifies the exact weight that the past input $u(s)$ at time $s$ carries in forming the current output $y(t)$.
- **Connection to LTI Systems**: Compare `(5.1)` with the LTI model `(2.8)`: a system is time-invariant if and only if $\tilde{g}(t, s)$ depends only on the elapsed time $k = t - s$, i.e., $\tilde{g}(t, s) = g(t - s)$ so $g_t(k) = g(k)$ is independent of $t$.

Just as in the LTI case, we can formally introduce a **time-varying transfer function** (or transfer operator) $G_t(q)$ at time $t$ as a formal power series in the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$:

$$
G_t(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g_t(k) q^{-k} \tag{5.3}
$$

While one could repeat the polynomial difference-equation and rational transfer-function developments of Section 4.2 for $G_t(q)$, Ljung emphasizes a key practical insight: **in practice, it is much easier and more natural to handle time variation in state-space form** than in transfer-function form (where non-commutativity of time-varying polynomials in $q^{-1}$ complicates algebra).

#### 2.2.2 Time-Varying State-Space Models & One-Step-Ahead Predictor Derivation
Starting from the LTI innovations state-space representation `(4.91)`, we obtain a **time-varying state-space model** `(5.4)` simply by allowing the system matrices to depend explicitly on the time index $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t + 1, \theta) &= A_t(\theta) x(t, \theta) + B_t(\theta) u(t) + K_t(\theta) e(t) \\
y(t) &= C_t(\theta) x(t, \theta) + e(t)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4}
$$

To derive the corresponding **one-step-ahead predictor** $\hat{y}(t \mid \theta)$ (analogous to `(4.86)` in Chapter 4), we replace the unknown future/current white innovation $e(t)$ by its reconstructed value from the output equation, $e(t) = y(t) - C_t(\theta) \hat{x}(t, \theta)$:

1. Substitute $e(t) = y(t) - C_t(\theta) \hat{x}(t, \theta)$ into the state update equation:

$$
\hat{x}(t + 1, \theta) = A_t(\theta) \hat{x}(t, \theta) + B_t(\theta) u(t) + K_t(\theta) \big[ y(t) - C_t(\theta) \hat{x}(t, \theta) \big]
$$

2. Group the terms multiplying $\hat{x}(t, \theta)$ to obtain the time-varying observer/predictor state recursion `(5.5)`:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{x}(t + 1, \theta) &= \big[ A_t(\theta) - K_t(\theta) C_t(\theta) \big] \hat{x}(t, \theta) + B_t(\theta) u(t) + K_t(\theta) y(t) \\
\hat{y}(t \mid \theta) &= C_t(\theta) \hat{x}(t, \theta)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.5}
$$

3. **Unrolling the State Recursion into Infinite-Impulse-Response Predictor Form**:
   To express $\hat{y}(t \mid \theta)$ directly in terms of all past inputs $\{u(t-k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$ and past outputs $\{y(t-k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$, define the closed-loop predictor transition matrix at step $j$ as $\Phi_j(\theta) \triangleq A_j(\theta) - K_j(\theta) C_j(\theta)$. Unrolling `(5.5)` backward from $t$ to $-\infty$:
   - At step $t-1$: $\hat{x}(t, \theta) = \Phi_{t-1}(\theta) \hat{x}(t-1, \theta) + B_{t-1}(\theta) u(t-1) + K_{t-1}(\theta) y(t-1)$.
   - At step $t-2$: $\hat{x}(t-1, \theta)$ contributes $\Phi_{t-1}(\theta) \big[ B_{t-2}(\theta) u(t-2) + K_{t-2}(\theta) y(t-2) \big]$, and so forth.
   - In general, an input $u(t-k)$ and output $y(t-k)$ entering at time $t-k$ through $B_{t-k}(\theta)$ and $K_{t-k}(\theta)$ are propagated forward through the ordered product of closed-loop predictor matrices from $j = t-k$ to $j = t-1$, and finally mapped to the output at time $t$ via $C_t(\theta)$.

   This yields the explicit linear predictor expansion `(5.6)`:

$$
\hat{y}(t \mid \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} w_t^u(k, \theta) u(t - k) + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} w_t^y(k, \theta) y(t - k) \tag{5.6}
$$

   where the time-varying predictor weight sequences $w_t^u(k, \theta)$ and $w_t^y(k, \theta)$ are given by `(5.7)`:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_t^u(k, \theta) &= C_t(\theta) \prod_{j = t - k}^{t - 1} \big[ A_j(\theta) - K_j(\theta) C_j(\theta) \big] B_{t - k}(\theta) \\
w_t^y(k, \theta) &= C_t(\theta) \prod_{j = t - k}^{t - 1} \big[ A_j(\theta) - K_j(\theta) C_j(\theta) \big] K_{t - k}(\theta)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.7}
$$

> [!important] Matrix Multiplication Order in `(5.7)`
> Because matrix multiplication is non-commutative, the product $\prod_{j = t - k}^{t - 1} \big[ A_j(\theta) - K_j(\theta) C_j(\theta) \big]$ represents the **time-ordered state transition matrix** from time $t - k + 1$ to time $t$:
>
> $$
> \prod_{j = t - k}^{t - 1} \Phi_j(\theta) = \Phi_{t-1}(\theta) \Phi_{t-2}(\theta) \cdots \Phi_{t-k}(\theta)
> $$
>
> For $k = 1$, the product index range $j = t-1$ to $t-1$ contains no intermediate transitions (an empty product equal to the identity matrix $I$), giving $w_t^u(1, \theta) = C_t(\theta) B_{t-1}(\theta)$ and $w_t^y(1, \theta) = C_t(\theta) K_{t-1}(\theta)$.

**Alternative Form via Process & Measurement Noise Covariances**:
Instead of parameterizing the innovation gain $K_t(\theta)$ directly as in `(5.4)`, one can start from the state-space model with separate process noise $w(t)$ and measurement noise $v(t)$—analogous to `(4.84)` and `(4.85)`—where the system matrices $A, B, C$ and noise covariance/cross-covariance matrices $R_1, R_{12}, R_2$ are all explicit functions of $t$. The optimal one-step-ahead predictor is then still given directly by the time-varying Kalman filter equations `(4.94)` and `(4.95)` (the Riccati recursion already accommodates time-varying matrices naturally!).

#### 2.2.3 How Time-Invariant Plants Become LTV: Non-Uniform Sampling, Multirate Systems & Missing Data
Ljung highlights a crucial structural phenomenon: **two very common problems associated with underlying time-invariant continuous-time systems directly produce discrete-time LTV models**:
1. **Non-Equal Sampling Intervals ($T_k = t_{k+1} - t_k \neq \text{const}$)**:
   Suppose the underlying continuous-time LTI state-space model `(4.62)`–`(4.63)` is sampled at irregular time instants $t = t_k$ ($k = 1, 2, \dots$), where the input is held constant over $[t_k, t_{k+1})$. The exact zero-order-hold sampling formulas `(4.66)`–`(4.68)` still hold for stepping from $t_k$ to $t_{k+1}$ using the instantaneous sampling interval $T_k = t_{k+1} - t_k$:

$$
F(T_k) = e^{A T_k}, \qquad G(T_k) = \int_0^{T_k} e^{A \tau} B \, d\tau
$$

   Because $T_k$ varies with $k$, the resulting discrete-time system `(4.67)` has time-varying matrices $F_k = F(T_k)$ and $G_k = G(T_k)$—making it a **linear time-varying system** even though the physical plant is time-invariant!
2. **Multirate Systems & Missing Output Data**:
   - In a **multirate system**, different output variables (sensors) are sampled at different clock rates. At any fundamental discrete time instant $t$, only a subset of outputs is measured. This is modeled exactly by letting the output observation matrix $C_t(\theta)$ in `(5.4)` be time-varying so that at instant $t$ it selects (picks out) only those state combinations corresponding to the sensors active at time $t$.
   - Similarly, **missing output data** (intermittent sensor dropouts) is simply a special case of non-uniform sampling where $C_t(\theta)$ drops rows (or becomes empty) at the instants $t$ where measurements are lost (see Section 14.2).

#### 2.2.4 Linearization of Nonlinear Systems Around a Trajectory
Perhaps the single most important source of LTV models in control and system identification is the **perturbation linearization of a nonlinear system around a time-varying nominal trajectory**.

Consider a general discrete-time nonlinear state-space system `(5.8)`:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t + 1) &= f\big(x(t), u(t)\big) + r\big(x(t), u(t)\big) w(t) \\
y(t) &= h\big(x(t)\big) + m\big(x(t), u(t)\big) v(t)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.8}
$$

**Explicit Assumptions**:
1. **Small White Disturbances**: The process disturbance sequence $\{w(t)\}$ and measurement disturbance sequence $\{v(t)\}$ are **white noise processes** with **small amplitudes**.
2. **Known Nominal Disturbance-Free Trajectory**: In the absence of disturbances ($w(t) \equiv 0$ and $v(t) \equiv 0$), applying a nominal input sequence $u^*(t)$ generates a nominal state trajectory $x^*(t)$ satisfying the deterministic recursion:

$$
x^*(t + 1) = f\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)
$$

   with nominal disturbance-free output $y^*(t) = h\big(x^*(t)\big)$.
3. **Differentiability & Small Deviations**: $f(x, u)$ and $h(x)$ are continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of $(x^*(t), u^*(t))$, and the actual trajectory stays close enough to the nominal trajectory that higher-order Taylor terms ($O(\|\Delta x\|^2, \|\Delta u\|^2)$) and cross-products between state/input perturbations and the small noise terms (such as $\Delta x(t) \cdot v(t)$ or $\Delta x(t) \cdot w(t)$) can be neglected.

**Step-by-Step Perturbation Derivation**:
Define the perturbation variables (deviations from the nominal trajectory):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x(t) &= x(t) - x^*(t) \\
\Delta y(t) &= y(t) - h\big(x^*(t)\big) \\
\Delta u(t) &= u(t) - u^*(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

1. **First-Order Taylor Expansion of the State Equation**:
   Expand $f\big(x(t), u(t)\big)$ and $r\big(x(t), u(t)\big) w(t)$ around $\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t + 1) &= f\big(x^*(t) + \Delta x(t), u^*(t) + \Delta u(t)\big) + r\big(x^*(t) + \Delta x(t), u^*(t) + \Delta u(t)\big) w(t) \\
&\approx \underbrace{f\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)}_{x^*(t+1)} + \left. \frac{\partial f(x, u)}{\partial x} \right|_{x^*(t), u^*(t)} \Delta x(t) + \left. \frac{\partial f(x, u)}{\partial u} \right|_{x^*(t), u^*(t)} \Delta u(t) \\
&\quad + r\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) w(t) + \underbrace{O(\Delta x \cdot w, \Delta u \cdot w)}_{\text{neglected cross terms}}
\end{aligned}
$$

   Subtracting $x^*(t + 1) = f\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$ from both sides gives the linearized state perturbation update in `(5.9)`.

2. **First-Order Taylor Expansion of the Output Equation**:
   Expand $h\big(x(t)\big)$ and $m\big(x(t), u(t)\big) v(t)$ around $\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= h\big(x^*(t) + \Delta x(t)\big) + m\big(x^*(t) + \Delta x(t), u^*(t) + \Delta u(t)\big) v(t) \\
&\approx h\big(x^*(t)\big) + \left. \frac{\partial h(x)}{\partial x} \right|_{x^*(t)} \Delta x(t) + m\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) v(t) + \underbrace{O(\Delta x \cdot v, \Delta u \cdot v)}_{\text{neglected cross terms}}
\end{aligned}
$$

   Subtracting $h\big(x^*(t)\big)$ from both sides yields the complete **Linear Time-Varying (LTV) Perturbation Model** `(5.9)`:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x(t + 1) &= F(t) \Delta x(t) + G(t) \Delta u(t) + \bar{w}(t) \\
\Delta y(t) &= H(t) \Delta x(t) + \bar{v}(t)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.9}
$$

   where the time-varying Jacobian matrices are evaluated along the nominal trajectory:

$$
F(t) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial x} f(x, u) \right|_{x^*(t), u^*(t)}, \qquad G(t) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial u} f(x, u) \right|_{x^*(t), u^*(t)}, \qquad H(t) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial x} h(x) \right|_{x^*(t)}
$$

   and the effective linearized white disturbance vectors are:

$$
\bar{w}(t) = r\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) w(t), \qquad \bar{v}(t) = m\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) v(t)
$$

3. **Time-Varying Noise Covariance Structure `(5.10)`**:
   Because $w(t)$ and $v(t)$ are white noises modulated by the nominal trajectory functions $r\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$ and $m\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$, the resulting disturbances $\bar{w}(t)$ and $\bar{v}(t)$ in `(5.9)` remain **white noise sequences**, but with **time-varying covariance and cross-covariance matrices** `(5.10)`:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1(t) &= E \, \bar{w}(t) \bar{w}^T(t) = r\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) \, E w(t) w^T(t) \, r^T\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) \\
R_2(t) &= E \, \bar{v}(t) \bar{v}^T(t) = m\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) \, E v(t) v^T(t) \, m^T\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) \\
R_{12}(t) &= E \, \bar{w}(t) \bar{v}^T(t) = r\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big) \, E w(t) v^T(t) \, m^T\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)
\end{aligned} \tag{5.10}
$$

Thus, in a neighborhood of the nominal trajectory $\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$, the nonlinear stochastic system `(5.8)` is approximated by the LTV state-space model `(5.9)`–`(5.10)`, which fits directly into the time-varying Kalman predictor framework of Section 5.1!

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Notation Trap — Two Indexing Conventions for Impulse Responses ($g_t(k)$ vs. $\tilde{g}(t, s)$)**:
>   Be careful not to confuse the second argument of $g_t(k)$ with the second argument of $\tilde{g}(t, s)$. In $g_t(k)$, $k = 1, 2, \dots$ is the **relative lag** (how many steps ago the input occurred relative to $t$). In $\tilde{g}(t, s)$, $s = t - k$ is the **absolute clock time** when the unit pulse was injected. They are linked by $g_t(k) = \tilde{g}(t, t - k)$.
> - **Conceptual Leap — Why State-Space Dominates LTV Identification**:
>   In LTI systems (Chapter 4), polynomial transfer-function models (ARX, ARMAX, OE, BJ) and state-space models are equally convenient because constant polynomials in $q^{-1}$ commute ($A(q) B(q) = B(q) A(q)$). For LTV systems, time-varying operators $G_t(q)$ do *not* commute with time-varying coefficients ($q^{-1} a(t) u(t) = a(t-1) u(t-1) \neq a(t) q^{-1} u(t)$). In contrast, state-space recursions `(5.4)`–`(5.5)` and the time-varying Kalman filter require **zero structural changes** when matrices become functions of $t$.
> - **Subtlety in Linearization — Why Cross-Terms Like $\Delta x(t) \cdot v(t)$ Vanish**:
>   Notice that in `(5.8)`, the noise modulation matrices $r(x(t), u(t))$ and $m(x(t), u(t))$ depend on the *actual* state $x(t)$ and input $u(t)$, whereas in `(5.10)` they are evaluated at the *nominal* trajectory $\big(x^*(t), u^*(t)\big)$. A first-order expansion of $m(x(t), u(t)) v(t)$ around $x^*(t)$ produces $\big[ m(x^*, u^*) + \frac{\partial m}{\partial x} \Delta x(t) \big] v(t)$. Under the small-disturbance assumption, both $\Delta x(t)$ and $v(t)$ are first-order small quantities, making their product $\Delta x(t) \cdot v(t)$ a **second-order small term** that is neglected alongside $O(\|\Delta x\|^2)$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *How does non-uniform sampling turn a constant continuous-time matrix $A$ into a time-varying discrete-time matrix $F_k = e^{A T_k}$?*
>   2. *If a nonlinear system is linearized around a constant equilibrium point $(x^*, u^*)$ rather than a time-varying trajectory $(x^*(t), u^*(t))$, what happens to $F(t), G(t), H(t)$ and $R_1(t), R_2(t), R_{12}(t)$ in `(5.9)`–`(5.10)`?* (They collapse back to standard LTI matrices!)
