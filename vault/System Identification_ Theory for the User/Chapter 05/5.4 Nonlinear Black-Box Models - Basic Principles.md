# 5.4 Nonlinear Black-Box Models: Basic Principles

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 5**: After Section 5.3 established how physical insight yields tailored nonlinear state-space and grey-box models, Section 5.4 addresses the opposite scenario—when we have *no prior physical insight* into the nonlinearities of the system. It establishes a unified mathematical framework that decomposes every nonlinear black-box model into two orthogonal design choices: (1) constructing a finite-dimensional regression vector $\varphi(t)$ from past data (mirroring linear ARX, ARMAX, OE, and FIR structures), and (2) parameterizing a flexible static nonlinear function expansion $g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k g_k(\varphi)$ using dilated and translated versions of a single scalar "mother basis function" $\kappa(x)$—providing the theoretical foundation for the neural networks, wavelet networks, and fuzzy models surveyed in Section 5.5.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

When a system exhibits strong nonlinearities (such as saturation, dead-zones, hysteresis, or operating-point-dependent gains) and first-principles modeling (Section 5.3) is either intractable or too costly, we need a **nonlinear black-box model structure** capable of approximating "all kinds of reasonable system behavior."

At first glance, the general nonlinear predictor from Section 5.3,

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(Z^{t-1}, \theta)
$$

seems hopelessly open-ended: $Z^{t-1}$ is an ever-growing sequence of past input-output measurements, and the space of arbitrary nonlinear mappings on an expanding domain is infinite-dimensional. Ljung tames this complexity through a crucial **two-stage architectural decomposition**:

1. **Dynamic Compression via Regressors ($Z^{t-1} \mapsto \varphi(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$)**:
   Instead of feeding the entire growing history $Z^{t-1}$ directly into an arbitrary nonlinear function, we first compress the relevant dynamical history into a fixed-dimension **regression vector** $\varphi(t) = \varphi(Z^{t-1}, \theta) \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Because this step only concerns *which* past signals carry the system's memory, it carries over directly from linear black-box modeling (Chapter 4). Choosing lagged inputs $u(t-k)$ and measured outputs $y(t-k)$ gives **NARX** (Nonlinear ARX) or **NFIR** structures; incorporating past model predictions $\hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$ or residuals $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$ yields **NOE** (Nonlinear Output Error), **NARMAX**, or **NBJ** structures.
2. **Static Nonlinear Function Approximation ($\varphi(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d \mapsto g(\varphi(t), \theta) \in \mathbb{R}^p$)**:
   Once $\varphi(t)$ is fixed in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the dynamical identification problem reduces to a standard **multivariate function approximation** problem: how to parameterize a flexible surface $g(\varphi, \theta)$ over $\mathbb{R}^d$. Rather than inventing disparate ad-hoc formulas, almost all modern nonlinear black-box models (radial basis networks, feedforward neural networks, wavelet estimators, spline models) use a **linear-in-coordinates basis expansion**:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k g_k(\varphi)
$$

   Furthermore, rather than designing $d$-dimensional basis functions $g_k(\varphi)$ from scratch, they are generated from a **single scalar "mother basis function"** $\kappa(x)$ (such as a Gaussian bell, unit step, or sigmoid) through three geometric operations:
   - **Dilation / Scale ($\beta_k$)**: Controls how sharply or broadly the basis function responds (frequency in Fourier series, inverse interval width $1/\Delta$ in piecewise-constant grids, or inverse variance in Gaussians).
   - **Translation / Location ($\gamma_k$)**: Moves the active region of the basis function to a specific center point or hyperplane in the regressor space $\mathbb{R}^d$.
   - **Multivariate Lifting ($d > 1$)**: Extends the 1D function $\kappa(x)$ to $\mathbb{R}^d$ via **tensor products** (separable coordinate-wise products), **radial constructions** (distance $\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k}$ from a center point $\gamma_k$), or **ridge constructions** (projection $\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k$ onto a direction vector, constant along hyperplanes).

Why does Ljung unify these under a single equation before naming "neural networks" or "wavelets" in Section 5.5? Because doing so strips away the domain-specific jargon and exposes the fundamental trade-offs shared by all of them:
- **Linear vs. Nonlinear Parameters**: Fixing the scale and location parameters $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$ makes $g(\varphi, \theta)$ a **linear regression** in the coordinate weights $\alpha_k$, whereas optimizing $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$ adaptively to match the target function $g_0(\varphi)$ drastically reduces the number of basis terms $n$ needed when $g_0(\varphi)$ is smooth over most of the space and steep only locally (Example 5.3).
- **The Curse of Dimensionality**: For local radial basis expansions of an $s$-times differentiable function on $\mathbb{R}^d$, achieving uniform accuracy $\delta \ll 1$ requires $n \sim \delta^{-d/s}$ basis functions—growing **exponentially** with the number of regressors $d$.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 2.2.1 Decomposition of the General Nonlinear Mapping via Regressors

Recall from Eq. (5.23) that a general nonlinear model maps past data $Z^{t-1}$ to the predicted output space. Omitting explicit time dependence $t$ in the mapping $g$ (assuming time-invariance of the underlying nonlinear mechanism), Ljung writes **(Eq. 5.28)**:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(Z^{t-1}, \theta) \tag{5.28}
$$

Because $Z^{t-1}$ grows in dimension as time $t$ progresses, Eq. (5.28) is too general to parameterize directly. We decompose $g$ as the concatenation of two mappings **(Eq. 5.29)**:

$$
g(Z^{t-1}, \theta) = g(\varphi(t), \theta) \tag{5.29}
$$

where the first mapping compresses the expanding observation history $Z^{t-1}$ into a finite-dimensional **regression vector** $\varphi(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of fixed dimension $d$ **(Eq. 5.30)**:

$$
\varphi(t) = \varphi(Z^{t-1}) \tag{5.30}
$$

whose components $\varphi_j(t)$ are called **regressors**. More generally, the formation of the regressors may itself depend on the model parameters $\theta$ (for example, when past simulated model outputs $\hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$ or past prediction errors $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$ are used as regressors) **(Eq. 5.31)**:

$$
\varphi(t) = \varphi(Z^{t-1}, \theta) \tag{5.31}
$$

which is abbreviated as $\varphi(t, \theta)$ (cf. Eq. 4.40), though the explicit argument $\theta$ in $\varphi(t)$ is suppressed whenever not essential to the discussion.

> [!important] Fundamental Two-Step Decomposition for Dynamical Systems
> By introducing $\varphi(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, designing a nonlinear black-box model (Eq. 5.28) splits into two distinct subproblems:
> 1. **Dynamic Regressor Selection**: How to choose the regression vector $\varphi(t)$ from past inputs $u(t-k)$, past measured outputs $y(t-k)$, and possibly past model outputs $\hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$ or prediction errors $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$.
> 2. **Static Nonlinear Mapping Parameterization**: How to choose the nonlinear mapping $g(\varphi, \theta) : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^p$ from the regressor space to the output space.

Analogously to the linear black-box classification in Table 4.1 (Equations 4.11, 4.20, and 4.40), the choice of regressors defines the dynamical family:
- **NFIR (Nonlinear Finite Impulse Response)**: $\varphi(t)$ contains only past measured inputs $u(t-k)$.
- **NARX (Nonlinear ARX)**: $\varphi(t)$ contains only measured quantities—past inputs $u(t-k)$ and past measured outputs $y(t-k)$. Because $\varphi(t) = \varphi(Z^{t-1})$ does not depend on $\theta$, **NFIR and NARX are the most common choices** in nonlinear black-box identification.
- **NOE (Nonlinear Output Error)**: $\varphi(t, \theta)$ uses past inputs $u(t-k)$ and past *undisturbed/simulated model outputs* $\hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$.
- **NARMAX (Nonlinear ARMAX)** and **NBJ (Nonlinear Box-Jenkins)**: $\varphi(t, \theta)$ incorporates past prediction errors $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta) = y(t-k) - \hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$ alongside inputs and outputs.

#### 2.2.2 Function Expansions, Volterra Series, and the Mother Basis Function $\kappa(x)$

For simplicity, assume a scalar output ($p = 1$; for $p > 1$, each output component can be expanded separately or share hidden basis functions). Regardless of how $\varphi = [\varphi_1 \dots \varphi_d]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$ was constructed from time-series data, it is simply a point in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and $g(\varphi, \theta) : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is parameterized as a **function expansion** over $n$ **basis functions** $g_k(\varphi)$ **(Eq. 5.32)**:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k g_k(\varphi), \qquad \theta = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 & \dots & \alpha_n \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{5.32}
$$

*(Note: When $g_k$ also contains internal scale/location parameters, $\theta$ collects both $\alpha_k$ and those internal parameters, as formalized in Eq. 5.43.)*

##### Polynomial / Volterra Expansions
An immediate classical choice for $g_k(\varphi)$ is a Taylor (polynomial) expansion **(Eq. 5.33)**:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = \varphi^k \tag{5.33}
$$

where for $d > 1$, the notation $\varphi^k$ stands for all monomial products of the regressor components $\varphi_1^{m_1} \varphi_2^{m_2} \cdots \varphi_d^{m_d}$ whose exponents sum to $k$ ($\sum_{j=1}^d m_j = k$). In dynamical system modeling (especially when $\varphi(t)$ consists of lagged inputs), such polynomial expansions are called **Volterra expansions**. While historically important, polynomial basis functions blow up rapidly for large $|\varphi|$ and struggle with localized or saturating nonlinearities.

##### The Unified "Mother Basis Function" Paradigm
Modern nonlinear black-box structures instead construct all $g_k(\varphi)$ from a single scalar **"mother basis function"** $\kappa(x)$ of a scalar variable $x \in \mathbb{R}$. In the scalar regressor case ($d = 1$), each basis function $g_k(\varphi)$ is formed by **dilating (scaling)** and **translating (shifting)** $\kappa(x)$ **(Eq. 5.34)**:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = g_k(\varphi, \beta_k, \gamma_k) = \kappa(\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k)) \tag{5.34}
$$

where:
- $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **dilation (scale) parameter**, controlling the width or frequency of $g_k$.
- $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **translation (location) parameter**, shifting the center or phase of $g_k$ to $\varphi = \gamma_k$.

##### Canonical Scalar Examples ($d = 1$)
1. **Fourier Series**: Take $\kappa(x) = \cos(x)$. Substituting into Eqs. (5.32) and (5.34) yields $\sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k \cos(\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k))$, the classical Fourier series expansion where $\beta_k$ are the **frequencies** and $\gamma_k$ are the **phases**.
2. **Piecewise-Constant Functions (Indicator Boxcar)**: Take $\kappa(x)$ as the unit interval indicator function **(Eq. 5.35)**:

$$
\kappa(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{for } 0 \le x < 1 \\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases} \tag{5.35}
$$

   Choosing a uniform grid with translations $\gamma_k = k\Delta$, dilations $\beta_k = 1/\Delta$, and weights $\alpha_k = f(k\Delta)$, the argument $\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k) = (\varphi - k\Delta)/\Delta$ lies in $[0, 1)$ precisely when $\varphi \in [k\Delta, (k+1)\Delta)$. Thus Eqs. (5.32) and (5.34) produce a piecewise-constant histogram approximation of any function $f(\varphi)$ over intervals of length $\Delta$.
3. **Gaussian Bell (Smooth Indicator)**: Replacing the discontinuous boxcar (Eq. 5.35) with the standard normal density gives a smooth localized basis function **(Eq. 5.36)**:

$$
\kappa(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2 / 2} \tag{5.36}
$$

4. **Unit Step Function**: Take $\kappa(x)$ as the Heaviside unit step function **(Eq. 5.37)**:

$$
\kappa(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{for } x < 0 \\ 1 & \text{for } x \ge 0 \end{cases} \tag{5.37}
$$

   Because the boxcar indicator (Eq. 5.35) equals the difference of two translated unit steps, $\mathbf{1}_{[0,1)}(x) = \kappa(x) - \kappa(x - 1)$, linear combinations of step functions span the exact same piecewise-constant space as Eq. (5.35).
5. **Sigmoid Function (Smooth Step)**: Replacing the discontinuous step (Eq. 5.37) with a smooth logistic transition gives the **sigmoid** mother function **(Eq. 5.38)**:

$$
\kappa(x) = \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \tag{5.38}
$$

#### 2.2.3 Classification of Single-Variable Basis Functions: Local vs. Global

Single-variable basis functions $\kappa(x)$ divide into two fundamental classes according to where their variation is concentrated:

- **Local Basis Functions**: Functions whose significant variation (value or derivative) takes place within a bounded, local neighborhood of the origin (and hence near $\varphi = \gamma_k$ after translation).
  - *Examples*: The indicator function (Eq. 5.35) and Gaussian bell (Eq. 5.36) are localized in value around $x = 0$; the unit step (Eq. 5.37) and sigmoid (Eq. 5.38) are constant ($0$ or $1$) away from $x = 0$ and concentrate all of their **transition/variation** locally around $x = 0$, so Ljung classifies all four of **(5.35)–(5.38) as local functions**.
- **Global Basis Functions**: Functions that exhibit significant variation over the entire real axis $(-\infty, \infty)$.
  - *Examples*: The **Fourier series** ($\kappa(x) = \cos(x)$) and **Volterra / polynomial expansions** ($g_k(\varphi) = \varphi^k$). Modifying a single coefficient $\alpha_k$ in a global expansion alters the model's behavior across the entire regressor domain.

---

#### 2.2.4 Construction of Multi-Variable Basis Functions ($d > 1$)

When the regression vector $\varphi = [\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_d]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$ has dimension $d > 1$, how do we construct a multivariate basis function $g_k(\varphi) : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ from the 1D mother function $\kappa(x) : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$? Ljung formulates the **three standard constructions**:

##### 1. Tensor Product Construction
Apply the 1D dilated-and-translated mother function $\kappa$ independently to each coordinate $\varphi_j$ ($j = 1, \dots, d$) and multiply the $d$ scalar results together **(Eq. 5.39)**:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = g_k(\varphi, \beta_k, \gamma_k) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} \kappa\big(\beta_k^j (\varphi_j - \gamma_k^j)\big) \tag{5.39}
$$

where $\beta_k = [\beta_k^1, \dots, \beta_k^d]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\gamma_k = [\gamma_k^1, \dots, \gamma_k^d]^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$ contain coordinate-wise scales and centers.

##### 2. Radial Construction
Let the value of $g_k(\varphi)$ depend exclusively on the **distance** between $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a center point $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ **(Eq. 5.40)**:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = g_k(\varphi, \beta_k, \gamma_k) = \kappa\big(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k}\big) \tag{5.40}
$$

Here $\|\cdot\|_{\beta_k}$ is a weighted norm on $\mathbb{R}^d$, typically chosen as the **quadratic (Mahalanobis-type) norm** **(Eq. 5.41)**:

$$
\|\varphi\|_{\beta_k}^2 = \varphi^T \beta_k \varphi \tag{5.41}
$$

where the dilation parameter $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a symmetric **positive definite matrix** ($\beta_k \succ 0$) that shapes the ellipsoidal level sets around $\gamma_k$. In simpler isotropic cases, $\beta_k$ is chosen as a scalar multiple of the $d \times d$ identity matrix ($\beta_k = b_k^2 I_d$). If $\kappa(x)$ has local support (or decays rapidly like the Gaussian in Eq. 5.36), then the radial basis function $g_k(\varphi)$ is localized in all $d$ directions simultaneously around $\gamma_k$.

##### 3. Ridge Construction
Let the value of $g_k(\varphi)$ depend only on the signed **projection (distance from a hyperplane)** determined by a direction/scale vector $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and scalar offset $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}$ **(Eq. 5.42)**:

$$
g_k(\varphi) = g_k(\varphi, \beta_k, \gamma_k) = \kappa(\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k), \qquad \varphi \in \mathbb{R}^d \tag{5.42}
$$

> [!warning] Geometric Property of Ridge Functions
> Along any hyperplane perpendicular to $\beta_k$,
>
> $$
> \mathcal{H}_c = \left\{ \varphi \in \mathbb{R}^d : \beta_k^T \varphi = \text{constant} \right\}
> $$
>
> the argument $\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k$ does not change, so $g_k(\varphi)$ is **strictly constant along the entire $(d-1)$-dimensional hyperplane** $\mathcal{H}_c$. Consequently, **even if the scalar mother function $\kappa(x)$ has bounded/local support in $\mathbb{R}$**, the resulting multivariate ridge basis function $g_k(\varphi)$ has **unbounded support in $\mathbb{R}^d$** (stretching infinitely like a mountain ridge along the subspace orthogonal to $\beta_k$).

#### 2.2.5 Unified Expansion, Parameter Roles, and Approximation Efficiency

Abusing notation slightly so that $\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k)$ represents whichever of the three multivariate constructions (tensor product Eq. 5.39, radial norm Eq. 5.40, or ridge projection Eq. 5.42) is chosen, every model structure in this class takes the unified form **(Eq. 5.43)**:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k \kappa(\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k)) \tag{5.43}
$$

This expansion is completely specified by:
1. The scalar mother function $\kappa(x)$ of a scalar variable $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
2. The multivariate construction rule expanding $\kappa$ to depend on the vector $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

Furthermore, the parameter vector $\theta$ splits into **three structurally distinct parameter types**:
- **Coordinates ($\alpha_k$)**: Enter Eq. (5.43) **linearly**. If the scales $\beta_k$ and locations $\gamma_k$ are fixed *a priori*, then $g(\varphi, \theta)$ reduces to a **linear regression** model $\varphi_{\text{basis}}^T \alpha$, allowing global least-squares estimation in one shot!
- **Scale / Dilation parameters ($\beta_k$)**: Enter $\kappa(\cdot)$ **nonlinearly**; control spatial resolution, frequency, or hyperplane orientation/steepness.
- **Location / Translation parameters ($\gamma_k$)**: Enter $\kappa(\cdot)$ **nonlinearly**; control where in the regressor space $\mathbb{R}^d$ each basis element is centered or positioned.

##### Universal Approximation Property
How well can Eq. (5.43) approximate an arbitrary "true system" function $g_0(\varphi)$?
> [!tip] Universal Approximation Theorem (Informal Statement)
> **For almost any choice of mother basis function $\kappa(x)$—except being a polynomial—the expansion (5.43) can approximate any "reasonable" function $g_0(\varphi)$ arbitrarily well for sufficiently large $n$.**
> 
> - **Why Radial + Local $\kappa$ works**: A localized radial function $\kappa(\|\varphi - \gamma_k\|_{\beta_k})$ with large scale $\beta_k$ acts directly as a **Dirac delta / indicator function approximator** on arbitrarily small volume elements around $\gamma_k$. Summing scaled indicators immediately approximates any reasonable continuous or integrable function.
> - **Why Ridge Construction works (Cybenko, 1989)**: Even though a single ridge function $\kappa(\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k)$ is constant along hyperplanes, superimposing *many* ridge functions whose hyperplanes $\beta_k$ intersect at a point $\gamma$ with large norm $\|\beta_k\|$ (so $\kappa$ is nonzero only very close to each hyperplane) causes the constructive interference to localize around $\gamma$, thereby synthesizing localized bumps and approximating arbitrary continuous functions on compact sets.

##### Approximation Efficiency: Fixed Grid vs. Tunable $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$ (Example 5.3)
While universal approximation guarantees existence for *sufficiently large* $n$, practical identification depends on **efficiency**: how large must $n$ be to achieve uniform error $\epsilon$?

1. **Advantage of Function-Adapted $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$ (Example 5.3: Constant Functions)**:
   Suppose we approximate a scalar function $g_0(\varphi)$ on the interval **(Eq. 5.44)**:

$$
g_0(\varphi), \qquad 0 \le \varphi \le B \tag{5.44}
$$

   using a piecewise-constant expansion with unit indicator basis functions $\kappa(x) = \mathbf{1}_{[0,1)}(x)$ from Eq. (5.35) **(Eq. 5.45)**:

$$
\hat{g}_n(\varphi) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \alpha_k \kappa(\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k)) \tag{5.45}
$$

   Suppose we require a uniform approximation error bound

$$
\sup_{\varphi \in [0, B]} \left| g_0(\varphi) - \hat{g}_n(\varphi) \right| \le \epsilon
$$

   under the prior derivative bound $\sup_{\varphi} |g_0'(\varphi)| \le C$.
   - **Case A: A Priori Fixed Sequence $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$**:
     If $\beta_k = 1/\Delta$ and $\gamma_k = k\Delta$ must be fixed *before* seeing $g_0(\varphi)$ so as to guarantee accuracy $\epsilon$ for *every* function satisfying $|g_0'(\varphi)| \le C$, then on each interval of length $\Delta$ (picking $\alpha_k$ at the interval midpoint so the maximum excursion from the midpoint is $\frac{\Delta}{2} C$), we must enforce $\frac{\Delta}{2} C \le \epsilon$, i.e.,

$$
\Delta \le \frac{2\epsilon}{C} \implies n = \frac{B}{\Delta} \ge \frac{CB}{2\epsilon}
$$

     Thus, a fixed grid must prepare everywhere for the worst-case slope $|g_0'(\varphi)| = C$.
   - **Case B: Adaptive / Tunable $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$**:
     If the actual $g_0(\varphi)$ is very flat over most of $[0, B]$ and only reaches a steep derivative near a small localized region $\varphi^*$, allowing $\beta_k = 1/\Delta_k$ and $\gamma_k$ to adapt to $g_0(\varphi)$ lets us choose fine spacing $\Delta_k \approx 2\epsilon / |g_0'(\varphi^*)|$ *only* in the small neighborhood around $\gamma_k \approx \varphi^*$, while using wide intervals (small $\beta_k$) elsewhere—achieving the same error $\epsilon$ with **drastically fewer terms $n$**.

2. **The Curse of Dimensionality for Local Radial Basis Functions**:
   For the local, radial approach in $d$ dimensions, approximating an $s$-times differentiable function $g_0 : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ to accuracy $\delta \ll 1$ requires a number of basis terms $n$ proportional to **(Eq. 5.46)**:

$$
n \sim \frac{1}{\delta^{(d/s)}}, \qquad \delta \ll 1 \tag{5.46}
$$

   Because the dimension $d$ of the regressor vector $\varphi(t)$ appears in the exponent of $1/\delta$, the required number of basis functions grows **exponentially with the number of regressors $d$**—the classic **curse of dimensionality** (Problem 5G.2).

#### 2.2.6 Network Architectures: Multi-Layer and Recurrent Networks

Because Eq. (5.43) evaluates the same "mother basis function" $\kappa$ across $n$ parallel nodes and sums the weighted outputs, graphical representations of these expansions look like interconnected **networks**.

##### Multi-Layer Feedforward Networks
Instead of immediately taking a linear combination $\sum \alpha_k g_k(\varphi)$ as the final output (which constitutes a **one-hidden-layer network**), we can treat the outputs of the first set of basis functions as **new intermediate regressors**:

$$
\varphi_k^{(2)}(t) = g_k(\varphi(t)) = \kappa(\varphi(t), \beta_k, \gamma_k), \qquad k = 1, \dots, n
$$

Collecting them into an intermediate feature vector:

$$
\varphi^{(2)}(t) = \left[ \varphi_1^{(2)}(t), \dots, \varphi_n^{(2)}(t) \right]
$$

and feeding $\varphi^{(2)}(t)$ into a second basis expansion yields a **two-hidden-layer network** **(Eq. 5.47)**:

$$
g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{l} \alpha_l^{(2)} \kappa\big(\varphi^{(2)}, \beta_l^{(2)}, \gamma_l^{(2)}\big) \tag{5.47}
$$

where $\theta$ collects all parameters across both layers: $\beta_k, \gamma_k, \alpha_l^{(2)}, \beta_l^{(2)}, \gamma_l^{(2)}$. The layers computing $\kappa(\varphi(t), \beta_k, \gamma_k)$ and $\kappa(\varphi^{(2)}, \beta_l^{(2)}, \gamma_l^{(2)})$ are called **hidden layers** because their internal node outputs do not appear explicitly at the external model output $g(\varphi, \theta)$ (though they remain accessible to the user). Repeating this composition produces general **multi-layer networks**.

![[assets/fig_5_4_two_hidden_layer_network.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 5.2: Feedforward Network with Two Hidden Layers)
> - **Input Layer (Left)**: The external regression vector $\varphi(t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ enters from the left, augmented by a constant bias input $1$ (weighted by location/threshold parameters $\gamma_k \subset \theta$).
> - **First Hidden Layer (Middle-Left)**: Each block labeled $\Sigma \to \kappa$ first forms a weighted linear combination ($\Sigma$, applying scale/projection weights $\beta_k$ to $\varphi$ and adding the offset $\gamma_k \cdot 1$) and then passes that scalar sum through the nonlinear mother activation function $\kappa(\cdot)$ to produce $\varphi_k^{(2)}(t)$.
> - **Second Hidden Layer (Middle-Right)**: Takes the vector of first-layer outputs $\varphi^{(2)}(t)$ plus a bias input $1$, applies second-layer weights $\beta_l^{(2)}, \gamma_l^{(2)}$ inside a second bank of $\Sigma \to \kappa$ units, and outputs the second-layer basis activations.
> - **Output Layer (Right)**: Purely linear summation units ($\Sigma$) multiply the final hidden-layer activations by the linear output coordinates $\alpha_l^{(2)}$ (plus an optional output offset from the $1$ node) to produce the model output vector $g(\varphi, \theta)$.
> - **Practical Layer Depth Note**: While one hidden layer with sufficiently many basis functions is theoretically universal for almost any reasonable system, Sontag (1993) demonstrates that adding a **second hidden layer** can significantly improve parsimony and representational capability for certain nonlinear mappings (such as discontinuous or inverse mappings).

##### Recurrent Networks
In dynamical systems, some components of the regression vector $\varphi(t)$ at time $t$ may be obtained by feeding back outputs from the model structure itself at previous time instants:

$$
\varphi_k(t) = g(\varphi(t - k), \theta)
$$

More generally, a regressor component $\varphi_j(t)$ at time $t$ can be fed back from an **interior (hidden) node** of the network at a previous time instant, rather than only from the external output layer.

![[assets/fig_5_4_recurrent_network.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 5.3: Example of a Recurrent Network)
> - **Forward Path**: External regressors $\varphi$ and bias $1$ feed into hidden units ($\Sigma \to \kappa$), whose outputs are linearly combined at the output summer ($\Sigma$) to produce $g$.
> - **Delay Feedback Loops ($q^{-1}$)**: The output signal $g(t)$ is tapped and passed through unit-sample delay blocks $q^{-1}$ (where $q^{-1} g(t) = g(t-1)$ and a second cascaded $q^{-1}$ yields $g(t-2)$), which are fed back as additional inputs into the hidden layer's $\Sigma \to \kappa$ nodes at subsequent time steps.
> - **Input/Output-Based vs. State-Space-Based Recurrent Networks**:
>   - **Input/Output-based networks**: Feed back only past external model outputs $g(t-k)$ as recurrent regressors (analogous to NOE models, Eq. 4.20).
>   - **State-Space-based networks**: Feed back interior hidden-node activations to the input layer through delay elements $q^{-1}$ (analogous to general recursive predictor states, Eq. 4.40). Although the distinction between input/output and state-space forms is less rigid in the nonlinear case than in the linear case, experience with state-space-based recurrent networks is often very favorable (Nerrand et al., 1993).

##### Estimation Aspects
A major benefit of formulating nonlinear black-box models within the predictor framework $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(\varphi(t), \theta)$ is that **all the estimation theory, asymptotic convergence properties, and prediction-error algorithms developed in Chapters 7 and onwards apply directly without modification** alongside linear model structures.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: Separating Dynamics ($\varphi$) from Nonlinearity ($g$)**: Students often confuse "recurrent vs. feedforward networks" with "nonlinear vs. linear functions." Keep them cleanly separated:
>   - **How $\varphi(t)$ is built** determines the *dynamical memory and feedback structure*: if $\varphi(t)$ contains only measured $u(t-k), y(t-k)$ (NFIR/NARX), the network is purely **feedforward** during estimation even though it models a dynamical system! If $\varphi(t, \theta)$ contains past model predictions $\hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$ or internal hidden states (NOE/NARMAX/state-space), the network becomes **recurrent**.
>   - **How $g(\varphi, \theta)$ is built** (radial vs. ridge vs. tensor product, $\kappa(x)$ choice) is purely a *static multivariate function approximation* question on $\mathbb{R}^d$.
> - **Leap 2: Why Sigmoids/Steps Are "Local" in 1D Yet "Unbounded" as Ridge Functions in $\mathbb{R}^d$**:
>   - On pages 150–151, Ljung classifies the unit step (5.37) and sigmoid $\sigma(x)$ (5.38) as **local basis functions** in 1D because their *significant variation* (derivative $\sigma'(x)$) is concentrated locally near $x = 0$.
>   - However, when lifted to $\mathbb{R}^d$ ($d > 1$) via the **ridge construction** $\kappa(\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k)$ (Eq. 5.42), the function is constant along every $(d-1)$-dimensional hyperplane $\beta_k^T \varphi = \text{const}$. Thus its support and transition region form an infinite hyper-slab across $\mathbb{R}^d$! Do not conflate 1D localization of variation with $d$-dimensional compact support.
> - **Notation Trap: Overloaded Meaning of $\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k)$ in Eq. (5.43)**:
>   - In the unified equation $g(\varphi, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k \kappa(\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k))$ (Eq. 5.43), the expression $\beta_k(\varphi - \gamma_k)$ is **symbolic shorthand** for three algebraically incompatible operations depending on the construction:
>     1. **Tensor product (Eq. 5.39)**: Coordinate-wise product $\prod_{j=1}^d \kappa(\beta_k^j(\varphi_j - \gamma_k^j))$ where $\beta_k, \gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
>     2. **Radial (Eq. 5.40–5.41)**: Weighted quadratic norm $\sqrt{(\varphi - \gamma_k)^T \beta_k (\varphi - \gamma_k)}$ where $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a positive definite matrix and $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a center vector.
>     3. **Ridge (Eq. 5.42)**: Inner product plus scalar offset $\beta_k^T \varphi + \gamma_k$ where $\beta_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a direction vector and $\gamma_k \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar bias.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *When should we fix $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$ on a grid (making estimation of $\alpha_k$ a convex linear regression problem) versus optimizing $(\beta_k, \gamma_k)$ jointly with $\alpha_k$ (non-convex optimization, but avoiding the worst-case grid explosion of Example 5.3)?*
>   2. *How does the curse of dimensionality $n \sim \delta^{-d/s}$ (Eq. 5.46) for radial basis functions motivate ridge constructions (neural networks) when the number of regressors $d$ is large?*
