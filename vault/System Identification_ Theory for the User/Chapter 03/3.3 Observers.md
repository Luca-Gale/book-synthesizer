# 3.3 Observers

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 3**: Section 3.3 bridges stochastic prediction theory (Section 3.2) and deterministic control/estimation theory by showing that the exact same linear predictor structure $\hat{y}(t|t-k) = [1 - W(q)]y(t) + W(q)G(q)u(t)$ arises when designing an input-output **observer** for a noise-free model $y(t) = G(q)u(t)$. Ultimately, this establishes one of Ljung's core philosophical pillars: **the predictor filter itself is the fundamental system description**, while the noise model $H(q)$ is merely a convenient vehicle (an "alibi") for choosing the observer filter $W(q)$.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

In Section 3.2, we derived the optimal $k$-step-ahead predictor by assuming a full stochastic description $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ driven by stationary white noise $e(t)$. However, in classical systems and control engineering, practitioners frequently work with a purely **deterministic** ("noise-free") model:

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t)
$$

Even when writing down $y(t) = G(q)u(t)$, every engineer keeps in the back of their mind that this equation is an idealization—initial conditions may be unknown, past data prior to $t = 0$ may be missing, and output measurements $y(t)$ are inevitably corrupted by sensor noise or unmodeled disturbances.

How do we "guess" or "predict" future outputs $y(t)$ when we do not have an explicit stochastic noise model $H(q)$? In state-space control theory (such as Luenberger observers, which will be treated in Section 4.3), this is solved by constructing an **observer** that blends open-loop model simulation with closed-loop output feedback. Here in Section 3.3, Ljung introduces the exact same observer concept directly in **input-output transfer-function form**:
1. **Pure Open-Loop Simulation (Infinite Impulse Response / MA Form)**: We can express $y(t)$ strictly in terms of past inputs $u(t-1), u(t-2), \dots$ using the impulse response of $G(q)$. This completely ignores noisy output measurements $y(s)$, making it immune to output sensor noise, but any error in initial conditions (or missing inputs prior to $s = 0$) decays at the natural pole rate of $G(q)$.
2. **Pure Autoregressive / Difference-Equation Recursion**: Alternatively, we can invert the denominator of $G(q)$ so that $y(t)$ is computed recursively from recent outputs $y(t-1), \dots$ and recent inputs $u(t-1), \dots$. This eliminates dependence on the distant past (initial condition errors vanish immediately), but any measurement error in $y(t-1)$ is fed directly into the prediction!
3. **The Observer Trade-Off Filter $W(q)$**: By introducing a general pre-filter $W(q) = 1 + \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell}$, we create a continuum of predictors:

$$
\hat{y}(t|t-k) = [1 - W(q)]y(t) + W(q)G(q)u(t)
$$

Comparing this deterministic observer to the stochastic predictor from Section 3.2 ($\hat{y}(t|t-1) = H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t) + [1 - H^{-1}(q)]y(t)$ for $k=1$, or $W(q) = W_k(q) = \bar{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)$ for general $k$), we see they are **mathematically identical** with $W(q) = H^{-1}(q)$ (for $k=1$). Thus, specifying a stochastic noise model $H(q)$ and solving for the conditional expectation is simply one systematic way of picking the observer design filter $W(q)$ to attenuate measurement noise in the frequency domain!

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

| Symbol | Meaning / Physical Interpretation | Space / Dimensions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y(t)$ | Measured system output at discrete time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}$ (scalar output) |
| $u(t)$ | Known system input at discrete time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}$ (scalar input) |
| $G(q)$ | Deterministic plant transfer operator in the forward shift operator $q$ | Causal rational/linear operator |
| $a, b$ | Pole and gain parameters in the first-order example $G(z) = \frac{bz^{-1}}{1 - az^{-1}}$ | $\mathbb{R}$ (with $\|a\| < 1$ for stability) |
| $\hat{y}(t\|t-1)$ | One-step-ahead predicted ("guessed") output at time $t$ given data up to $t-1$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\hat{y}(t\|t-k)$ | $k$-step-ahead predicted output at time $t$ given data $y(s)$ for $s \le t-k$ and $u(s)$ for $s \le t-1$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $W(q)$ | Observer/predictor design filter $1 + \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell}$ governing error dynamics and noise sensitivity | Stable causal linear filter |
| $w_\ell$ | Impulse response coefficients of $W(q)$ for lags $\ell \ge k$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $y_M(t)$ | True ("useful") deterministic model output $G(q)u(t)$ before measurement corruption | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $v(t)$ | Additive output measurement error / disturbance ($y(t) = y_M(t) + v(t)$) | $\mathbb{R}$ (stationary process) |
| $\varepsilon(t)$ | $k$-step-ahead prediction error $y(t) - \hat{y}(t\|t-k)$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\Phi_v(\omega)$ | Power spectrum of the measurement disturbance $v(t)$ | $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ |
| $\Phi_\varepsilon(\omega)$ | Power spectrum of the resulting prediction error $\varepsilon(t)$ | $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 1. Deterministic Model & First-Order Motivating Example
Suppose we start from a noise-free ("deterministic") linear time-invariant system description:

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) \tag{3.35}
$$

To see why multiple distinct predictors exist for the same deterministic transfer function $G(q)$, consider a concrete first-order causal system with $|a| < 1$:

$$
G(z) = b \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (a)^{k-1} z^{-k} = \frac{bz^{-1}}{1 - az^{-1}} \tag{3.36}
$$

**Two Equivalent Representations of the Exact System**:
1. **Infinite Impulse Response (Convolution) Form**: Expanding $G(q)$ as a geometric series in $q^{-1}$ yields:

$$
y(t) = b \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (a)^{k-1} u(t - k) \tag{3.37}
$$

2. **Difference Equation (Recursive) Form**: Writing $y(t) = \frac{bq^{-1}}{1 - aq^{-1}} u(t)$ and multiplying both sides by $(1 - aq^{-1})$ gives:

$$
(1 - aq^{-1})y(t) = bq^{-1}u(t)
$$

Evaluating the backward shift operator $q^{-1}x(t) = x(t-1)$ produces the difference equation:

$$
y(t) - ay(t - 1) = bu(t - 1) \tag{3.38}
$$

**Two Candidate One-Step-Ahead Predictors ("Observers")**:
Suppose we are given the model (3.35)–(3.36) and historical input-output data $y(s), u(s)$ for $s \le t - 1$, and we want to produce a prediction $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ of $y(t)$:
- **Predictor 1 (Pure Simulation from Inputs)**: Directly use the impulse response expansion (3.37):

$$
\hat{y}(t|t - 1) = b \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (a)^{k-1} u(t - k) \tag{3.39}
$$

- **Predictor 2 (One-Step Output Recursion)**: Rearrange the difference equation (3.38) by moving $-ay(t-1)$ to the right-hand side:

$$
\hat{y}(t|t - 1) = ay(t - 1) + bu(t - 1) \tag{3.40}
$$

**Engineering Vulnerability Analysis (Why (3.39) and (3.40) Differ in Practice)**:
If the data and the model $y(t) = G(q)u(t)$ are completely exact for $s > -\infty$, then (3.39) and (3.40) yield identical predictions. However, under realistic imperfections, they sit at opposite extremes of a fundamental engineering trade-off:
- **Vulnerability to Missing Past Data / Wrong Initial Conditions**: Suppose input-output measurements are only available starting at time $s = 0$ (so $u(s)$ for $s < 0$ is unknown and replaced by zeros in (3.39)). Then Predictor 1 (3.39) suffers from a transient initial-condition error that decays proportionally to $a^t$. By contrast, Predictor 2 (3.40) only needs $y(t-1)$ and $u(t-1)$, so it is **exact for all $t \ge 1$** regardless of what happened before $s = 0$.
- **Vulnerability to Output Measurement Errors**: Suppose the measured output $y(s)$ contains sensor noise. Predictor 1 (3.39) does not use $y(s)$ at all, so it is **completely unaffected** by output measurement noise! Conversely, Predictor 2 (3.40) feeds the noisy measurement $y(t-1)$ directly into the prediction (multiplied by $a$).
- **Connection to Section 3.2**: If (3.35) is augmented with an explicit stochastic noise model $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ as in (3.1), the ambiguity disappears: the conditional expectation $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = E[y(t)|y^{t-1}, u^{t-1}]$ uniquely pins down the optimal predictor (cf. Problem 3E.3). Specifically:
  - Predictor 1 (3.39) corresponds to an Output-Error (OE) noise structure $H(q) = 1$ (where output disturbances are pure white measurement noise $e(t)$ uncorrelated with past dynamics).
  - Predictor 2 (3.40) corresponds to an ARX noise structure $H(q) = \frac{1}{1 - aq^{-1}}$ (where process noise enters the difference equation directly as $y(t) - ay(t-1) = bu(t-1) + e(t)$).

#### 2. A Family of Predictors for $y(t) = G(q)u(t)$
To systematically parameterize the trade-off between **fast decay of initial-condition errors** and **low sensitivity to output measurement noise** for general $k$-step-ahead prediction, we introduce a causal linear design filter $W(q)$ whose impulse response starts with a unit direct feedthrough ($1$) followed by zero coefficients for lags $1, \dots, k-1$:

$$
W(q) = 1 + \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell} \tag{3.41}
$$

**Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation of the General Predictor Family**:
1. Start from the deterministic plant relationship (3.35):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t)
$$

2. Apply the linear filter $W(q)$ to both sides of (3.35):

$$
W(q)y(t) = W(q)G(q)u(t)
$$

3. Add $[1 - W(q)]y(t)$ to both sides (equivalently, write $y(t) = y(t) - W(q)y(t) + W(q)y(t)$ and substitute $W(q)y(t) = W(q)G(q)u(t)$):

$$
y(t) = [1 - W(q)]y(t) + W(q)G(q)u(t)
$$

4. **Verify Causality / Information Availability at Time $t-k$**:
   - Inspect the output filter $[1 - W(q)]$: Because $W(q) = 1 + \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell}$, subtracting $W(q)$ from $1$ cancels the leading $1$ and leaves:

$$
1 - W(q) = -\sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell}
$$

   - Therefore, $[1 - W(q)]y(t) = -\sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell y(t - \ell)$ depends **only on past outputs $y(s)$ for $s \le t - k$**.
   - Similarly, since $G(q)$ contains at least a one-sample delay ($G(q) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} g(\ell)q^{-\ell}$) and $W(q)$ is causal, the product filter $W(q)G(q)$ has at least a one-sample delay, meaning $W(q)G(q)u(t)$ depends **only on inputs $u(s)$ for $s \le t - 1$**.
5. Because the entire right-hand side is known from data $y(s)$ ($s \le t-k$) and $u(s)$ ($s \le t-1$), it defines a valid $k$-step-ahead predictor ("guess") of $y(t)$:

$$
\hat{y}(t|t - k) = [1 - W(q)]y(t) + W(q)G(q)u(t) \tag{3.42}
$$

---

#### 3. Frequency-Domain Trade-Off & Prediction Error Spectrum
How should the designer choose the filter $W(q)$ in (3.42)? Ljung formulates two competing engineering criteria:

> [!important] The Two Observer Design Criteria (Equation 3.43)
> 1. **Initial-Condition Forgetting**: Select $W(q)$ so that both $W(q)$ and $W(q)G(q)$ have **rapidly decaying filter coefficients** (impulse responses), thereby minimizing the influence of erroneous initial conditions or missing data prior to $t = 0$.
> 2. **Measurement Noise Attenuation**: Select $W(q)$ so that measurement imperfections in $y(t)$ are **maximally attenuated**.

To analyze Criterion 2 quantitatively in the frequency domain, suppose the actual measured output $y(t)$ consists of the true ("useful") deterministic signal $y_M(t) = G(q)u(t)$ plus an additive stationary measurement disturbance $v(t)$:

$$
y(t) = y_M(t) + v(t), \qquad y_M(t) = G(q)u(t)
$$

**Step-by-Step Derivation of the Prediction Error $\varepsilon(t)$**:
1. Form the $k$-step-ahead prediction error $\varepsilon(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-k)$ using (3.42):

$$
\varepsilon(t) = y(t) - \Big( [1 - W(q)]y(t) + W(q)G(q)u(t) \Big)
$$

2. Combine the $y(t)$ terms ($y(t) - [1 - W(q)]y(t) = W(q)y(t)$):

$$
\varepsilon(t) = W(q)y(t) - W(q)G(q)u(t) = W(q)\big[ y(t) - G(q)u(t) \big]
$$

3. Substitute $y(t) - G(q)u(t) = y(t) - y_M(t) = v(t)$:

$$
\varepsilon(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t - k) = W(q)v(t) \tag{3.44}
$$

4. Applying **Theorem 2.2** (the spectral transformation theorem for linear time-invariant filters), if $v(t)$ has power spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega)$, the spectrum of the prediction error $\varepsilon(t) = W(q)v(t)$ is:

$$
\Phi_\varepsilon(\omega) = \left| W(e^{i\omega}) \right|^2 \Phi_v(\omega) \tag{3.45}
$$

**Interpretation & Equivalence with Section 3.2**:
- Under Criterion 2, the observer design problem is to choose $W(q)$—subject to the structural constraint (3.41) that $W(q) = 1 + \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell}$—so that the prediction error spectrum $\Phi_\varepsilon(\omega)$ in (3.45) has an acceptable magnitude and frequency shape.
- Comparing (3.42) with the stochastic $k$-step-ahead predictor (3.31) from Section 3.2 ($\hat{y}(t|t-k) = W_k(q)G(q)u(t) + [1 - W_k(q)]y(t)$), we see that **the two expressions are identical with $W(q) = W_k(q) = \bar{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)$**!
- When we specify a stochastic noise model $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ as in (3.1), $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$. Minimizing the prediction error variance $\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \Phi_\varepsilon(\omega)\,d\omega$ analytically yields $W(q) = W_k(q)$. Note that in Section 3.2, Criterion 1 (initial-condition decay) was ignored because infinite past data ($s > -\infty$) was assumed available—though as long as $W_k(q)$ and $W_k(q)G(q)$ are exponentially stable, initial condition errors decay exponentially fast anyway.

#### 4. Fundamental Role of the Predictor Filter

In almost every practical application of a system model—whether forecasting future outputs or designing a feedback controller to regulate the predicted output—what we actually use is **not** the raw equation (3.1) or (3.35), but the **predictor filter** (3.20), (3.31), or (3.42).

![[assets/fig_3_3_predictor_filter.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 3.1: The Predictor Filter)
> - **Inputs ($y$ and $u$ on the left)**: The predictor is simply a two-input, single-output causal linear filter that continuously ingests the observed output sequence $\{y(t)\}$ and known input sequence $\{u(t)\}$.
> - **Filter Block (center)**: Internally, the block applies the transfer operator $[1 - W(q)]$ to $y(t)$ and $W(q)G(q)$ to $u(t)$ (where $W(q) = H^{-1}(q)$ for one-step-ahead prediction, or $W(q) = W_k(q)$ for $k$-step-ahead prediction).
> - **Output ($\hat{y}$ on the right)**: The block outputs the predicted sequence $\hat{y}(t|t-k)$. Once this filter is implemented in software or hardware, **the philosophical route by which the designer arrived at $W(q)$ is completely immaterial**!

**The Noise Model $H(q)$ as an "Alibi"**:
Whether a designer arrived at $W(q) = W_k(q)$ via:
1. A deterministic frequency-domain observer trade-off (3.43) and (3.45), **or**
2. Assuming a stochastic white-noise innovation model $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ and computing conditional expectations via (3.27) and (3.29),

the resulting linear filter in Figure 3.1 is **identical**. As Ljung famously remarks:
- *"The noise model $H$ in (3.1) is from this point of view just an alibi for determining the predictor. This is the viewpoint we are going to adopt. The predictor filter is the fundamental system description (Figure 3.1). Our rationale for arriving at the filter is secondary."*
- Consequently, the philosophical divide between a "stochastic system" (3.1) and a "deterministic system" (3.35) is **not fundamental** in system identification.
- Nonetheless, we retain $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ as our standard working representation because it stands in a **one-to-one correspondence** with the one-step-ahead predictor (3.20) (see Problem 3D.2) and connects naturally to classical transfer-function descriptions.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 (Why $W(q)$ Must Start with $1 + 0q^{-1} + \dots + 0q^{-(k-1)}$)**: Watch equation (3.41) closely: $W(q) = 1 + \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} w_\ell q^{-\ell}$. Why are the coefficients for $q^{-1}, \dots, q^{-(k-1)}$ missing? Because in the predictor formula $\hat{y}(t|t-k) = [1 - W(q)]y(t) + W(q)G(q)u(t)$, the filter multiplying $y(t)$ is $1 - W(q)$. Having $W(q)$ start with $1 + w_k q^{-k} + \dots$ guarantees that the $1$ cancels out and the earliest output term used in $[1 - W(q)]y(t)$ is $y(t-k)$!
> - **Leap 2 (Why $H(q)$ is Called an "Alibi")**: Do not get bogged down debating whether real physical disturbances are truly generated by stationary white noise $e(t)$ passing through a rational filter $H(q)$. Ljung's point on page 74 is pragmatic: in prediction-error system identification, we fit a predictor filter (Figure 3.1) to data. Writing $H(q)$ is simply a convenient, standardized parameterization of the observer filter $W(q) = H^{-1}(q)$ (for $k=1$).
> - **Notation Trap ($W(q)$ vs. $W_y(q)$ and $W_u(q)$)**: In Section 3.2, $W_u(q)$ and $W_y(q)$ denoted the filters multiplying $u(t)$ and $y(t)$ respectively. In Section 3.3, Ljung drops the subscript on $W_k(q)$ and writes $W(q)$ for the error filter $\varepsilon(t) = W(q)v(t)$, so the filter on $y(t)$ is $1 - W(q)$ and the filter on $u(t)$ is $W(q)G(q)$. Keep this distinction clear: $W_y(q) = 1 - W(q)$ and $W_u(q) = W(q)G(q)$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. In the first-order example, what choice of $W(q)$ recovers Predictor 1 (3.39), and what choice of $W(q)$ recovers Predictor 2 (3.40)? *(Hint: Compare $W(q) = 1$ versus $W(q) = 1 - aq^{-1}$.)*
>   2. How does the equation $\varepsilon(t) = W(q)v(t)$ explain why making $W(q)$ decay very fast (e.g., high-gain observer poles near $z = 0$) typically amplifies high-frequency measurement noise $\Phi_v(\omega)$?
