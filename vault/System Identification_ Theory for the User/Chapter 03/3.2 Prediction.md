# 3.2 Prediction

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 3**: Whereas Section 3.1 showed that pure simulation ($y_u(t) = G(q)u(t)$) suffers from an irreducible disturbance error $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$, Section 3.2 demonstrates how observing past outputs $y^t$ allows us to *invert* the noise filter $H(q)$, reconstruct past white-noise shocks $e(s)$, and compute the optimal minimum mean-square error (MMSE) one-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ and $k$-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|t-k)$.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

In Section 3.1, we saw that when we simulate a system using only the input sequence $u^t$, the discrepancy between the true output $y(t)$ and the simulated output $y_u(t) = G(q)u(t)$ is the entire stochastic disturbance $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$. Because $e(t)$ is an unmeasurable white-noise sequence, a pure simulator has no way of knowing what the disturbance is doing.

**Prediction** fundamentally changes the game by granting us access to *past measurements of the output* $y(s)$ (for $s \le t-1$) in addition to the input $u(s)$. Why does knowing past outputs help us predict a random disturbance at time $t$?
1. **Extracting Past Disturbances from Input-Output Data**: Since $y(s) = G(q)u(s) + v(s)$, knowing both $y(s)$ and $u(s)$ for all $s \le t-1$ means we can subtract the deterministic input response to uniquely determine the exact historical disturbance realizations: $v(s) = y(s) - G(q)u(s)$ for $s \le t-1$.
2. **Temporal Correlation in Colored Noise ($H(q)$)**: Although the underlying driving innovations $e(t)$ are independent from step to step, the disturbance $v(t) = H(q)e(t) = e(t) + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k)$ is *filtered* (colored) noise. It has memory: today's disturbance $v(t)$ is made up of a brand-new, unpredictable shock $e(t)$ *plus* a weighted tail of past shocks $e(t-1), e(t-2), \dots$ that are still reverberating through the noise dynamics $H(q)$.
3. **Inverting the Noise Model to Recover Past Shocks**: If we can *invert* the filter $H(q)$—running our known history of $v(s)$ ($s \le t-1$) backward through an inverse filter $H^{-1}(q)$—we can reconstruct the exact past shocks $e(t-1), e(t-2), \dots$! Once we know those past shocks, we can compute the exact reverberating tail $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k)$ that will show up at time $t$. The only part of $v(t)$ we cannot predict is the fresh shock $e(t)$ arriving right at time $t$, which has zero mean.

This section formalizes when $H(q)$ can be stably inverted (**Lemma 3.1**), derives the conditional expectation predictor $\hat{v}(t|t-1)$ and the full system predictor $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$, handles practical finite-data records where initial conditions before $s = 0$ are unknown, and generalizes the framework to $k$-step-ahead prediction $\hat{y}(t+k|t)$ and multivariable ($p \times p$) systems.

| $H(q), H(z)$ | Monic, stable linear filter ($H(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k)q^{-k}$ with $h(0) = 1$) and its $z$-transform | Rational transfer operator / $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ |

| $f_e(x), f_v(x)$ | Probability density function (PDF) of $e(t)$ and conditional (posterior) PDF of $v(t)$ given $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$ | $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ |
| $\hat{v}(t \mid t-1)$ | One-step-ahead conditional expectation predictor of $v(t)$ given $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |

| $\ell(k)$ | Impulse response coefficients of the predictor input filter $G(z)/H(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \ell(k)z^{-k}$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\bar{H}_k(q)$ | Truncated finite impulse response (FIR) part of $H(q)$ over the first $k$ steps: $\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1} h(\ell)q^{-\ell}$ | Polynomial in $q^{-1}$ of degree $k-1$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 1. Invertibility of the Noise Model & Lemma 3.1

We begin with the disturbance process $v(t)$ described by Eq. (3.4):

$$
v(t) = H(q)e(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k) \tag{3.4}
$$

For Eq. (3.4) to be meaningful, we impose **Assumption 1 (Stability of $H$)**:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} |h(k)| < \infty \tag{3.5}
$$

To predict future values of $v(t)$ from past observations $v(s)$ ($s \le t$), a crucial property we impose on Eq. (3.4) is **invertibility**: if $v(s)$ for $s \le t$ are known, we must be able to compute the current innovation $e(t)$ via a causal, stable linear filter $\tilde{H}(q)$:

$$
e(t) = \tilde{H}(q)v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)v(t-k), \qquad \text{with } \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left|\tilde{h}(k)\right| < \infty \tag{3.6}
$$

How do we construct $\tilde{H}(q)$ from $H(q)$? **Lemma 3.1** establishes the exact complex-analytic condition required to invert $H(q)$ via its $z$-transform.

> [!important] Lemma 3.1 (Invertibility of the Noise Filter)
> Consider $\{v(t)\}$ defined by Eq. (3.4) and assume that the filter $H$ is stable. Let
>
> $$
> H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k)z^{-k} \tag{3.7}
> $$
>
> and assume that the function $1/H(z)$ is analytic in $|z| \ge 1$:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{H(z)} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)z^{-k} \tag{3.8}
> $$
>
> Define the filter $H^{-1}(q)$ by
>
> $$
> H^{-1}(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)q^{-k} \tag{3.9}
> $$
>
> Then $\tilde{H}(q) = H^{-1}(q)$ satisfies Eq. (3.6).
> - **Terminology (*Inversely Stable*)**: That Eq. (3.8) exists for $|z| \ge 1$ also means that the inverse filter $H^{-1}(q)$ is stable ($\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} |\tilde{h}(k)| < \infty$). For convenience, we say that $H(q)$ is an **inversely stable** filter (often also called *minimum phase*).
> - **Pole-Zero Meaning & Connection to Spectral Factorization**: Analyticity of $1/H(z)$ in $|z| \ge 1$ means $1/H(z)$ has no poles on or outside the unit circle—equivalently, **$H(z)$ has no zeros on or outside the unit circle** ($|z| \ge 1$). By the spectral factorization theorem (Section 2.3), for any rational strictly positive spectrum, we can always find a representation $H(q)$ with both stability and inverse stability.

**Step-by-Step Proof of Lemma 3.1**:
1. **Cauchy Product of $z$-Transforms**: Multiplying Eq. (3.7) and Eq. (3.8) gives $H(z) \cdot \frac{1}{H(z)} = 1$ for $|z| \ge 1$. Expanding the double sum and substituting the index change $k + s = \ell$ (so $s = \ell - k$, with $\ell$ running from $0$ to $\infty$ and $k$ from $0$ to $\ell$):

$$
1 = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(k)\tilde{h}(s)z^{-(k+s)} = [k + s = \ell] = \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \sum_{k=0}^{\ell} h(k)\tilde{h}(\ell-k)z^{-\ell}
$$

2. **Matching Powers of $z^{-\ell}$**: Comparing coefficients of $z^{-\ell}$ on both sides implies the discrete-time convolution identity between $\{h(k)\}$ and $\{\tilde{h}(k)\}$:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\ell} h(k)\tilde{h}(\ell-k) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } \ell = 0 \\ 0, & \text{if } \ell \neq 0 \end{cases} \tag{3.10}
$$

3. **Applying the Filter to $v(t)$**: Now substitute Eq. (3.4), $v(t-k) = \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(s)e(t-k-s)$, into the candidate inverse filter expansion $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)v(t-k)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)v(t-k) &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k) \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} h(s)e(t-k-s) \\
&= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)h(s)e(t-k-s) \qquad [\text{let } k + s = \ell] \\
&= \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{\ell} \tilde{h}(k)h(\ell-k) \right] e(t-\ell) = e(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

where swapping the order of summation is justified by the absolute summability of both $\{h(k)\}$ and $\{\tilde{h}(k)\}$ (and boundedness of moments of $e$), and the inner bracket collapses to $1$ at $\ell = 0$ and $0$ for $\ell \ge 1$ by Eq. (3.10). This proves Lemma 3.1 and justifies writing the operator shorthand:

$$
H^{-1}(q) = \frac{1}{H(q)} \tag{3.11}
$$

#### 2. Example 3.1: Inverting a Moving Average Process MA(1)

Suppose $v(t)$ is a first-order moving average process, $\text{MA}(1)$:

$$
v(t) = e(t) + ce(t-1) \iff H(q) = 1 + cq^{-1} \tag{3.12}
$$

Taking the $z$-transform gives:

$$
H(z) = 1 + cz^{-1} = \frac{z + c}{z}
$$

- **Poles and Zeros**: $H(z)$ has a pole at $z = 0$ (always stable) and a single zero at $z = -c$.
- **Invertibility Condition**: The zero $z = -c$ lies strictly inside the unit circle if and only if $|c| < 1$.
- **Geometric Series Expansion**: When $|c| < 1$, for $|z| \ge 1$ we have $|cz^{-1}| \le |c| < 1$, so $H^{-1}(z)$ expands into a convergent geometric power series:

$$
H^{-1}(z) = \frac{1}{H(z)} = \frac{1}{1 + cz^{-1}} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-c)^k z^{-k}
$$

Thus, $\tilde{h}(k) = (-c)^k$, and the innovation $e(t)$ is recovered from the infinite past of $v(t)$ as:

$$
e(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-c)^k v(t-k)
$$

#### 3. One-Step-Ahead Prediction of $v(t)$ & Conditional Expectation

Suppose we have observed $v(s)$ for all $s \le t-1$ (denoted $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$) and wish to predict $v(t)$. Since $H(q)$ is **monic** ($h(0) = 1$), we split Eq. (3.4) into the unpredictable current innovation $e(t)$ ($k=0$) and the historical tail ($k \ge 1$):

$$
v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k) = e(t) + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k) \tag{3.13}
$$

By Eq. (3.6), knowing $v(s)$ for all $s \le t-1$ is completely equivalent to knowing $e(s)$ for all $s \le t-1$. Therefore, the second term in Eq. (3.13) is **deterministic and completely known** at time $t-1$. Let us denote it provisionally by $m(t-1)$:

$$
m(t-1) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k)
$$

**Posterior Probability Density Function of $v(t)$**:
Assume $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with probability density function (PDF) $f_e(x)$, so $P(x \le e(t) \le x + \Delta x) \approx f_e(x)\Delta x$. Because $e(t)$ is independent of all past shocks $e(s)$ ($s \le t-1$), conditioning on $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$ fixes $m(t-1)$ without altering the distribution of $e(t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_v(x)\Delta x &\approx P\left(x \le v(t) \le x + \Delta x \;\middle|\; v_{-\infty}^{t-1}\right) \\
&= P\left(x \le m(t-1) + e(t) \le x + \Delta x\right) \\
&= P\left(x - m(t-1) \le e(t) \le x + \Delta x - m(t-1)\right) \\
&\approx f_e\left(x - m(t-1)\right)\Delta x
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, **the posterior PDF of $v(t)$ given observations up to time $t-1$ is simply the noise PDF shifted by $m(t-1)$**: $f_v(x) = f_e(x - m(t-1))$.

**Choosing a Point Predictor (MAP vs. Conditional Expectation / MMSE)**:
- If we choose the value of $x$ that maximizes $f_e(x - m(t-1))$, we get the *most probable value* of $v(t)$, known as the **maximum a posteriori (MAP)** prediction.
- In system identification, we almost exclusively work with the mean value of the posterior distribution—the **conditional expectation** of $v(t)$ given $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$, denoted $\hat{v}(t|t-1)$. Since $e(t)$ has zero mean ($E[e(t)] = 0$), we obtain:

$$
\hat{v}(t \mid t-1) = m(t-1) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)e(t-k) \tag{3.14}
$$

Furthermore, the conditional expectation uniquely minimizes the **mean-square prediction error** over all possible functions $\hat{v}(t)$ of the past observations $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$ (Problem 3D.3):

$$
\min_{\hat{v}(t)} E\left(v(t) - \hat{v}(t)\right)^2 \implies \hat{v}(t) = \hat{v}(t \mid t-1)
$$

*(Proof sketch: For any predictor $\hat{v}(t)$ depending only on $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$, write $v(t) - \hat{v}(t) = e(t) + [m(t-1) - \hat{v}(t)]$. Since $e(t)$ has zero mean and is independent of $v_{-\infty}^{t-1}$, the cross-term $2E[e(t)(m(t-1)-\hat{v}(t))] = 0$, leaving $E[e^2(t)] + E[(m(t-1)-\hat{v}(t))^2]$, which is minimized when $\hat{v}(t) = m(t-1)$.)*

**Expressing $\hat{v}(t|t-1)$ Directly in Terms of Observed $v(s)$**:
To compute $\hat{v}(t|t-1)$ directly from the observations $v(s)$ ($s \le t-1$), we rewrite Eq. (3.14) in operator form using $h(0) = 1$ (so $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)q^{-k} = H(q) - 1$) and then substitute $e(t) = H^{-1}(q)v(t)$ from Eqs. (3.6) and (3.11):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{v}(t \mid t-1) &= \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)q^{-k} \right] e(t) = \left[ H(q) - 1 \right] e(t) \\
&= \frac{H(q) - 1}{H(q)} v(t) = \left[ 1 - H^{-1}(q) \right] v(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} -\tilde{h}(k)v(t-k)
\end{aligned} \tag{3.15}
$$

*(Note why the sum starts at $k=1$: since $H(q)$ is monic with $h(0)=1$, Eq. (3.10) at $\ell=0$ gives $h(0)\tilde{h}(0) = 1 \implies \tilde{h}(0) = 1$. Therefore, $1 - H^{-1}(q) = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)q^{-k} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} -\tilde{h}(k)q^{-k}$, confirming that $\hat{v}(t|t-1)$ depends strictly on past values $v(t-1), v(t-2), \dots$!)*

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (3.15) by $H(q)$ yields the alternative difference-equation form:

$$
H(q)\hat{v}(t \mid t-1) = \left[ H(q) - 1 \right] v(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)v(t-k) \tag{3.16}
$$

#### 4. Examples 3.2 & 3.3: Predicting MA(1) and AR(1) Processes

##### Example 3.2: A Moving Average Process MA(1)
For the $\text{MA}(1)$ process $v(t) = e(t) + ce(t-1)$ with $H(q) = 1 + cq^{-1}$ and $|c| < 1$:
- **Recursive Filter Form**: Using Eq. (3.16), $(1 + cq^{-1})\hat{v}(t|t-1) = cq^{-1}v(t)$, which writes out as the first-order recursion:

$$
\hat{v}(t \mid t-1) + c\hat{v}(t-1 \mid t-2) = cv(t-1) \tag{3.17}
$$

- **Explicit Infinite History Form**: Alternatively, using $\tilde{h}(k) = (-c)^k$ from Example 3.1 in Eq. (3.15) gives:

$$
\hat{v}(t \mid t-1) = -\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-c)^k v(t-k)
$$

Notice that although an $\text{MA}(1)$ process has a finite 1-step memory in terms of $e(t)$, its optimal predictor requires an *infinite* exponentially decaying memory of past observations $v(t-k)$ (or a recursive IIR filter Eq. (3.17)) because we must invert the moving average dynamics to reconstruct $e(t-1)$.

##### Example 3.3: An Autoregressive Process AR(1)
Consider the process:

$$
v(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a^k e(t-k), \qquad |a| < 1
$$

Taking the $z$-transform gives the $\text{AR}(1)$ noise filter and its finite-order inverse:

$$
H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a^k z^{-k} = \frac{1}{1 - az^{-1}} \implies H^{-1}(z) = 1 - az^{-1}
$$

Substituting $1 - H^{-1}(q) = 1 - (1 - aq^{-1}) = aq^{-1}$ into Eq. (3.15) yields the remarkably simple predictor:

$$
\hat{v}(t \mid t-1) = av(t-1) \tag{3.18}
$$

Unlike the $\text{MA}(1)$ case, the inverse filter $H^{-1}(q) = 1 - aq^{-1}$ of an $\text{AR}(1)$ process is a *finite impulse response (FIR)* polynomial, so the optimal predictor depends only on the most recent observation $v(t-1)$.

#### 5. One-Step-Ahead Prediction of System Output $y(t)$

Now consider the complete linear system model from Eq. (3.1):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + v(t), \qquad v(t) = H(q)e(t)
$$

Assume that past outputs $y(s)$ and inputs $u(s)$ are known for all $s \le t-1$ (and recall $G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)q^{-k}$ has at least a one-step delay, so $G(q)u(t)$ only depends on $u(s)$ for $s \le t-1$). Since

$$
v(s) = y(s) - G(q)u(s) \tag{3.19}
$$

knowing $y(s)$ and $u(s)$ for $s \le t-1$ implies that $v(s)$ is also known for all $s \le t-1$. Taking the conditional expectation of $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + v(t)$ given all data up to $t-1$, and substituting Eq. (3.15) for $\hat{v}(t|t-1)$ and Eq. (3.19) for $v(t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}(t \mid t-1) &= G(q)u(t) + \hat{v}(t \mid t-1) \\
&= G(q)u(t) + \left[ 1 - H^{-1}(q) \right] v(t) \\
&= G(q)u(t) + \left[ 1 - H^{-1}(q) \right] \left[ y(t) - G(q)u(t) \right]
\end{aligned}
$$

Expanding the brackets—notice that $G(q)u(t)$ cancels $-G(q)u(t)$, leaving $+H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t)$—gives the **fundamental one-step-ahead predictor equations**:

$$
\boxed{\hat{y}(t \mid t-1) = H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t) + \left[ 1 - H^{-1}(q) \right] y(t)} \tag{3.20}
$$

or, multiplying both sides by $H(q)$:

$$
\boxed{H(q)\hat{y}(t \mid t-1) = G(q)u(t) + \left[ H(q) - 1 \right] y(t)} \tag{3.21}
$$

**Explicit Convolution Expansion**:
Equations (3.20) and (3.21) are operator shorthand for infinite series expansions. Let $\{\ell(k)\}$ be defined by the power series expansion of $G(z)/H(z)$:

$$
\frac{G(z)}{H(z)} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \ell(k)z^{-k} \tag{3.22}
$$

*(Condition for Existence: This expansion exists and is stable for $|z| \ge 1$ if $H(z)$ has no zeros in $|z| \ge 1$ and $G(z)$ has no poles in $|z| \ge 1$. Moreover, because $G(\infty) = 0$ and $H(\infty) = 1$, the ratio starts at $k = 1$.)*
Then Eq. (3.20) writes out explicitly in the time domain as:

$$
\hat{y}(t \mid t-1) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \ell(k)u(t-k) + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} -\tilde{h}(k)y(t-k) \tag{3.23}
$$

---

#### 6. Unknown Initial Conditions (Finite Data Records $0 \le s \le t-1$)

In Eqs. (3.20) and (3.23), we assumed that the entire data record from $s = -\infty$ to $t-1$ is available. In practical engineering applications, however, measurements are only available over a finite interval $[0, t-1]$ (i.e., $u(s)$ and $y(s)$ are known only for $0 \le s \le t-1$).

- **Zero-Padding Approximation**: The simplest practical procedure is to replace the unobserved data prior to $s = 0$ by zero in Eq. (3.23), truncating the infinite sums at $k = t$:

$$
\hat{y}(t \mid t-1) \approx \sum_{k=1}^{t} \ell(k)u(t-k) + \sum_{k=1}^{t} -\tilde{h}(k)y(t-k) \tag{3.24}
$$

- **Relationship to Exact Finite-Data Prediction (Kalman Filtering)**: One must realize that Eq. (3.24) is an *approximation* to the true conditional expectation $E[y(t) \mid y_0^{t-1}, u_0^{t-1}]$. The exact conditional expectation given finite data involves *time-varying* filter coefficients and can be computed recursively using the **Kalman filter** (see Eq. (4.94) in Chapter 4).
- **Why Eq. (3.24) Suffices in Practice**: Because $H^{-1}(z)$ and $G(z)/H(z)$ are analytic in $|z| \ge 1$ (no poles on or outside the unit circle), the impulse responses $\{\ell(k)\}$ and $\{\tilde{h}(k)\}$ **decay exponentially** with lag $k$ (Problem 3G.1). Consequently, the transient error caused by setting unknown pre-zero data to $0$ dies out exponentially fast as $t$ increases, making Eq. (3.24) completely satisfactory for most practical purposes.

---

#### 7. The Prediction Error (Innovations)

Subtracting the one-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ in Eq. (3.20) from the true system equation $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ yields the **one-step-ahead prediction error**:

$$
\boxed{y(t) - \hat{y}(t \mid t-1) = -H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t) + H^{-1}(q)y(t) = e(t)} \tag{3.25}
$$

*(Algebraic check: $y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1) = y(t) - H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t) - [1 - H^{-1}(q)]y(t) = H^{-1}(q)[y(t) - G(q)u(t)] = H^{-1}(q)v(t) = e(t)$.)*

> [!note] Physical Meaning of $e(t)$ as the "Innovation"
> Equation (3.25) reveals the profound physical meaning of the white noise sequence $e(t)$ in the model $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$: **$e(t)$ is the exact part of the output $y(t)$ that cannot be predicted from past data**. Because it represents the "new information" arriving at time $t$, $e(t)$ is called the **innovation** at time $t$.

#### 8. $k$-Step-Ahead Prediction of $y(t)$ ($\star$)

We now generalize from $1$-step-ahead to $k$-step-ahead prediction ($k \ge 1$).

##### Step 1: $k$-Step-Ahead Prediction of the Disturbance $v(t+k)$
Suppose we have observed $v(s)$ for $s \le t$ and want to predict $v(t+k)$. Writing out the moving average expansion of $v(t+k)$ and splitting the sum at index $\ell = k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v(t+k) &= \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} h(\ell)e(t+k-\ell) \\
&= \underbrace{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1} h(\ell)e(t+k-\ell)}_{\text{future shocks } e(t+1), \dots, e(t+k)} + \underbrace{\sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} h(\ell)e(t+k-\ell)}_{\text{past/present shocks } e(t), e(t-1), \dots}
\end{aligned} \tag{3.26}
$$

To express these two pieces cleanly as filters operating at the right time indices, we define two component filters from $H(q)$:

$$
\bar{H}_k(q) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1} h(\ell)q^{-\ell}, \qquad \tilde{H}_k(q) = \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} h(\ell)q^{-\ell+k} \tag{3.27}
$$

> [!tip] Understanding the Filter Split $H(q) = \bar{H}_k(q) + q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q)$
> - $\bar{H}_k(q)$ is a **finite impulse response (FIR) polynomial** of order $k-1$ in $q^{-1}$ containing the first $k$ impulse response coefficients $h(0), h(1), \dots, h(k-1)$. Applied to $e(t+k)$, $\bar{H}_k(q)e(t+k) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1} h(\ell)e(t+k-\ell)$ captures the unpredictable future innovations occurring at times $t+1, \dots, t+k$.
> - $\tilde{H}_k(q)$ is a **causal filter** (since $-\ell + k \le 0$ for $\ell \ge k$) whose impulse response starts with $h(k) + h(k+1)q^{-1} + \dots$. Because we factored out $q^{-k}$, applying $\tilde{H}_k(q)$ to $e(t)$ gives:
>
> $$
> \tilde{H}_k(q)e(t) = \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} h(\ell)q^{-\ell+k}e(t) = \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} h(\ell)e(t+k-\ell)
> $$
>
> Notice how the identity $H(q) = \bar{H}_k(q) + q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q)$ holds by inspection!

At time $t$, the second sum in Eq. (3.26) depends only on $\{e(s)\}_{s \le t}$ and is therefore completely known from $v_{-\infty}^t$, whereas the first sum depends on future innovations $\{e(t+1), \dots, e(t+k)\}$, which are independent of everything up to time $t$ and have zero mean. Thus, the conditional mean of $v(t+k)$ given $v_{-\infty}^t$ is:

$$
\hat{v}(t+k \mid t) = \sum_{\ell=k}^{\infty} h(\ell)e(t+k-\ell) = \tilde{H}_k(q)e(t) = \tilde{H}_k(q) \cdot H^{-1}(q)v(t)
$$

##### Step 2: $k$-Step-Ahead Prediction of $y(t+k)$
Now suppose we have measured outputs $y_{-\infty}^t$ up to time $t$ and know the input sequence $u_{-\infty}^{t+k-1}$ up to time $t+k-1$ (including future planned/known inputs from $t$ to $t+k-1$). Since $y(t+k) = G(q)u(t+k) + v(t+k)$, the conditional expectation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}\left(t+k \;\middle|\; y_{-\infty}^t, u_{-\infty}^{t+k-1}\right) &\triangleq \hat{y}(t+k \mid t) = G(q)u(t+k) + \hat{v}(t+k \mid t) \\
&= G(q)u(t+k) + \tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)v(t) \\
&= G(q)u(t+k) + \tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)\left[ y(t) - G(q)u(t) \right]
\end{aligned} \tag{3.28}
$$

Notice that in the last line of Eq. (3.28), $G(q)u(t+k)$ is evaluated at $t+k$, whereas $G(q)u(t) = q^{-k}G(q)u(t+k)$ is evaluated at $t$. To combine the $u(t+k)$ terms, we introduce the **$k$-step predictor filter $W_k(q)$**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_k(q) &\triangleq 1 - q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q) = \left[ H(q) - q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q) \right] H^{-1}(q) \\
&= \bar{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)
\end{aligned} \tag{3.29}
$$

Using $1 - q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q) = W_k(q)$ in Eq. (3.28) combines $G(q)u(t+k) - \tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)q^{-k}G(q)u(t+k)$ into $W_k(q)G(q)u(t+k)$, yielding:

$$
\hat{y}(t+k \mid t) = W_k(q)G(q)u(t+k) + \tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)y(t) \tag{3.30}
$$

Or, shifting the time index by $k$ steps ($t+k \to t$ and $t \to t-k$) and noting from the first equality of Eq. (3.29) that $q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)y(t) = [1 - W_k(q)]y(t)$, we obtain the canonical boxed form of the **$k$-step-ahead predictor**:

$$
\boxed{\hat{y}(t \mid t-k) = W_k(q)G(q)u(t) + \left[ 1 - W_k(q) \right] y(t)} \tag{3.31}
$$

- **Consistency Check ($k = 1$ vs. $k \to \infty$)**:
  - When $k = 1$, $\bar{H}_1(q) = h(0) = 1$, so $W_1(q) = H^{-1}(q)$, and Eq. (3.31) reduces identically to the one-step-ahead predictor Eq. (3.20).
  - As $k \to \infty$, $\bar{H}_k(q) \to H(q)$, so $W_k(q) = \bar{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q) \to 1$. Thus $\hat{y}(t|t-\infty) \to G(q)u(t)$, recovering the pure **simulator** $y_u(t) = G(q)u(t)$ of Section 3.1!
- **Equivalent One-Step-Ahead Model Interpretation**: Comparing Eq. (3.31) with Eq. (3.20) shows that the $k$-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|t-k)$ has the exact same structure as a *one-step-ahead* predictor associated with the modified noise model:

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + W_k^{-1}(q)e(t) \tag{3.32}
$$

##### Step 3: The $k$-Step-Ahead Prediction Error $e_k(t+k)$
Subtracting Eq. (3.30) from $y(t+k)$ gives the $k$-step-ahead prediction error $e_k(t+k)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e_k(t+k) &\triangleq y(t+k) - \hat{y}(t+k \mid t) = -W_k(q)G(q)u(t+k) + \left[ q^k - \tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q) \right] y(t) \\
&= W_k(q)\left[ y(t+k) - G(q)u(t+k) \right] = W_k(q)H(q)e(t+k) \\
&= \bar{H}_k(q)e(t+k)
\end{aligned} \tag{3.33}
$$

where we used $q^k - \tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q) = q^k[1 - q^{-k}\tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)] = q^k W_k(q)$ in the second equality and $W_k(q)H(q) = \bar{H}_k(q)$ from Eq. (3.29) in the fourth equality. Since $\bar{H}_k(q) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1} h(\ell)q^{-\ell}$ is a polynomial in $q^{-1}$ of order $k-1$, **the $k$-step-ahead prediction error is a moving average of order $k-1$ of the future innovations $e(t+k), e(t+k-1), \dots, e(t+1)$**.

#### 9. The Multivariable Case ($\star$)

For a multivariable (MIMO) system with $p$-dimensional output $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$ (see Eq. (3.1) or Eq. (2.90)), $H(q)$ is a $p \times p$ matrix filter. We define its inverse $p \times p$ matrix filter $H^{-1}(q)$ as:

$$
H^{-1}(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)q^{-k} \tag{3.34a}
$$

where $\tilde{h}(k) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ are the matrix impulse response coefficients defined by the Laurent expansion of the inverted $p \times p$ transfer matrix $[H(z)]^{-1}$:

$$
\left[ H(z) \right]^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \tilde{h}(k)z^{-k} \tag{3.34}
$$

- **Invertibility Condition for MIMO**: The expansion Eq. (3.34) is interpreted entrywise in the matrix $[H(z)]^{-1}$ (formed by standard matrix inversion, $[H(z)]^{-1} = \text{adj}(H(z)) / \det H(z)$). It exists and is analytic in $|z| \ge 1$ provided the scalar determinant function **$\det H(z)$ has no zeros in $|z| \ge 1$**.
- **Preservation of Predictor Formulas**: With $H^{-1}(q)$ thus defined, all previous derivations and predictor formulas carry over directly to the multivariable case (being careful to respect matrix multiplication order, e.g., $H^{-1}(q)G(q)$ and $\tilde{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)$).

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: Why $\hat{v}(t|t-1) = [1 - H^{-1}(q)]v(t)$ Is Strictly Causal (No Algebraic Loop!)**: At first glance, Eq. (3.15) $\hat{v}(t|t-1) = [1 - H^{-1}(q)]v(t)$ and Eq. (3.20) $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t) + [1 - H^{-1}(q)]y(t)$ look like they use $v(t)$ and $y(t)$ at time $t$ to predict time $t$! The secret is that **$H(q)$ is monic ($h(0) = 1$)**, which forces its inverse $H^{-1}(q)$ to also be monic ($\tilde{h}(0) = 1$). Subtracting $H^{-1}(q)$ from $1$ cancels the $q^0$ feedthrough term ($1 - (1 + \tilde{h}(1)q^{-1} + \dots) = -\tilde{h}(1)q^{-1} - \dots$), guaranteeing that $[1 - H^{-1}(q)]y(t)$ depends *only* on past outputs $y(t-1), y(t-2), \dots$. Similarly, because $G(q)$ contains at least a one-sample delay ($g(0) = 0$), $H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t)$ depends *only* on past inputs $u(t-1), u(t-2), \dots$.
> - **Leap 2: How $k$-Step Prediction Interpolates Between 1-Step Prediction and Pure Simulation**: Watch how the filter $W_k(q) = \bar{H}_k(q)H^{-1}(q)$ in Eq. (3.31) governs the balance between trusting the deterministic input simulation $G(q)u(t)$ and correcting it with output measurements $y(t)$. For $k = 1$, $W_1(q) = H^{-1}(q)$. As the horizon $k \to \infty$, $\bar{H}_k(q) \to H(q)$, so $W_k(q) \to 1$ and $1 - W_k(q) \to 0$: far into the future, past output measurements lose all predictive power over the noise, and the optimal predictor gracefully degenerates into the pure open-loop simulator $G(q)u(t)$ from Section 3.1.
> - **Notation Trap: $\tilde{H}(q)$ vs. $\tilde{H}_k(q)$ vs. $\bar{H}_k(q)$**: Ljung uses $\tilde{H}(q)$ on page 64 (Eq. 3.6) for the inverse filter $H^{-1}(q)$ with coefficients $\tilde{h}(k)$, and then later on page 70 (Eq. 3.27) introduces $\tilde{H}_k(q)$ (with a subscript $k$) for the shifted tail of $H(q)$ alongside $\bar{H}_k(q)$ for the truncated head of $H(q)$. Do not confuse the unsubscripted inverse $\tilde{H}(q) = H^{-1}(q)$ with the subscripted tail filter $\tilde{H}_k(q)$!
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why does an $\text{MA}(1)$ noise model $v(t) = e(t) + ce(t-1)$ require an *infinite* history of past observations $v(t-k)$ to predict one step ahead, whereas an $\text{AR}(1)$ noise model requires only a *single* past observation $v(t-1)$?
>   2. What happens to the predictor Eq. (3.20) if $H(z)$ has a zero outside the unit circle ($|c| > 1$ in Example 3.1)? Why can't we use that $H(q)$ for stable prediction?
