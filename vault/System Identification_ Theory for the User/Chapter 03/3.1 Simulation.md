# 3.1 Simulation

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 3**: This opening section bridges the abstract linear time-invariant (LTI) system descriptions of Chapter 2 with their practical engineering applications, starting with pure open-loop simulation. By separating the undisturbed deterministic response $y^*(t)$ from a synthetic stochastic disturbance realization $v^*(t)$ — where no historical output measurements are available — it establishes the foundational contrast for Section 3.2, where observed outputs *are* available and can be exploited to predict future behavior.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Before diving into Section 3.1, the opening paragraph of Chapter 3 sets out **two foundational pillars** that govern the entire remainder of the textbook:
1. **Prediction is the heartbeat of system identification**: Knowing how to predict future output values from a candidate model and past measurements (developed in Section 3.2) is the single most essential building block for constructing parameter estimation methods (Prediction Error Methods in Chapter 7).
2. **Application-driven modeling effort**: A mathematical model is never an end in itself; the effort and structural complexity invested in identifying a system must be directly tied to its **intended application** (simulation, prediction, or feedback control). Illustrating how models are actually used reveals what properties are required for a system description to be *adequate* for its intended purpose.

### Why Start with Simulation?
The most direct and intuitive way to use a mathematical system description is **simulation**: testing how the system responds to hypothetical input scenarios $\{u^*(t)\}$ on a computer *before* or *instead of* experimenting on the physical plant.
- **Physical & Engineering Reality**: Whether designing a simple industrial process controller or building high-fidelity flight simulators and nuclear power station training simulators (which use nonlinear models as in Chapter 5, yet follow the exact same conceptual paradigm), engineers need a "virtual laboratory" to evaluate system behavior safely and cheaply under diverse operating conditions.
- **Deterministic vs. Stochastic Simulation**: In pure simulation, we do **not** have access to actual measurements from the plant during the test run. Therefore:
  - We compute the **undisturbed output** $y^*(t)$ by passing our chosen test input $u^*(t)$ through the deterministic plant transfer operator $G(q)$.
  - Because the real system is also subject to unmeasurable random disturbances $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$, we cannot know the *exact* noise waveform that will occur in the future. However, we can assess the *statistical character and magnitude* of the disturbance influence by generating a synthetic white-noise sequence $e^*(t)$ via a computer random-number generator (with variance $\lambda$) and filtering it through $H(q)$ to obtain a typical disturbance realization $v^*(t)$.
- **Connecting to Frequency-Domain Model Adequacy**: Because simulation operates strictly in **open loop** (driven only by the user-selected input $u^*(t)$ without corrective feedback from measured outputs), if we approximate a true system $G_0(q)$ by a model $\tilde{G}(q)$, any undisturbed simulation error $\tilde{y}(t) = \big(G_0(q) - \tilde{G}(q)\big)u^*(t) = \Delta G(q)u^*(t)$ accumulates directly through the transfer function mismatch $\Delta G(e^{i\omega})$. From Chapter 2's spectral theory, the error spectrum is $\Phi_{\tilde{y}}(\omega) = |\Delta G(e^{i\omega})|^2 \Phi_{u^*}(\omega)$, meaning that a model used for simulation only needs to be highly accurate over the frequency bands where the intended input spectrum $\Phi_{u^*}(\omega)$ has significant energy.

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 1. Baseline Standard System Description — Eq. (3.1)
Throughout Chapter 3, the true or postulated linear time-invariant (LTI) system is assumed to be given in the standard additive input-disturbance form introduced in Eq. (2.97):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t) \tag{3.1}
$$

- **Underlying Assumptions**:
  1. **Linearity and Time-Invariance**: Both the deterministic path $G(q)$ and the noise filter $H(q)$ are linear, time-invariant causal operators expanded in non-positive powers of the shift operator $q$.
  2. **Additive Stochastic Disturbance**: The disturbance $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ enters additively at the output and is generated by a white-noise sequence $\{e(t)\}$ with zero mean and variance $\lambda$.
  3. **Known Initial / Pre-Window Conditions**: When evaluating $G(q)u^*(t)$ over a finite horizon $t = 1, 2, \dots, N$, the infinite impulse response convolution $G(q)u^*(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)u^*(t-k)$ implicitly requires specifying or assuming values for $u^*(t)$ for $t \le 0$ (typically quiescent zero initial conditions, $u^*(t) = 0$ for $t \le 0$).

---

#### 2. Undisturbed Deterministic Simulation — Eq. (3.2)
To evaluate how the system responds to a candidate input scenario without the masking effect of random noise, the user selects a deterministic input sequence $u^*(t)$ for $t = 1, 2, \dots, N$ and applies it strictly to the input-output transfer operator $G(q)$ of Eq. (3.1):

$$
y^*(t) = G(q)u^*(t), \quad t = 1, 2, \dots, N \tag{3.2}
$$

- **Physical Interpretation**: $y^*(t)$ is the exact counterfactual trajectory that the system (3.1) would produce had there been **no disturbances** ($e(t) \equiv 0$). Notice that $y^*(t)$ depends *exclusively* on $G(q)$ and $u^*(t)$; the noise model $H(q)$ plays zero role in deterministic simulation.

---

#### 3. Stochastic Disturbance Realization — Eq. (3.3)
To evaluate how sensitive the system response is to the stochastic disturbance term $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$, we cannot predict the exact future values of $e(t)$ from scratch, but we can synthesize a statistically representative trajectory:
1. Use a digital random-number generator on a computer to draw a sequence of independent numbers $e^*(t)$ for $t = 1, 2, \dots, N$ that acts as a sample realization of a zero-mean white-noise stochastic process with variance $\lambda$.
2. Pass $e^*(t)$ through the noise-shaping filter $H(q)$ to compute the simulated disturbance sequence:

$$
v^*(t) = H(q)e^*(t) \tag{3.3}
$$

3. **Combined Presentation**: By presenting both $y^*(t)$ and $v^*(t)$ (or their superposition $y^*(t) + v^*(t)$) to the user, the engineer gains both the clean nominal response to $\{u^*(t)\}$ and a realistic visual/quantitative sense of the signal-to-noise ratio and correlation structure of the fluctuations around that nominal trajectory.

---

#### 4. Frequency-Domain Perspective on Simulation Model Accuracy (Connection to Chapter 2)
Why does the distinction between **simulation** (Section 3.1) and **prediction** (Section 3.2) matter so much for model adequacy?
- Suppose the true deterministic transfer function is $G_0(q)$, and we use an approximate model $\tilde{G}(q)$ to simulate the undisturbed response to $u^*(t)$:

$$
y_0^*(t) = G_0(q)u^*(t), \qquad \tilde{y}^*(t) = \tilde{G}(q)u^*(t)
$$

- Defining the transfer function error $\Delta G(q) = G_0(q) - \tilde{G}(q)$, the undisturbed simulation error is a linear filtered version of $u^*(t)$:

$$
\tilde{y}(t) = y_0^*(t) - \tilde{y}^*(t) = \Delta G(q)u^*(t)
$$

- By the LTI spectral transformation theorem from Chapter 2 (Eq. 2.51), the power spectrum of the simulation error is:

$$
\Phi_{\tilde{y}}(\omega) = \left|\Delta G\left(e^{i\omega}\right)\right|^2 \Phi_{u^*}(\omega)
$$

- By Parseval's theorem (Eq. 2.47), the mean-square undisturbed simulation error is the integral of this spectrum:

$$
\bar{E}\tilde{y}^2(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \left|\Delta G\left(e^{i\omega}\right)\right|^2 \Phi_{u^*}(\omega)\,d\omega
$$

- **Key Takeaway**: For pure open-loop simulation, model quality depends **only on $G(q)$** (not $H(q)$), and the frequency-domain error $\Delta G(e^{i\omega})$ is weighted directly by the spectrum $\Phi_{u^*}(\omega)$ of the simulation input scenario.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 — Simulation vs. Prediction (Why $H(q)$ is Used Differently)**: In Section 3.1 (Simulation), we have **no measured outputs** $y(s)$ from the actual plant during the run. Thus, $H(q)$ can only be used to filter synthetic random numbers $e^*(t)$ to visualize a *typical* realization $v^*(t) = H(q)e^*(t)$. In Section 3.2 (Prediction), we *do* observe actual past outputs $y(s)$ for $s \le t-1$, which lets us **invert** $H(q)$ to reconstruct the actual past disturbances $v(s)$ and predict the specific future value $v(t)$!
> - **Leap 2 — Model Complexity Depends on Application**: Do not assume that "system identification" means finding the single universal truth of a plant. A low-order linear model (3.1) may be completely adequate for regulating a system around an operating point, whereas a full-envelope training simulator requires a complex nonlinear model (Chapter 5) — yet both share the exact same architectural separation of deterministic response and disturbance realization.
> - **Notation Trap — The Superscript Asterisk $(\cdot)^*$**: In Ljung's notation in Section 3.1, the superscript asterisk in $u^*(t)$, $y^*(t)$, $e^*(t)$, and $v^*(t)$ does **not** denote complex conjugation or an optimal estimate; it denotes a **user-chosen or computer-synthesized simulation sequence** (as opposed to true physical signals $u(t), y(t), e(t), v(t)$ measured on the real plant). Later in Section 3.2, the optimal predictor will be denoted with a hat, $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why do we compute $y^*(t)$ and $v^*(t)$ separately in Eqs. (3.2)–(3.3) rather than only computing their sum $y^*(t) + v^*(t)$? *(Hint: How does seeing the deterministic trend separately from the noise envelope help an engineer evaluate a design scenario?)*
>   2. If our sole intended use of an identified model is undisturbed simulation $y^*(t) = G(q)u^*(t)$, why will we still care about the noise model $H(q)$ when we move to parameter estimation in later chapters?
