# 4.2 A Family of Transfer-Function Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 4**: Building directly on the general parametrized frequency-domain description $y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t)$ introduced in Section 4.1, Section 4.2 constructs the complete taxonomy of **black-box transfer-function model structures** (ARX, FIR, ARMAX, ARARX, ARARMAX, OE, BJ, and the unified 5-polynomial family) by parametrizing $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ as rational functions in $q^{-1}$, deriving their one-step-ahead predictors as linear or pseudolinear regressions, and extending these structures to orthonormal basis expansions, continuous-time transfer functions, and multivariable Matrix Fraction Descriptions (MFDs) before Section 4.3 transitions to physically structured state-space models.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

When modeling a linear time-invariant (LTI) system without detailed prior physical laws—a paradigm known as **black-box modeling**—the most direct way to parametrize the input-to-output transfer function $G(q, \theta)$ and the disturbance filter $H(q, \theta)$ in

$$
y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t)
$$

is to represent both as rational functions of the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$ and collect their numerator and denominator polynomial coefficients into the parameter vector $\theta$.

However, choosing *which* polynomials appear in the denominators and numerators of $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ creates a fundamental engineering trade-off between **computational simplicity** (linearity of the predictor in $\theta$) and **physical realism** (independent parametrization of plant dynamics vs. disturbance dynamics):

1. **Equation Error Models (ARX & FIR) — Computational Simplicity vs. Coupled Noise Dynamics**:
   - In an **ARX** (AutoRegressive with eXogenous input) model, $G(q, \theta) = B(q)/A(q)$ and $H(q, \theta) = 1/A(q)$ share the exact same denominator polynomial $A(q)$.
   - **Why introduce it?** Because multiplying through by $A(q)$ yields $A(q)y(t) = B(q)u(t) + e(t)$, making the one-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$ a **linear regression** in $\theta$. The regression vector $\varphi(t)$ depends *only* on measured past data, so the least-squares estimation problem is convex and solvable in closed form.
   - **The engineering flaw**: Physically, white noise $e(t)$ rarely enters a process directly at the internal state before passing through the full plant poles $1/A(q)$. Forcing $G$ and $H$ to share $A(q)$ couples the noise spectrum to the plant poles.
   - Setting $A(q) = 1$ ($n_a = 0$) gives the **FIR** (Finite Impulse Response) model, which is simultaneously a linear regression and an output error model, though slowly decaying systems require a very high order $n_b$.

2. **General Equation Error Family (ARMAX, ARARX, ARARMAX) — Adding Disturbance Flexibility**:
   - Because pure white equation error $e(t)$ in ARX lacks flexibility to describe colored disturbances, **ARMAX** replaces $e(t)$ with a Moving Average (MA) term $C(q)e(t)$, **ARARX** replaces it with an autoregression $\frac{1}{D(q)}e(t)$, and **ARARMAX** uses a full ARMA disturbance $\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$ prior to the shared plant denominator $1/A(q)$.
   - **The trade-off**: Whenever $C(q) \neq 1$ or $D(q) \neq 1$, the predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t, \theta)\theta$ becomes a **pseudolinear regression**—it looks like a scalar product, but the regression vector $\varphi(t, \theta)$ now contains past *prediction errors* $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$ or auxiliary filtered residuals $v(t-k, \theta)$ that depend nonlinearly on $\theta$, requiring iterative numerical optimization.

3. **Output Error (OE) & Box-Jenkins (BJ) Models — Physical Decoupling of Plant and Noise**:
   - From a physical standpoint, plant dynamics and measurement/disturbance noise arise from independent mechanisms.
   - The **Output Error (OE)** model assumes an undisturbed deterministic output $w(t, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t)$ is corrupted at the sensor by additive white measurement noise $e(t)$, so $H(q, \theta) = 1$. Its predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = w(t, \theta)$ is a pure simulation driven *solely by past inputs* $u$.
   - The **Box-Jenkins (BJ)** model generalizes OE by modeling the output additive disturbance itself as an ARMA process $\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$, yielding completely independent rational parametrizations $G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}$ and $H(q, \theta) = \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}$.

4. **The Generalized 5-Polynomial Family, Orthonormal Bases, Continuous-Time, and Multivariable MFD**:
   - To avoid writing separate software and proofs for 32 possible combinations of polynomials, Ljung unifies them into the master structure $A(q)y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$.
   - Furthermore, to capture slowly decaying impulse responses without losing the linear regression property of FIR models, one can expand $G(q, \theta)$ in **orthonormal basis functions** (such as **Laguerre filters** parameterized by a dominant pole estimate $\alpha$). Finally, the rational transfer-function philosophy extends naturally to **continuous-time black-box models** $G_c(s, \theta)$ and multivariable **Matrix Fraction Descriptions (MFDs)** $A^{-1}(q)B(q)$.

| $A_i, B_i, C_i$ | Coefficient matrices in multivariable difference equations / Matrix Fraction Descriptions (MFD) | $\mathbb{R}^{p \times p}, \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}, \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

### 4.2.1 Equation Error Model Structure (ARX and FIR)

The simplest input-output relationship is obtained by describing the system directly as a linear difference equation in which the white-noise term $e(t)$ enters as a direct error in the difference equation—hence the name **equation error model (structure)**:

$$
y(t) + a_1 y(t-1) + \dots + a_{n_a} y(t-n_a) = b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_{n_b} u(t-n_b) + e(t) \tag{4.7}
$$

The adjustable parameter vector is

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_{n_a} & b_1 & \dots & b_{n_b} \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.8}
$$

Introducing the monic autoregressive polynomial $A(q)$ and the exogenous input polynomial $B(q)$ in the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$:

$$
A(q) = 1 + a_1 q^{-1} + \dots + a_{n_a} q^{-n_a}
$$

$$
B(q) = b_1 q^{-1} + \dots + b_{n_b} q^{-n_b}
$$

we see that `(4.7)` can be written as $A(q)y(t) = B(q)u(t) + e(t)$, which corresponds to the general LTI model $y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t)$ from `(4.4)` with:

$$
G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}, \qquad H(q, \theta) = \frac{1}{A(q)} \tag{4.9}
$$

> [!note] Notation Remark: Why $A(q)$ Instead of $A(q^{-1})$?
> Ljung writes $A(q)$ with $q$ as the argument even though $A(q)$ is a polynomial in the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$. This convention is chosen strictly to maintain consistency with the standard definition of the $z$-transform (Section 2.2, Eq. `(2.17)`), where causality and delay operators are written in negative powers of $z$ (or $q$).

- **Terminology**:
  - **ARX Model**: Model `(4.7)` is called an **ARX** model, where **AR** refers to the *autoregressive* part $A(q)y(t)$ and **X** refers to the extra *exogenous* input $B(q)u(t)$ (terminology originating in econometrics).
  - **FIR (Finite Impulse Response) Model**: In the special case where $n_a = 0$, we have $A(q) = 1$, so $y(t) = B(q)u(t) + e(t)$ is modeled as a finite impulse response, which is ubiquitous in signal-processing applications.

![[assets/fig_4_2_arx_structure.png]]

> [!tip] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 4.1 — The ARX Model Structure)
> - **Signal Flow**: The deterministic input $u(t)$ passes through the rational plant transfer function block $\frac{B}{A}$, while the white-noise disturbance $e(t)$ passes through the autoregressive all-pole filter block $\frac{1}{A}$ before the two paths sum to form the measured output $y(t)$.
> - **Physical Interpretation & Limitation**: Because both blocks share the exact same denominator $A(q)$, the block diagram makes clear why ARX is often physically unnatural: any measurement or process noise $e(t)$ is forced to resonate through the exact same poles $1/A(q)$ as the input $u(t)$. Its compelling advantage, however, is that its predictor defines a *linear regression*.

### 4.2.2 Linear Regressions

To derive the one-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ for the ARX model `(4.7)`, recall the general predictor formula `(4.6)` from Section 4.1:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = H^{-1}(q, \theta)G(q, \theta)u(t) + \left[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)\right]y(t)
$$

Substituting $G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}$ and $H^{-1}(q, \theta) = A(q)$ from `(4.9)` into `(4.6)` yields:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = B(q)u(t) + \left[1 - A(q)\right]y(t) \tag{4.10}
$$

Expanding $B(q)$ and $1 - A(q) = -a_1 q^{-1} - \dots - a_{n_a} q^{-n_a}$ explicitly in the time domain gives:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = -a_1 y(t-1) - \dots - a_{n_a} y(t-n_a) + b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_{n_b} u(t-n_b)
$$

> [!note] Deterministic Interpretation of the ARX Predictor
> As emphasized in Section 3.3, one does not need a stochastic framework to motivate `(4.10)`: if the equation error term $e(t)$ in `(4.7)` is simply regarded as "insignificant" or "difficult to guess," dropping $e(t)$ from `(4.7)` immediately produces `(4.10)`.

Now introduce the **regression vector** $\varphi(t)$ containing the known past outputs and inputs:

$$
\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t-1) & \dots & -y(t-n_a) & u(t-1) & \dots & u(t-n_b) \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.11}
$$

Then the predictor `(4.10)` can be written compactly as the inner product:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \theta^T \varphi(t) = \varphi^T(t)\theta \tag{4.12}
$$

Because $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is a scalar product between a **known data vector** $\varphi(t)$ (completely free of $\theta$) and the parameter vector $\theta$, such a model is called a **linear regression** in statistics. In case some coefficients of the polynomials $A(q)$ and $B(q)$ are known a priori, moving those known terms into a known signal $\mu(t)$ yields the affine linear regression form:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta + \mu(t) \tag{4.13}
$$

### 4.2.3 ARMAX Model Structure

The basic disadvantage of the simple ARX model `(4.7)` is its lack of flexibility in describing the frequency properties of the disturbance term. We can add flexibility by modeling the equation error in `(4.7)` as a **moving average (MA)** of white noise rather than pure white noise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) + a_1 y(t-1) + \dots + a_{n_a} y(t-n_a) &= b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_{n_b} u(t-n_b) \\
&\quad + e(t) + c_1 e(t-1) + \dots + c_{n_c} e(t-n_c)
\end{aligned} \tag{4.14}
$$

Defining the monic moving-average polynomial $C(q)$:

$$
C(q) = 1 + c_1 q^{-1} + \dots + c_{n_c} q^{-n_c}
$$

Equation `(4.14)` is written in operator form as:

$$
A(q)y(t) = B(q)u(t) + C(q)e(t) \tag{4.15}
$$

which corresponds to `(4.4)` with transfer functions:

$$
G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}, \qquad H(q, \theta) = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)} \tag{4.16}
$$

and parameter vector:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & \dots & a_{n_a} & b_1 & \dots & b_{n_b} & c_1 & \dots & c_{n_c} \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.17}
$$

- **ARIMA(X) Variant for Slow Disturbances / Drifts**: When a system experiences slow, non-stationary or drifting disturbances, it is useful to enforce an explicit integration (a pole at $z = 1$) in the noise description. Replacing $y(t)$ and $u(t)$ in `(4.15)` by their first differences $\Delta y(t) = y(t) - y(t-1) = (1 - q^{-1})y(t)$ and $\Delta u(t) = u(t) - u(t-1)$ yields the **ARIMA(X)** model (*AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average with eXogenous input*, Box & Jenkins, 1970).

---

### 4.2.4 Pseudolinear Regressions

Let us derive the one-step-ahead predictor for the ARMAX model `(4.15)`. Inserting `(4.16)` into the general predictor `(4.6)` gives:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \frac{B(q)}{C(q)}u(t) + \left[1 - \frac{A(q)}{C(q)}\right]y(t)
$$

Multiplying both sides by $C(q)$ shows that the predictor satisfies the linear difference equation:

$$
C(q)\hat{y}(t|\theta) = B(q)u(t) + \left[C(q) - A(q)\right]y(t) \tag{4.18}
$$

- **Stability Assumption & Initial Conditions**: Equation `(4.18)` means that $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is computed by filtering $u$ and $y$ through an IIR filter whose denominator is $C(q)$ (requiring $C(z)$ to have all zeros strictly inside the unit circle for predictor stability). Starting this recursion at $t = 0$ requires initial conditions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\hat{y}(0|\theta), \dots, \hat{y}(-n_c + 1|\theta) \\
&y(0), \dots, y(-n^* + 1), \qquad n^* = \max(n_c, n_a) \\
&u(0), \dots, u(-n_b + 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

If unknown prior values are set to zero, the transient error between the initialized predictor and the true infinite-horizon predictor decays geometrically as $c \cdot \mu^t$, where $\mu < 1$ is the maximum magnitude of the zeros of $C(z)$. Alternatively, one can start the recursion at $t = \max(n^*, n_b)$ and treat the unknown initial predictions $\hat{y}(k|\theta), k = 1, \dots, n_c$ as additional parameters in $\theta$.

- **Algebraic Derivation of the Pseudolinear Form**: To rewrite `(4.18)` in formal analogy with the linear regression `(4.12)`, add $\left[1 - C(q)\right]\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ to both sides of `(4.18)`:
  1. Left-hand side: $C(q)\hat{y}(t|\theta) + \left[1 - C(q)\right]\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \hat{y}(t|\theta)$.
  2. Right-hand side: $B(q)u(t) + \left[C(q) - A(q)\right]y(t) + \left[1 - C(q)\right]\hat{y}(t|\theta) = B(q)u(t) + \left[1 - A(q)\right]y(t) + \left[C(q) - 1\right]\left[y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)\right]$.

  This yields:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = B(q)u(t) + \left[1 - A(q)\right]y(t) + \left[C(q) - 1\right]\left[y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)\right] \tag{4.19}
$$

Now introduce the **one-step-ahead prediction error** (residual):

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)
$$

and define the parameter-dependent vector $\varphi(t, \theta)$:

$$
\varphi(t, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t-1) & \dots & -y(t-n_a) & u(t-1) & \dots & u(t-n_b) & \varepsilon(t-1, \theta) & \dots & \varepsilon(t-n_c, \theta) \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.20}
$$

Because $C(q) - 1 = c_1 q^{-1} + \dots + c_{n_c} q^{-n_c}$, Equation `(4.19)` collapses into the inner-product form:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t, \theta)\theta \tag{4.21}
$$

Although `(4.21)` looks superficially identical to `(4.12)`, it is **not** a linear regression because $\theta$ enters nonlinearly inside the past prediction errors $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$ in $\varphi(t, \theta)$. To highlight its structural kinship to `(4.12)`, `(4.21)` is called a **pseudolinear regression**.

### 4.2.5 Other Equation-Error-Type Model Structures (ARARX and ARARMAX)

Instead of modeling the equation error in `(4.7)` as a moving average $C(q)e(t)$, we can model it as an autoregression $\frac{1}{D(q)}e(t)$, giving the **ARARX** model structure:

$$
A(q)y(t) = B(q)u(t) + \frac{1}{D(q)}e(t) \tag{4.22}
$$

with monic denominator polynomial

$$
D(q) = 1 + d_1 q^{-1} + \dots + d_{n_d} q^{-n_d}
$$

More generally, using a full ARMA description $\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$ for the equation error yields the **ARARMAX** model structure:

$$
A(q)y(t) = B(q)u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t) \tag{4.23}
$$

which contains ARX `(4.7)`, ARMAX `(4.15)`, and ARARX `(4.22)` as special cases and defines the complete family of equation-error-related model sets.

![[assets/fig_4_2_equation_error_family.png]]

> [!tip] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 4.2 — The Equation Error Model Family)
> - **Signal Flow**: Input $u(t)$ is filtered by the numerator polynomial $B(q)$, while white noise $e(t)$ is shaped by the rational ARMA filter $\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}$. The two signals are summed at the central node (forming the equation error $A(q)y(t)$) and then passed together through the common all-pole plant filter $\frac{1}{A(q)}$ to produce $y(t)$.
> - **Key Takeaway**: Every member of the equation-error family (`(4.7)`, `(4.15)`, `(4.22)`, `(4.23)`) shares the common factor $A(q)$ in the denominators of both $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$.

### 4.2.6 Output Error (OE) Model Structure

From a physical perspective, it is often much more natural to parametrize the plant transfer function $G(q, \theta)$ and the noise transfer function $H(q, \theta)$ *independently* rather than forcing them to share $A(q)$. Suppose that the relationship between the input $u(t)$ and the **undisturbed output** $w(t)$ is governed by a linear difference equation, and that the only disturbance acting on the system is additive white measurement noise $e(t)$ at the output sensor:

$$
w(t) + f_1 w(t-1) + \dots + f_{n_f} w(t-n_f) = b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_{n_b} u(t-n_b) \tag{4.24a}
$$

$$
y(t) = w(t) + e(t) \tag{4.24b}
$$

Introducing the monic plant denominator polynomial $F(q)$:

$$
F(q) = 1 + f_1 q^{-1} + \dots + f_{n_f} q^{-n_f}
$$

we can write the model compactly as the **Output Error (OE) model (structure)**:

$$
y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + e(t) \tag{4.25}
$$

with parameter vector:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & \dots & b_{n_b} & f_1 & f_2 & \dots & f_{n_f} \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.26}
$$

![[assets/fig_4_2_oe_structure.png]]

> [!tip] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 4.3 — The Output Error Model Structure)
> - **Signal Flow**: Input $u(t)$ passes through the rational plant transfer function $\frac{B}{F}$ to produce the noise-free internal signal $w(t, \theta)$. White measurement noise $e(t)$ enters directly at the output summing junction without passing through any plant dynamics ($H(q, \theta) = 1$).
> - **Contrast with ARX (Figure 4.1)**: Unlike Figure 4.1, $e(t)$ bypasses the denominator dynamics completely. Consequently, estimating $\theta$ in OE models corresponds to fitting a pure simulation model $w(t, \theta)$ to $y(t)$.

- **Derivation of the OE Predictor & Pseudolinear Form**:
  Because the undisturbed output $w(t)$ in `(4.24a)` is never directly measured in the presence of noise, it must be computed recursively from the known input $u(t)$ for a given parameter vector $\theta$, and therefore rightly carries the index $\theta$:

$$
w(t, \theta) + f_1 w(t-1, \theta) + \dots + f_{n_f} w(t-n_f, \theta) = b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_{n_b} u(t-n_b) \tag{4.27}
$$

  Comparing `(4.25)` with `(4.4)`, we have $G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}$ and $H(q, \theta) = 1$. Substituting these into the general predictor `(4.6)` immediately gives:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) = w(t, \theta) \tag{4.28}
$$

  Notice a crucial property: **the OE predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is constructed from past inputs $u$ only**—measured outputs $y(t-k)$ are never fed back into the predictor! Defining the vector

$$
\varphi(t, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t-1) & \dots & u(t-n_b) & -w(t-1, \theta) & \dots & -w(t-n_f, \theta) \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.29}
$$

  allows `(4.27)`–`(4.28)` to be written in the pseudolinear regression form:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t, \theta)\theta \tag{4.30}
$$

  where the unobserved quantities $w(t-k, \theta)$ in `(4.29)` are simply the past model predictions $w(t-k, \theta) = \hat{y}(t-k|\theta)$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots, n_f$.

### 4.2.7 Box-Jenkins (BJ) Model Structure

A natural development of the Output Error model `(4.25)` is to allow the additive output error itself to be colored noise rather than white noise. Describing the output disturbance as an ARMA process $\frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$ yields the **Box-Jenkins (BJ) model structure** (Box & Jenkins, 1970):

$$
y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t) \tag{4.31}
$$

In a fundamental sense, `(4.31)` is the most natural finite-dimensional rational parametrization of `(4.4)` because the system transfer function $G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}$ and the noise transfer function $H(q, \theta) = \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}$ are parametrized completely independently. Substituting $G$ and $H$ from `(4.31)` into the general predictor `(4.6)` yields the BJ one-step-ahead predictor:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \frac{D(q)B(q)}{C(q)F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q) - D(q)}{C(q)}y(t) \tag{4.32}
$$

![[assets/fig_4_2_bj_structure.png]]

> [!tip] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 4.4 — The BJ-Model Structure)
> - **Signal Flow**: Input $u(t)$ passes through the plant transfer function $\frac{B}{F}$ and white noise $e(t)$ passes through the completely separate disturbance transfer function $\frac{C}{D}$. Their outputs sum at the right-hand node to form $y(t)$.
> - **Comparison with Figure 4.2**: In Figure 4.2 (ARARMAX), the summing junction sits *before* $\frac{1}{A}$, forcing both channels through $1/A$. In Figure 4.4 (BJ), the summing junction sits at the *very output*, completely decoupling the plant poles (roots of $F$) from the noise poles (roots of $D$).

### 4.2.8 A General Family of Model Structures

Depending on which of the five polynomials $A, B, C, D,$ and $F$ are used (or fixed to unity), the structures discussed so far actually give rise to $2^5 = 32$ different model sets. To develop unified estimation algorithms and analytical results that cover all cases simultaneously, Ljung introduces the **generalized model structure**:

$$
A(q)y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t) \tag{4.33}
$$

- **Explicit Input-Output Delay $n_k$**: Sometimes the dynamics from $u$ to $y$ contain a pure delay of $n_k$ samples, meaning the leading $n_k - 1$ coefficients of $B(q)$ are zero:

$$
B(q) = b_{n_k} q^{-n_k} + b_{n_k+1} q^{-n_k-1} + \dots + b_{n_k+n_b-1} q^{-n_k-n_b+1} = q^{-n_k}\bar{B}(q), \qquad b_{n_k} \neq 0
$$

  Displaying this delay explicitly gives:

$$
A(q)y(t) = q^{-n_k}\frac{\bar{B}(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t) \tag{4.34}
$$

  For notational simplicity, Ljung works primarily with $n_k = 1$ and `(4.33)`; formulas for general $n_k$ in `(4.34)` are recovered immediately by replacing $u(t)$ with $u(t - n_k + 1)$.

- **Predictor for the Generalized Structure**: In `(4.33)`, we have $G(q, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)F(q)}$ and $H(q, \theta) = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)D(q)}$. Substituting these into `(4.6)` gives the general one-step-ahead predictor:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \frac{D(q)B(q)}{C(q)F(q)}u(t) + \left[1 - \frac{D(q)A(q)}{C(q)}\right]y(t) \tag{4.35}
$$

- **Summary of Special Cases (Table 4.1)**: In practice, `(4.33)` with all five polynomials free is too general; one or several polynomials are fixed to $1$. Table 4.1 summarizes the most common SISO black-box model structures:

| Polynomials Used in `(4.33)` (Others $= 1$) | Name of Model Structure | Transfer Functions $G(q,\theta)$ and $H(q,\theta)$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $B$ | **FIR** (finite impulse response) | $G = B(q), \quad H = 1$ |
| $AB$ | **ARX** | $G = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}, \quad H = \frac{1}{A(q)}$ |
| $ABC$ | **ARMAX** | $G = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}, \quad H = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)}$ |
| $AC$ | **ARMA** (time-series, no input $u$) | $G = 0, \quad H = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)}$ |
| $ABD$ | **ARARX** | $G = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}, \quad H = \frac{1}{A(q)D(q)}$ |
| $ABCD$ | **ARARMAX** | $G = \frac{B(q)}{A(q)}, \quad H = \frac{C(q)}{A(q)D(q)}$ |
| $BF$ | **OE** (output error) | $G = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}, \quad H = 1$ |
| $BFCD$ | **BJ** (Box-Jenkins) | $G = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}, \quad H = \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}$ |

### 4.2.9 A Pseudolinear Form for `(4.35)` (*)

The generalized predictor `(4.35)` can be computed either as a direct filter recursion or as a unified pseudolinear regression:

1. **Direct Filter Recursion**: Multiplying `(4.35)` through by the common denominator $C(q)F(q)$ yields the input-output recursion:

$$
C(q)F(q)\hat{y}(t|\theta) = F(q)\left[C(q) - D(q)A(q)\right]y(t) + D(q)B(q)u(t) \tag{4.36}
$$

2. **Step-by-Step Derivation of the Unified Pseudolinear Regression**:
   - **Step 1 (Prediction Error)**: From `(4.35)`, the prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is:

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = \frac{D(q)}{C(q)}\left[A(q)y(t) - \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t)\right] \tag{4.37}
$$

   - **Step 2 (Introduce Auxiliary Variables $w(t, \theta)$ and $v(t, \theta)$)**: Decompose the bracketed term in `(4.37)` by defining the noise-free input-driven response $w(t, \theta)$ and the auxiliary equation error $v(t, \theta)$:

$$
w(t, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) \tag{4.38a}
$$

$$
v(t, \theta) = A(q)y(t) - w(t, \theta) \tag{4.38b}
$$

     Substituting `(4.38b)` into `(4.37)` isolates the ARMA noise filter relation:

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta) = \frac{D(q)}{C(q)}v(t, \theta) \tag{4.39}
$$

   - **Step 3 (Define the Extended Regression Vector and Parameter Vector)**: Introduce the "state vector" (generalized regression vector) $\varphi(t, \theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_a + n_b + n_f + n_c + n_d}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(t, \theta) = \big[ &-y(t-1), \dots, -y(t-n_a), \; u(t-1), \dots, u(t-n_b), \\
&-w(t-1, \theta), \dots, -w(t-n_f, \theta), \; \varepsilon(t-1, \theta), \dots, \varepsilon(t-n_c, \theta), \\
&-v(t-1, \theta), \dots, -v(t-n_d, \theta) \big]^T
\end{aligned} \tag{4.40}
$$

     and the corresponding full parameter vector:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \dots a_{n_a} & b_1 \dots b_{n_b} & f_1 \dots f_{n_f} & c_1 \dots c_{n_c} & d_1 \dots d_{n_d} \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.41}
$$

   - **Step 4 (Expand the Auxiliary Difference Equations)**:
     Expanding $F(q)w(t, \theta) = B(q)u(t)$ from `(4.38a)` gives the recursion for $w(t, \theta)$:

$$
w(t, \theta) = b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_{n_b} u(t-n_b) - f_1 w(t-1, \theta) - \dots - f_{n_f} w(t-n_f, \theta) \tag{4.42}
$$

     Expanding $C(q)\varepsilon(t, \theta) = D(q)v(t, \theta)$ from `(4.39)` gives the recursion for $\varepsilon(t, \theta)$:

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = v(t, \theta) + d_1 v(t-1, \theta) + \dots + d_{n_d} v(t-n_d, \theta) - c_1 \varepsilon(t-1, \theta) - \dots - c_{n_c} \varepsilon(t-n_c, \theta) \tag{4.43}
$$

     Expanding $A(q)y(t)$ in `(4.38b)` gives:

$$
v(t, \theta) = y(t) + a_1 y(t-1) + \dots + a_{n_a} y(t-n_a) - w(t, \theta)
$$

   - **Step 5 (Substitute and Collect into Inner Product)**: Inserting this expression for $v(t, \theta)$ into the first term on the right-hand side of `(4.43)`, and then substituting `(4.42)` for $w(t, \theta)$, every delayed term matches the exact entries of $-\theta^T \varphi(t, \theta)$:

$$
\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \theta^T \varphi(t, \theta) \tag{4.44}
$$

     Since $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$, we immediately obtain the unified pseudolinear predictor:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \theta^T \varphi(t, \theta) = \varphi^T(t, \theta)\theta \tag{4.45}
$$

### 4.2.10 Other Model Expansions (Basis Functions & Laguerre Models)

The Finite Impulse Response (FIR) model structure

$$
G(q, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} b_k q^{-k} \tag{4.46}
$$

possesses two major advantages:
1. It is a **linear regression** (as a special case of ARX with $A(q) = 1$), allowing fast, closed-form least-squares estimation.
2. It is simultaneously an **Output Error (OE) model** (with $F(q) = 1$ and $H(q) = 1$), making its plant estimate robust against colored output noise.

**The Disadvantage of FIR & the Basis Expansion Remedy**: If the true system has a pole close to the unit circle (slowly decaying impulse response), the order $n$ in `(4.46)` must be very large to approximate the tail of the impulse response, inflating parameter variance. To retain **both** the linear regression and output error properties while using only a small number of parameters $n$, we replace the raw delay basis functions $q^{-k}$ with a generalized basis expansion:

$$
G(q, \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \theta_k L_k(q, \alpha) \tag{4.47}
$$

where $L_k(q, \alpha)$ are known rational basis filters containing a **fixed user-chosen parameter $\alpha$** (an estimate of the system's dominating pole closest to the unit circle). Because $\alpha$ is fixed in the model structure, $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \theta_k \left[L_k(q, \alpha)u(t)\right]$ remains strictly linear in $\theta$!
- **SimpleShifted-Pole Basis**: A simple choice is

$$
L_k(q, \alpha) = \frac{q^{-k}}{q - \alpha}
$$

- **Orthonormal Laguerre Basis**: More sophisticated orthonormal basis expansions (e.g., Wahlberg, 1991; Van den Hof, Heuberger, & Bokor, 1995) provide superior numerical conditioning. In particular, the discrete-time **Laguerre polynomials/filters** are defined by:

$$
L_k(q, \alpha) = \frac{1}{q - \alpha}\left(\frac{1 - \alpha q}{q - \alpha}\right)^{k-1} \tag{4.48}
$$

  where each successive filter $L_k(q, \alpha)$ passes the signal through an additional first-order all-pass stage $\frac{1 - \alpha q}{q - \alpha}$ sharing the fixed dominant pole $q = \alpha$.

---

### 4.2.11 Continuous-Time Black-Box Models (*)

Instead of parametrizing discrete-time shift-operator rational functions, a linear system can be parametrized directly in terms of its continuous-time transfer function `(2.22)`:

$$
y(t) = G_c(p, \theta)u(t) \tag{4.49}
$$

where $p$ denotes the differentiation operator ($pu(t) = \frac{d}{dt}u(t)$). Fitting `(4.49)` to discrete-time sampled measurements can be achieved by:
1. Numerically solving the underlying differential equations,
2. Applying an exact or approximate zero-order-hold / discrete sampling transformation `(2.24)`, or
3. Fitting $G_c(i\omega, \theta)$ in the frequency domain to Fourier-transformed band-limited input-output data (treated in Section 7.7).

Two specific continuous-time black-box structures are widely used in practice:
1. **First-Order System with Time Delay (Process Industry Standard)**:

$$
G_c(s, \theta) = \frac{K e^{-s\tau_c}}{s\tau + 1}, \qquad \theta = \begin{bmatrix} K & \tau_c & \tau \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.50}
$$

   where $K$ is the static gain, $\tau_c$ is the continuous transport delay (dead time), and $\tau$ is the dominant time constant.
2. **Continuous-Time Orthonormal Function Series Expansions (Laguerre Basis)**:

$$
G_c(s, \theta) = \sum_{k=0}^{d-1} a_k f_k(s), \qquad \theta = \begin{bmatrix} a_0 & \dots & a_{d-1} \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.51}
$$

   where the continuous-time **Laguerre basis functions** are given by:

$$
f_k(s) = \sqrt{2\alpha}\frac{(s - \alpha)^k}{(s + \alpha)^{k+1}}
$$

   with $\alpha > 0$ serving as a user-chosen time-scaling factor (dominant pole location $s = -\alpha$). As in `(2.23)`, `(4.49)` can be complemented with a discrete-time noise model at the sampling instants.

### 4.2.12 Multivariable Case: Matrix Fraction Descriptions (*)

Now consider a multi-input, multi-output (MIMO) system where the input $u(t)$ is an $m$-dimensional column vector ($u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^m$) and the output $y(t)$ is a $p$-dimensional column vector ($y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$).

1. **Multivariable Equation Error (ARX) Model**:
   The direct MIMO generalization of the difference equation `(4.7)` is:

$$
y(t) + A_1 y(t-1) + \dots + A_{n_a} y(t-n_a) = B_1 u(t-1) + \dots + B_{n_b} u(t-n_b) + e(t) \tag{4.52}
$$

   where each $A_i$ is a $p \times p$ matrix, each $B_i$ is a $p \times m$ matrix, and $e(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$. Defining the **matrix polynomials** in $q^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(q) &= I + A_1 q^{-1} + \dots + A_{n_a} q^{-n_a} \\
B(q) &= B_1 q^{-1} + \dots + B_{n_b} q^{-n_b}
\end{aligned} \tag{4.53}
$$

   the MIMO system is written in the standard form:

$$
y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t) \tag{4.54}
$$

   with $p \times m$ plant transfer matrix $G(q, \theta)$ and $p \times p$ noise transfer matrix $H(q, \theta)$ factored as a **(left) Matrix Fraction Description (MFD)** (Kailath, 1980):

$$
G(q, \theta) = A^{-1}(q)B(q), \qquad H(q, \theta) = A^{-1}(q) \tag{4.55}
$$

2. **MIMO Linear Regression Forms**:
   - **Full Unconstrained Matrix Parametrization**: Suppose all matrix entries in `(4.52)`—a total of $n_a \cdot p^2 + n_b \cdot p \cdot m$ scalar parameters—are freely included. Define the $[n_a p + n_b m] \times p$ parameter matrix $\Theta$:

$$
\Theta = \begin{bmatrix} A_1 & A_2 & \dots & A_{n_a} & B_1 & \dots & B_{n_b} \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{4.56}
$$

     and the $[n_a p + n_b m]$-dimensional column regression vector $\varphi(t)$:

$$
\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t-1) \\ \vdots \\ -y(t-n_a) \\ u(t-1) \\ \vdots \\ u(t-n_b) \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.57}
$$

     Then `(4.52)` can be rewritten as:

$$
y(t) = \Theta^T \varphi(t) + e(t) \tag{4.58}
$$

     which decouples into $p$ separate scalar linear regressions stacked on top of each other, all sharing the **exact same regression vector** $\varphi(t)$.
   - **Structured Parametrization**: When structural constraints are imposed on the matrices $A_i, B_i$ (so that some entries are zero or fixed, and different output channels $y_i(t)$ do not employ identical regressors), we collect the $d$ free scalar parameters into a column vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and construct a $p \times d$ regression matrix $\varphi^T(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times d}$ to write:

$$
y(t) = \varphi^T(t)\theta + e(t) \tag{4.59}
$$

3. **Multivariable ARMAX (VDE) and Box-Jenkins MFD Extensions**:
   - **Vector Difference Equation (VDE) / MIMO ARMAX**: Adding a moving average matrix polynomial $C(q) = I + C_1 q^{-1} + \dots + C_{n_c} q^{-n_c}$ (with $C_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$) yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) + A_1 y(t-1) + \dots + A_{n_a} y(t-n_a) &= B_1 u(t-1) + \dots + B_{n_b} u(t-n_b) \\
&\quad + e(t) + C_1 e(t-1) + \dots + C_{n_c} e(t-n_c)
\end{aligned} \tag{4.60a}
$$

     or in left-MFD form:

$$
G(q, \theta) = A^{-1}(q)B(q), \qquad H(q, \theta) = A^{-1}(q)C(q) \tag{4.60b}
$$

   - **Multivariable Box-Jenkins MFD**: Independently parametrizing the plant and disturbance matrix fractions gives:

$$
G(q, \theta) = F^{-1}(q)B(q), \qquad H(q, \theta) = D^{-1}(q)C(q) \tag{4.61}
$$

     The subtle canonical parametrization issues of MFD descriptions (ensuring unique $\theta$ without redundant matrix degrees of freedom) are treated in Appendix 4A and Section 4.6.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: Linear Regression vs. Pseudolinear Regression (`(4.12)` vs. `(4.21)`, `(4.30)`, `(4.45)`)**:
>   At first glance, the ARX predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$, the ARMAX predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t, \theta)\theta$, the OE predictor, and the general family predictor all look like simple inner products. Do not be fooled by the compact notation! Whenever $C(q) \neq 1$, $D(q) \neq 1$, or $F(q) \neq 1$, the regressor vector $\varphi(t, \theta)$ contains unobserved internal signals—past prediction errors $\varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$, simulated noise-free outputs $w(t-k, \theta)$, or equation residuals $v(t-k, \theta)$—that must be generated recursively using $\theta$ itself. Thus $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ is a rational (nonlinear) function of $\theta$.
> - **Leap 2: OE Predictors Never Use Measured Outputs $y(t-k)$**:
>   In the Output Error (OE) model `(4.25)`, $H(q, \theta) = 1$, so the term $[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)]y(t)$ in the general predictor `(4.6)` vanishes identically! As a result, $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = w(t, \theta) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t)$ is a pure open-loop simulation driven exclusively by the input sequence $u$. Past outputs $y(t-k)$ are used *only* when computing the prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - w(t, \theta)$ for parameter estimation, never inside the predictor recursion `(4.27)`.
> - **Notation Trap 1: Polynomials in $q^{-1}$ Written as Functions of $q$**:
>   All five polynomials $A(q), B(q), C(q), D(q), F(q)$ are polynomials in the **backward shift operator $q^{-1}$**, not $q$. Always remember that $A(q), C(q), D(q), F(q)$ are **monic** (their leading constant term is $1$), whereas $B(q) = b_1 q^{-1} + \dots + b_{n_b} q^{-n_b}$ has zero constant term (assuming at least a 1-sample delay $n_k \ge 1$).
> - **Notation Trap 2: Matrix Ordering in Multivariable MFDs (`(4.55)`, `(4.58)` vs. `(4.59)`)**:
>   Matrix multiplication does not commute! In the left Matrix Fraction Description `(4.55)`, $G(q, \theta) = A^{-1}(q)B(q)$ has $A^{-1}(q)$ on the *left* because $A(q)$ premultiplies $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$. Also note the transpose convention in `(4.58)` ($y(t) = \Theta^T \varphi(t) + e(t)$, where $\Theta$ is $[n_a p + n_b m] \times p$ and $\varphi(t)$ is a column vector) versus `(4.59)` ($y(t) = \varphi^T(t)\theta + e(t)$, where $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a column vector and $\varphi^T(t)$ is a $p \times d$ matrix).
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *Why is the FIR model `(4.46)` simultaneously a member of the ARX family and the OE family, and how do Laguerre basis expansions `(4.47)`–`(4.48)` preserve both advantages for systems with slow poles?*
>   2. *In the general 5-polynomial predictor `(4.35)`, why must $C(q)$ and $F(q)$ have all roots strictly inside the unit circle (in the variable $z$), whereas $A(q)$ and $D(q)$ appear in the numerators of the predictor filters?*
