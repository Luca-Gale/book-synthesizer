# 4.5 Model Sets, Model Structures, and Identifiability: Some Formal Aspects

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 4**: Section 4.5 elevates the concrete linear model families of Sections 4.1–4.4 (ARX, ARMAX, OE, BJ, and state-space) into a rigorous, unified mathematical framework—distinguishing between individual *predictor models* $W(q)$, unparametrized *model sets* $\mathcal{M}^*$, and smooth *model structures* $\mathcal{M}: D_{\mathcal{M}} \to \mathcal{M}^*$—that provides the exact analytical foundations (predictor gradients, stability regularity conditions, structural identifiability, and the true parameter set $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$) required for parameter estimation (Chapter 7) and asymptotic convergence analysis (Chapters 8–9).

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Throughout Sections 4.1–4.4, we constructed numerous linear time-invariant (LTI) model representations—polynomial input-output families ($A, B, C, D, F$), continuous-time physical state-space models, and discrete-time innovations state-space models. Before we can design numerical optimization algorithms to fit these models to data or prove mathematical theorems about what the parameter estimates converge to as $N \to \infty$, we must answer four foundational questions:

1. **What is a "model" at its most fundamental level?**
   In system identification, a model is judged by how it predicts future outputs from past observed data. Even if a system is described by transfer functions $G(q)$ and $H(q)$, its operational behavior in prediction-error identification is completely captured by its one-step-ahead predictor filter $W(q) = [W_u(q) \quad W_y(q)]$. Treating the stable predictor filter $W(q)$ as the primitive definition of a model (Definition 4.1) has two huge advantages:
   - It accommodates **open-loop unstable systems** whose predictor filters are nevertheless completely stable (as demonstrated in Example 4.4).
   - It generalizes effortlessly to nonlinear and time-varying systems (Chapter 5).

2. **Why distinguish between a *Model Set* ($\mathcal{M}^*$) and a *Model Structure* ($\mathcal{M}$)?**
   A **model set** $\mathcal{M}^*$ is simply a collection of candidate predictor models $W(q)$—an abstract geometric surface or set of input-output behaviors, independent of how we label them. A **model structure** $\mathcal{M}$ is a specific *coordinate system* (parametrization) that maps a parameter vector $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ to a model $\mathcal{M}(\theta) = W(q, \theta) \in \mathcal{M}^*$.
   - Just as a sphere in geometry is a single surface ($\mathcal{M}^*$) that can be parametrized by different coordinate charts ($\mathcal{M}_1, \mathcal{M}_2$), the same set of linear systems of order $n$ can be parametrized via companion-form state-space matrices, canonical polynomial coefficients, or physical constants.
   - Furthermore, for complex multi-output systems or variable-order problems, a single global coordinate chart without singularities may not exist, requiring us to cover the model set $\mathcal{M}^*$ by a **union of ranges of overlapping model structures** $\mathcal{M}^* = \bigcup_{i=1}^\beta \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}_i)$ (analogous to an atlas of coordinate charts on a differentiable manifold).

3. **Why do we require differentiability and gradient stability ($\Psi(q, \theta)$) on open/compact domains $D_{\mathcal{M}}$?**
   When searching for the best parameter $\hat{\theta}_N$ by minimizing a prediction-error criterion $V_N(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \ell(\varepsilon(t, \theta))$, gradient-based numerical routines (Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt) must compute the **predictor gradient**:

   $$\psi(t, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} \hat{y}(t|\theta) = -\frac{d}{d\theta} \varepsilon(t, \theta) = \Psi(q, \theta) z(t)$$

   For $\psi(t, \theta)$ to exist and remain bounded when driven by stationary data $z(t) = [u(t) \quad y(t)]^T$, the derivative filter $\Psi(z, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} W(z, \theta)$ must be **analytic on and outside the unit circle** ($|z| \ge 1$). Defining $\mathcal{M}$ on an open subset $D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ guarantees that derivatives are well-defined interior to the domain, while restricting actual optimization searches to a **compact subset** of $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ ensures *uniform stability* of both $W(q, \theta)$ and $\Psi(q, \theta)$—preventing poles of $C(q)$ or $F(q)$ from drifting onto the unit circle $|z| = 1$ where the predictor and its gradient blow up.

4. **What is Structural Identifiability, and why must we weaken "Strict Global Identifiability"?**
   Even if our experimental input $u(t)$ is infinitely rich (persistently exciting of all orders), can we uniquely recover $\theta^*$ from the external predictor behavior $W(q, \theta^*)$? This is a purely algebraic property of the mapping $\mathcal{M}$: **injectivity** ($\mathcal{M}(\theta) = \mathcal{M}(\theta^*) \Rightarrow \theta = \theta^*$).
   - Crucially, in almost all black-box transfer function structures (like ARMAX or Box-Jenkins), if the true system has a *lower dynamical order* than the model structure, common pole-zero factors cancel in $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$. Along these lower-order **hypersurfaces** $\delta D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset D_{\mathcal{M}}$, infinitely many $\theta$ vectors yield the exact same predictor $W(q, \theta)$.
   - Consequently, requiring injectivity at *every* point $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ (**strict global identifiability**, Definition 4.7) is too demanding for linear systems. Instead, Ljung defines **global identifiability** (Definition 4.8) as injectivity at *almost all* $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ (everywhere except on a lower-dimensional hypersurface $\delta D_{\mathcal{M}}$ of Lebesgue measure zero). When the true system $\mathcal{S}$ lies in the model structure at a globally identifiable point $\theta_0$, the **true parameter set** $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$ collapses to the singleton $\{\theta_0\}$.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 4.5.1 Compact Notation & The One-to-One Correspondence $T(q) \leftrightarrow W(q)$

To keep notation transparent, Section 4.5 explicitly treats single-input, single-output (SISO) models (the multi-output extension is direct; see Appendix 4A).

Recall the standard LTI system description from (4.1):

$$
y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$$

By stacking the transfer functions into a $1 \times 2$ row vector $T(q)$ and the driving signals into a $2 \times 1$ column vector $\chi(t)$:
$$

T(q) = \begin{bmatrix} G(q) & H(q) \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \chi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ e(t) \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.109}$$

we can rewrite the system equation (4.1) compactly as:

$$
y(t) = T(q)\chi(t) \tag{4.110}$$

Similarly, the parametrized model structure (4.4) is written as:
$$

y(t) = T(q, \theta)\chi(t), \qquad T(q, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} G(q, \theta) & H(q, \theta) \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.111}$$

**Derivation of the Compact Predictor Form (4.112)–(4.115)**:
From Chapter 3, Equation (3.46), the optimal one-step-ahead predictor for $y(t)$ given all past inputs $u^t$ and past outputs $y^{t-1}$ is:

$$
\hat{y}(t|t-1) = H^{-1}(q)G(q)u(t) + \left[1 - H^{-1}(q)\right]y(t)$$

Collecting the two filters acting on $u(t)$ and $y(t)$ into a $1 \times 2$ predictor filter vector $W(q)$ and the measured data into $z(t)$:
$$

\hat{y}(t|t-1) = W(q)z(t) \tag{4.112}$$

where:

$$
W(q) = \begin{bmatrix} W_u(q) & W_y(q) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad z(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ y(t) \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.113}$$

and the individual predictor filter components are:
$$

W_u(q) = H^{-1}(q)G(q), \qquad W_y(q) = \left[1 - H^{-1}(q)\right] \tag{4.114}$$

> [!note] Algebraic Inversion of (4.114): The Bijection $T(q) \leftrightarrow W(q)$
> Equation (4.114) defines an exact **one-to-one correspondence** between the system/noise description $T(q) = [G(q) \quad H(q)]$ and the predictor filter $W(q) = [W_u(q) \quad W_y(q)]$:
>
> $$
> T(q) \leftrightarrow W(q) \tag{4.115}$$
>
> **Proof of Inversion**: Given $W(q) = [W_u(q) \quad W_y(q)]$ with $W_y(\infty) = 0$ (strictly proper in $q^{-1}$ so that $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ only uses past outputs $y(t-1), y(t-2), \dots$), we solve (4.114) for $H(q)$ and $G(q)$:
> $$
>1 - H^{-1}(q) = W_y(q) \implies H^{-1}(q) = 1 - W_y(q) \implies H(q) = \left[1 - W_y(q)\right]^{-1}$$
> 
> Substituting $H(q)$ into $W_u(q) = H^{-1}(q)G(q)$ immediately yields:
>
> $$
> G(q) = H(q)W_u(q) = \left[1 - W_y(q)\right]^{-1} W_u(q)$$
>
> **Remark on $k$-Step-Ahead Predictors**: If we prefer to work with the $k$-step-ahead predictor (3.31), we can preserve the exact link (4.115) by viewing (3.31) as the one-step-ahead predictor for the reshaped innovation model (3.32).
>
> ---
>
> #### 4.5.2 Models: Predictor Models, Probabilistic Models & Unstable Systems
>
> While $T(q)$ and $W(q)$ are algebraically equivalent via (4.115), elevating the predictor (4.112) to be the **primary definition of a model** relaxes the need to separately specify stable $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ and extends directly to nonlinear/time-varying models (Section 5.7).
>
> [!important] Definition 4.1: Predictor Model
> A **predictor model** of a linear, time-invariant system is a **stable filter** $W(q)$, defining a predictor (4.112) as in (4.113):
> $$
>\hat{y}(t|t-1) = W(q)z(t) = W_u(q)u(t) + W_y(q)y(t)$$
> 
> **Stability Requirement**: Both filter components $W_u(q) = \sum_{\ell=0}^\infty w_u(\ell)q^{-\ell}$ and $W_y(q) = \sum_{\ell=1}^\infty w_y(\ell)q^{-\ell}$ must be stable in the sense of (2.27) ($\sum_{\ell} |w(\ell)| < \infty$, i.e., analytic on and outside the unit circle $|z| \ge 1$) so that the right-hand side of (4.112) is well-defined for bounded signals $z(t)$.

When we wish to complement the deterministic predictor with statistical assumptions on the innovations $e(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1)$, we use:

> [!important] Definition 4.2: Complete Probabilistic Model
> A **complete probabilistic model** of a linear, time-invariant system is a pair $(W(q), f_e(x))$ consisting of a predictor model $W(q)$ and the probability density function (PDF) $f_e(x)$ of the associated prediction errors (innovations).
> *(Note: One can also consider partially specified probabilistic models where only the variance $\lambda$ of $e(t)$ is specified. Throughout the remainder of Section 4.5, "model" refers to a predictor model in the sense of Definition 4.1).*

##### Model Equality and Special Predictor Classes
Two predictor models $W_1(q)$ and $W_2(q)$ are defined to be **equal** ($W_1 = W_2$) if their frequency responses coincide at almost all frequencies:

$$
W_1(e^{i\omega}) = W_2(e^{i\omega}), \qquad \text{almost all } \omega \in [-\pi, \pi] \tag{4.116}$$

*(Since $W_1(z)$ and $W_2(z)$ are analytic in $|z| \ge 1$, equality for almost all $\omega$ implies $W_1(z) = W_2(z)$ for all $|z| \ge 1$ and identical impulse response sequences).*

Furthermore, a predictor model $W(q) = [W_u(q) \quad W_y(q)]$ is classified by the structure of $W_y(q)$:
- **$k$-step-ahead predictor model**: if the output filter only uses outputs up to time $t - k$:

  $$W_y(q) = \sum_{\ell=k}^\infty w_y(\ell) q^{-\ell}, \qquad \text{with } w_y(k) \neq 0 \tag{4.117}$$

- **Output error (OE) model** (or **simulation model**): if the predictor uses *no past outputs at all*:

  $$W_y(q) \equiv 0$$

  *(In this case, $H(q) \equiv 1$ and $\hat{y}(t|t-1) = W_u(q)u(t) = G(q)u(t)$ is a pure open-loop simulation driven solely by $u$).*

##### Example 4.4: Unstable System with a Stable Predictor Model
Note that Definition 4.1 requires the **predictor** $W(q)$ to be stable, which does **not** require the underlying open-loop system $G(q)$ to be stable! Suppose that:
$$

G(q) = \frac{b q^{-1}}{1 + a q^{-1}}, \quad \text{with } |a| > 1, \qquad \text{and} \qquad H(q) = \frac{1}{1 + a q^{-1}}$$

1. **Time-Domain Difference Equation**: Since $A(q) = 1 + a q^{-1}$, the system is an unstable ARX(1,1) process:

   $$y(t) + a y(t-1) = b u(t-1) + e(t)$$

   Because the pole is at $z = -a$ with $|a| > 1$ (outside the unit circle), the open-loop dynamics from $u$ to $y$ ($G(q)$) and from $e$ to $y$ ($H(q)$) are **exponentially unstable**.
2. **Predictor Filter Calculation**: Using (4.114) with $H^{-1}(q) = 1 + a q^{-1}$:

   $$W_y(q) = 1 - H^{-1}(q) = 1 - (1 + a q^{-1}) = -a q^{-1}$$

   $$W_u(q) = H^{-1}(q)G(q) = (1 + a q^{-1}) \frac{b q^{-1}}{1 + a q^{-1}} = b q^{-1}$$

3. **Conclusion**: Both $W_y(q) = -a q^{-1}$ and $W_u(q) = b q^{-1}$ are finite impulse response (FIR) polynomials whose only poles are at $z = 0$ (strictly inside the unit circle). The resulting predictor:

   $$\hat{y}(t|t-1) = -a y(t-1) + b u(t-1)$$

   is completely **stable** for any finite $a$ and $b$, and therefore satisfies Definition 4.1 even when $|a| > 1$! (Physically, observing the actual unstable output $y(t-1)$ at each step stabilizes the one-step-ahead prediction error $y(t) - \hat{y}(t|t-1) = e(t)$). $\blacksquare$

#### 4.5.3 Model Sets ($\mathcal{M}^*$)

Whereas Definition 4.1 defines a single candidate model, system identification searches for a suitable model over a collection of candidates.

> [!important] Definition: Model Set $\mathcal{M}^*$
> A **model set** $\mathcal{M}^*$ is a collection of predictor models (each satisfying Definition 4.1), indexed ("enumerated") by an abstract index $\alpha$ ranging over an index set $\mathcal{A}$:
>
> $$
> \mathcal{M}^* = \left\{ W_\alpha(q) \;\middle|\; \alpha \in \mathcal{A} \right\} \tag{4.118}$$
>
> **Canonical Examples of Model Sets**:
> 1. **The Universal Linear Model Set**:
>
>    $$\mathcal{M}^* = \mathcal{L}^* = \left\{\text{all linear models satisfying Definition 4.1}\right\}$$
>
> 2. **Bounded-Degree FIR Predictor Set ($\mathcal{M}_n^*$)**:
>
>    $$\mathcal{M}_n^* = \left\{\text{all models such that } W_y(q) \text{ and } W_u(q) \text{ are polynomials in } q^{-1} \text{ of degree at most } n\right\} \tag{4.119}$$
>
>    *(Note that $\mathcal{M}_n^*$ is the set of all ARX($n, n$) predictor models).*
> 3. **Finite Model Set**:
>
>    $$\mathcal{M}^* = \left\{ W_1(q), \; W_2(q), \; W_3(q) \right\} \tag{4.120}$$
>
> **Equality of Model Sets**: Two model sets $\mathcal{M}_1^*$ and $\mathcal{M}_2^*$ are said to be **equal** ($\mathcal{M}_1^* = \mathcal{M}_2^*$) if for every model $W_1 \in \mathcal{M}_1^*$ there exists a model $W_2 \in \mathcal{M}_2^*$ such that $W_1 = W_2$ in the sense of (4.116), and vice versa.
>
> ---
>
> #### 4.5.4 Model Structures: Smooth Parametrization of Model Sets & Predictor Gradients
>
> Most model sets of practical interest are uncountably infinite. To conduct a numerical search over $\mathcal{M}^*$ for "the best model," we must index the models *smoothly* by a finite-dimensional real parameter vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$, writing $W(q, \theta)$.
>
> ##### Differentiability and the Predictor Gradient Filter $\Psi(q, \theta)$
> To formalize "smooth indexing," we require that for every complex frequency $z$ on or outside the unit circle ($|z| \ge 1$), the complex-valued transfer vector $W(z, \theta) = [W_u(z, \theta) \quad W_y(z, \theta)]$ is a differentiable function of $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$. We denote its derivative with respect to the $d \times 1$ column vector $\theta$ by the $d \times 2$ transfer matrix $\Psi(z, \theta)$:
> $$
>\Psi(z, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} W(z, \theta) \tag{4.121a}$$

Writing out the two columns of $\Psi(z, \theta)$ explicitly:

$$
\Psi(z, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \Psi_u(z, \theta) & \Psi_y(z, \theta) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{d}{d\theta} W_u(z, \theta) & \dfrac{d}{d\theta} W_y(z, \theta) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{d \times 2} \tag{4.121b}$$

where the $i$-th row of $\Psi(z, \theta)$ is $\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_i} W_u(z, \theta) \quad \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_i} W_y(z, \theta)\right]$ for $i = 1, \dots, d$. Consequently, the **gradient of the one-step-ahead prediction** $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = W(q, \theta)z(t)$ with respect to $\theta$ is a $d \times 1$ vector signal obtained by filtering the data vector $z(t) = [u(t) \quad y(t)]^T$ through $\Psi(q, \theta)$:
$$

\psi(t, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} \hat{y}(t|\theta) = \Psi(q, \theta) z(t) = \Psi_u(q, \theta) u(t) + \Psi_y(q, \theta) y(t) \tag{4.121c}$$

Because gradient-based search algorithms must compute $\psi(t, \theta)$ from measured data $z(t)$ at every iteration, **the gradient filters $\Psi(q, \theta)$ must themselves be stable**.

> [!important] Definition 4.3: Model Structure ($\mathcal{M}$)
> A **model structure** $\mathcal{M}$ is a differentiable mapping from a connected, open subset $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ of $\mathbb{R}^d$ to a model set $\mathcal{M}^*$, such that the gradients of the predictor functions are stable. In mathematical notation:
>
> $$
> \mathcal{M} : D_{\mathcal{M}} \ni \theta \longrightarrow \mathcal{M}(\theta) = W(q, \theta) \in \mathcal{M}^* \tag{4.122}$$
>
> such that for every $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$, both $W(q, \theta)$ and the derivative filter $\Psi(q, \theta)$ in (4.121) exist and are stable (analytic for $|z| \ge 1$). We use $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$ to denote the specific model at parameter $\theta$, and reserve $\mathcal{M}$ for the mapping itself.
>
> [!tip] Open vs. Compact Parameter Domains $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ and Uniform Stability Regularity Conditions
> - **Why $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ is defined as open in Definition 4.3**: Requiring $D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ to be an open set ensures that every $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ has an open neighborhood around it, so the partial derivatives in (4.121) are unambiguously well-defined without boundary-side derivative complications.
> - **Why Compact Subsets are used in Estimation & Convergence Analysis**: In numerical optimization (Chapter 7) and asymptotic convergence theorems (Chapters 8–9), we restrict $\theta$ to a **compact (closed and bounded) subset** $D_{\mathcal{M}, c} \subset D_{\mathcal{M}}$ contained strictly inside the open domain where (4.121) is defined.
> - **Uniform Stability on Compact Domains**: On a compact domain $D_{\mathcal{M}, c}$, continuity of the poles with respect to $\theta$ guarantees that the poles of $W(z, \theta)$ and $\Psi(z, \theta)$ are bounded away from the unit circle uniformly in $\theta$—meaning there exists a single decay bound $C < \infty$ and $\lambda < 1$ such that the impulse responses $w(\ell, \theta)$ and $\psi(\ell, \theta)$ satisfy:
> $$
>\sup_{\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}, c}} \|w(\ell, \theta)\| \le C \lambda^\ell, \qquad \sup_{\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}, c}} \|\psi(\ell, \theta)\| \le C \lambda^\ell \qquad (\ell \ge 0)$$
> 
>   For instance, in an ARMAX structure where stability depends on $C(z) = 1 + c_1 z^{-1} + \dots + c_{n_c} z^{-n_c}$ having all zeros strictly inside the unit circle, the open stability region is $\{c \mid C(z) \neq 0 \text{ for } |z| \ge 1\}$, and a compact subset is obtained by requiring the zeros of $C(z)$ to lie inside a disk of radius $1 - \delta$ ($|z| \le 1 - \delta < 1$) and bounding $\|\theta\| \le R$.
> - **Differentiable Manifolds**: As noted in the Remark on p. 108, differentiability of $\mathcal{M}$ can also be defined over more general subsets of $\mathbb{R}^d$ than open sets—namely, *differentiable manifolds* (Boothby, 1975).

##### Example 4.5: An ARX Structure
Consider the ARX(1,2) model:

$$
y(t) + a y(t-1) = b_1 u(t-1) + b_2 u(t-2) + e(t)$$

1. **Predictor Model $W(q, \theta)$**: From (4.10), the one-step-ahead predictor is $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = -a y(t-1) + b_1 u(t-1) + b_2 u(t-2)$, which corresponds to:

   $$W(q, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} W_u(q, \theta) & W_y(q, \theta) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 q^{-1} + b_2 q^{-2} & -a q^{-1} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \theta = \begin{bmatrix} a & b_1 & b_2 \end{bmatrix}^T \in \mathbb{R}^3$$

2. **Predictor Gradient Filter $\Psi(q, \theta)$**: Differentiating $W(q, \theta)$ with respect to $\theta = [a \quad b_1 \quad b_2]^T$ via (4.121b) yields the $3 \times 2$ polynomial filter matrix:

   $$\Psi(q, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial W_u}{\partial a} & \dfrac{\partial W_y}{\partial a} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial W_u}{\partial b_1} & \dfrac{\partial W_y}{\partial b_1} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial W_u}{\partial b_2} & \dfrac{\partial W_y}{\partial b_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -q^{-1} \\ q^{-1} & 0 \\ q^{-2} & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

   Multiplying by $z(t) = [u(t) \quad y(t)]^T$ gives the familiar linear regression gradient $\psi(t, \theta) = \Psi(q, \theta)z(t) = [-y(t-1) \quad u(t-1) \quad u(t-2)]^T = \varphi(t)$, which is independent of $\theta$ and stable for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^3$. $\blacksquare$

##### Step-by-Step Derivation of the General Gradient Formula (4.123)–(4.125)
In Sections 4.2 and 4.3, our model structures were specified in terms of $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ rather than directly in terms of $W(q, \theta)$:
$$

y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t), \qquad \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \tag{4.123}$$

or, using (4.111), $y(t) = T(q, \theta)\chi(t)$. How do we compute the predictor gradient filter $\Psi(q, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} W(q, \theta)$ directly from the derivatives of $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$?

**Step-by-Step Algebraic Proof of (4.124)**:
1. Recall from (4.114) that:

   $$W_u(q, \theta) = \frac{G(q, \theta)}{H(q, \theta)}, \qquad W_y(q, \theta) = 1 - \frac{1}{H(q, \theta)}$$

2. Differentiate $W_u(q, \theta)$ with respect to the vector $\theta$ using the quotient rule:

   $$\Psi_u(q, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} \left( \frac{G(q, \theta)}{H(q, \theta)} \right) = \frac{H(q, \theta) \dfrac{d}{d\theta} G(q, \theta) - G(q, \theta) \dfrac{d}{d\theta} H(q, \theta)}{\left(H(q, \theta)\right)^2}$$

3. Differentiate $W_y(q, \theta)$ with respect to $\theta$:

   $$\Psi_y(q, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} \left( 1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta) \right) = \frac{1}{\left(H(q, \theta)\right)^2} \frac{d}{d\theta} H(q, \theta)$$

4. Assemble $\Psi(q, \theta) = [\Psi_u(q, \theta) \quad \Psi_y(q, \theta)]$ into matrix form by factoring out $\frac{1}{(H(q, \theta))^2}$ and the $d \times 2$ derivative matrix $T'(q, \theta) = \left[\frac{d}{d\theta} G(q, \theta) \quad \frac{d}{d\theta} H(q, \theta)\right]$:

   $$\Psi(q, \theta) = \frac{1}{\left(H(q, \theta)\right)^2} \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{d}{d\theta} G(q, \theta) & \dfrac{d}{d\theta} H(q, \theta) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} H(q, \theta) & 0 \\ -G(q, \theta) & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

This establishes Ljung's compact transformation formula:

$$
\Psi(q, \theta) = \frac{1}{\left(H(q, \theta)\right)^2} T'(q, \theta) \begin{bmatrix} H(q, \theta) & 0 \\ -G(q, \theta) & 1 \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.124}$$

where $T'(q, \theta)$ is the $d \times 2$ matrix:
$$

T'(q, \theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta} T(q, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{d}{d\theta} G(q, \theta) & \dfrac{d}{d\theta} H(q, \theta) \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.125}$$

Therefore, differentiability and stability of $W(q, \theta)$ are assured whenever $T(q, \theta)$ is differentiable and stable and $H^{-1}(q, \theta)$ is stable!

##### Verification for Polynomial and State-Space Families (Lemmas 4.1 & 4.2)

> [!important] Lemma 4.1: General Polynomial Model Structure
> The general polynomial parametrization (4.35) together with (4.41),
>
> $$
> A(q)y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$$
>
> with $\theta$ confined to the open domain:
> $$
>D_{\mathcal{M}} = \left\{ \theta \;\middle|\; F(z)\cdot C(z) \text{ has no zeros on or outside the unit circle } (|z| \ge 1) \right\}$$
> 
> is a **model structure** in the sense of Definition 4.3.

**Proof of Lemma 4.1 & ARMAX/General Predictor Gradients**:
From (4.35), $G(z, \theta) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)F(z)}$ and $H(z, \theta) = \frac{C(z)}{A(z)D(z)}$. Substituting into (4.114), the common $A(z)$ cancels in $W_u$ and moves to the numerator in $W_y$:

$$
W_u(z, \theta) = \frac{B(z)D(z)}{C(z)F(z)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad W_y(z, \theta) = 1 - \frac{D(z)A(z)}{C(z)}$$

Because $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ guarantees that $C(z)F(z) \neq 0$ for all $|z| \ge 1$, both $W_u(z, \theta)$ and $W_y(z, \theta)$ are analytic in $|z| \ge 1$. Moreover, differentiating with respect to each polynomial coefficient produces rational filters whose denominators only contain powers of $C(z)$ and $F(z)$—for example, differentiating $W_u(z, \theta)$ with respect to the coefficient $c_k$ of $C(z) = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{n_c} c_k z^{-k}$ yields:
$$

\frac{\partial}{\partial c_k} W_u(z, \theta) = -\frac{B(z)D(z)z^{-k}}{\left[C(z)\right]^2 F(z)}$$

which is likewise analytic in $|z| \ge 1$ for all $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$. Specifically, for an **ARMAX** model ($F(z) = D(z) = 1$), $W_u(z, \theta) = \frac{B(z)}{C(z)}$ and $W_y(z, \theta) = 1 - \frac{A(z)}{C(z)}$, so:
- $\frac{\partial \hat{y}(t|\theta)}{\partial a_k} = -\frac{1}{C(q)} y(t-k)$
- $\frac{\partial \hat{y}(t|\theta)}{\partial b_k} = \frac{1}{C(q)} u(t-k)$
- $\frac{\partial \hat{y}(t|\theta)}{\partial c_k} = -\frac{B(q)}{C^2(q)} u(t-k) + \frac{A(q)}{C^2(q)} y(t-k) = \frac{1}{C(q)} \left[ y(t-k) - \hat{y}(t-k|\theta) \right] = \frac{1}{C(q)} \varepsilon(t-k, \theta)$

Thus $C(q)\psi(t, \theta) = [-y(t-1), \dots, u(t-1), \dots, \varepsilon(t-1, \theta), \dots]^T$, which is stable whenever $C(z)$ has all zeros strictly inside the unit circle. $\blacksquare$

> [!important] Lemma 4.2: State-Space Model Structure
> Consider the discrete-time innovations state-space parametrization (4.91):
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned} x(t+1) &= A(\theta)x(t) + B(\theta)u(t) + K(\theta)e(t) \\ y(t) &= C(\theta)x(t) + e(t) \end{aligned}$$
>
> Assume that the entries of the matrices $A(\theta), B(\theta), K(\theta), C(\theta)$ are differentiable with respect to $\theta$. Suppose that $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$, with:
> $$
>D_{\mathcal{M}} = \left\{ \theta \;\middle|\; \text{all eigenvalues of } A(\theta) - K(\theta)C(\theta) \text{ are strictly inside the unit circle} \right\}$$
> 
> Then the parametrization of the corresponding predictor is a **model structure** in the sense of Definition 4.3. *(Proof: See Problem 4D.1).*

**Kalman Filter Riccati Domain (4.126)**: When $K(\theta)$ is not parametrized directly, but is computed from process/measurement noise covariances $R_1(\theta), R_2(\theta)$ via the algebraic Riccati equation (4.87), a standard Kalman filter theorem (Anderson and Moore, 1979) guarantees that $A(\theta) - K(\theta)C(\theta)$ has all eigenvalues strictly inside the unit circle if and only if the pair $[A(\theta), R_1(\theta)]$ is stabilizable and $[A(\theta), C(\theta)]$ is detectable:

>
> $$
> D_{\mathcal{M}} = \left\{ \theta \;\middle|\; \left[A(\theta), R_1(\theta)\right] \text{ stabilizable and } \left[A(\theta), C(\theta)\right] \text{ detectable} \right\} \tag{4.126}$$
>

> ##### Relating Model Structures: Inclusion & Independent Parametrizations
>
> [!important] Definition 4.4: Model Structure Containment ($\mathcal{M}_1 \subset \mathcal{M}_2$)
> A model structure $\mathcal{M}_1$ is said to be **contained in** $\mathcal{M}_2$, written:
> $$
>\mathcal{M}_1 \subset \mathcal{M}_2 \tag{4.127}$$
> 
> if $D_{\mathcal{M}_1} \subset D_{\mathcal{M}_2}$ and the mapping $\mathcal{M}_1$ is obtained by restricting $\mathcal{M}_2$ to $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}_1}$.
> *(Archetypical example: $\mathcal{M}_2$ defines $n$-th order models and $\mathcal{M}_1$ defines $m$-th order models with $m < n$, obtained by fixing the trailing parameters to zero; note that strictly speaking, fixing parameters restricts $\theta$ to a lower-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^d$).*

> [!important] Definition 4.5: Independently Parametrized Transfer Function and Noise Model
> A model structure $\mathcal{M}$ is said to have an **independently parametrized transfer function and noise model** if the parameter vector $\theta$ partitions into two completely separate subvectors $\rho$ (for $G$) and $\eta$ (for $H$) over a Cartesian product domain:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned} \theta &= \begin{bmatrix} \rho \\ \eta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D_{\mathcal{M}} = D_\rho \times D_\eta, \qquad \rho \in D_\rho, \quad \eta \in D_\eta \\ T(q, \theta) &= \begin{bmatrix} G(q, \rho) & H(q, \eta) \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \tag{4.128}$$
>
> **In the General Polynomial Family (4.33)**: Any structure with $A(q) \equiv 1$—specifically the **Output Error (OE)** structure ($H(q) \equiv 1$), the **FIR** structure, and the **Box-Jenkins (BJ)** structure ($G(q, \rho) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}$, $H(q, \eta) = \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}$)—has independently parametrized $G$ and $H$. Conversely, **ARX** and **ARMAX** share the common denominator polynomial $A(q)$ between $G$ and $H$, so they are *not* independently parametrized. As we will see in Chapter 8, independent parametrization (4.128) is a crucial structural property: it allows $G(q, \rho)$ to be consistently estimated in open loop even if the noise model $H(q, \eta)$ is completely misspecified!
>
> *(Remark on "Finite Model Structures", p. 111: If a model set is finite as in (4.120), indexing it by a discrete set of parameter points $\theta$ does not satisfy the open-domain differentiability of Definition 4.3, yet the estimation procedures of Sections 7.1–7.4 and convergence analyses of Sections 8.1–8.5 still apply).*
>
> ---
>
> #### 4.5.5 Model Set as a Range (and Union of Ranges) of Model Structures
>
> Every model structure $\mathcal{M} : D_{\mathcal{M}} \to \mathcal{L}^*$ defines a specific model set $\mathcal{M}^*$ as its **range** (image):
> $$
>\mathcal{M}^* = \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}) = \text{Range } \mathcal{M} = \left\{ \mathcal{M}(\theta) \;\middle|\; \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \right\}$$

Conversely, given a desired target model set $\mathcal{M}^*$, a central problem in system identification is to construct a smooth model structure $\mathcal{M}$ whose range equals $\mathcal{M}^*$.

##### Example 4.6: Parametrizing $\mathcal{M}_3^*$
Consider the model set $\mathcal{M}_n^*$ defined in (4.119) with $n = 3$ (all models where $W_y(q)$ and $W_u(q)$ are polynomials in $q^{-1}$ of degree at most $3$). Choosing:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 & b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{bmatrix}^T, \qquad d = 6, \qquad D_{\mathcal{M}} = \mathbb{R}^6$$

and defining the linear parametrization:
$$

\begin{aligned} W_y(q, \theta) &= -a_1 q^{-1} - a_2 q^{-2} - a_3 q^{-3} \\ W_u(q, \theta) &= b_1 q^{-1} + b_2 q^{-2} + b_3 q^{-3} \end{aligned}$$

we immediately obtain a smooth model structure $\mathcal{M}$ whose range $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M})$ equals $\mathcal{M}_3^*$. $\blacksquare$

##### Model Set as a Union of Ranges of Model Structures (Overlapping Manifold Charts)
While $\mathcal{M}_3^*$ in Example 4.6 could be covered by a single global model structure $\mathcal{M}$ with nice identifiability properties, there are important model sets—most notably **multi-output (MIMO) linear systems of a given McMillan degree $n$** (Appendix 4A) and collections of models of **different dynamical orders**—that *cannot* be globally parametrized by a single smooth, identifiable model structure $\mathcal{M}$ without singularities.

The remedy is to cover the model set $\mathcal{M}^*$ by a **union of ranges of $\beta$ distinct model structures** $\mathcal{M}_1, \dots, \mathcal{M}_\beta$:

>
> $$
> \mathcal{M}^* = \bigcup_{i=1}^\beta \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}_i) \tag{4.129}$$
>
> Geometrically, each $\mathcal{M}_i : D_{\mathcal{M}_i} \to \mathcal{M}^*$ acts as a local coordinate chart (e.g., a specific observability/reachability Kronecker index structure in MIMO systems, or a specific model order $i$), and the family $\{\mathcal{M}_i\}_{i=1}^\beta$ forms an **atlas** covering the entire model set $\mathcal{M}^*$.
>
> ---
>
> #### 4.5.6 Identifiability Properties of Model Structures
>
> Loosely speaking, **identifiability** asks whether the identification procedure uniquely recovers the parameter vector $\theta$ and/or the true system. This question splits into two distinct legs:
> 1. **Experimental Informative Data (Sections 8.2–8.3)**: Are the input-output data $z(t)$ sufficiently informative (persistently exciting) to distinguish between *unequal* models $W(q, \theta_1) \neq W(q, \theta_2)$?
> 2. **Structural Identifiability / Injectivity of $\mathcal{M}$ (Section 4.5–4.6)**: Even if we know the exact predictor transfer function $W(q, \theta^*)$ at all frequencies, can two *different* parameter vectors $\theta \neq \theta^*$ produce the *exact same* predictor model $\mathcal{M}(\theta) = \mathcal{M}(\theta^*)$? That is, is the mapping $\mathcal{M}$ **invertible (injective)**?
>
> [!important] Definition 4.6: Globally Identifiable at a Point $\theta^*$
> A model structure $\mathcal{M}$ is **globally identifiable at $\theta^*$** if:
> $$
>\mathcal{M}(\theta) = \mathcal{M}(\theta^*), \quad \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \implies \theta = \theta^* \tag{4.130}$$
> 
> *(Recall that model equality $\mathcal{M}(\theta) = \mathcal{M}(\theta^*)$ is defined by (4.116): $W(e^{i\omega}, \theta) = W(e^{i\omega}, \theta^*)$ for almost all $\omega$, which by the bijection (4.115) is equivalent to $G(e^{i\omega}, \theta) = G(e^{i\omega}, \theta^*)$ and $H(e^{i\omega}, \theta) = H(e^{i\omega}, \theta^*)$ for almost all $\omega$).*

Moving from a single point $\theta^*$ to the entire parameter domain $D_{\mathcal{M}}$:

> [!important] Definition 4.7: Strictly Globally Identifiable
> A model structure $\mathcal{M}$ is **strictly globally identifiable** if it is globally identifiable at **all** $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$.

> [!warning] Why Strict Global Identifiability Fails on Lower-Order Hypersurfaces (Pole-Zero Cancellation)
> Definition 4.7 is actually **too demanding** for almost all rational transfer-function structures of order $\ge 1$!
> - **Concrete Mechanism (Pole-Zero Cancellation)**: Consider even a simple ARX(2,2) or ARMAX(1,1,1) structure, say:
>
>   $$
>   G(q, \theta) = \frac{b_1 q^{-1} + b_2 q^{-2}}{1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2}}, \qquad H(q, \theta) = \frac{1 + c_1 q^{-1} + c_2 q^{-2}}{1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2}}$$
>
>   At any parameter point $\theta^*$ corresponding to a *first-order* system (for instance, where $b_1 = b_2 = 0$ and $A(q, \theta^*) = C(q, \theta^*)$, or where $A(q), B(q), C(q)$ share a common root $1 + \gamma q^{-1}$), the common factor $(1 + \gamma q^{-1})$ cancels in both $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$. Varying $\gamma$ yields an entire one-dimensional curve of distinct parameter vectors $\theta(\gamma) \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ that all produce the **exact same** input-output predictor $W(q, \theta) = W(q, \theta^*)$!
>   - Geometrically, the points $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ where such pole-zero cancellations occur form lower-dimensional algebraic **hypersurfaces** $\delta D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset D_{\mathcal{M}}$ corresponding to lower-order systems. Because $\delta D_{\mathcal{M}}$ is non-empty, the structure is *not* strictly globally identifiable (Definition 4.7 fails). However, these degenerate hypersurfaces have **Lebesgue measure zero** in $\mathbb{R}^d$, motivating the realistic definition below.
>
>   [!important] Definition 4.8: Globally Identifiable (Almost Everywhere)
>   A model structure $\mathcal{M}$ is **globally identifiable** if it is globally identifiable at **almost all** $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$.
>
>   **Measure-Theoretic Meaning**: There exists a subset $\tilde{D}_{\mathcal{M}} \subset D_{\mathcal{M}}$ such that $\mathcal{M}$ is globally identifiable at every $\theta^* \in \tilde{D}_{\mathcal{M}}$, and the exceptional ("singular") set where identifiability is lost,
>   $$
>\delta D_{\mathcal{M}} = \left\{ \theta \;\middle|\; \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}; \; \theta \notin \tilde{D}_{\mathcal{M}} \right\}$$
> 
> is a set of **Lebesgue measure zero** in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

##### Local Identifiability
If we only require injectivity within a small open $\varepsilon$-ball $\mathcal{B}(\theta^*, \varepsilon) = \{\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \mid \|\theta - \theta^*\| < \varepsilon\}$ around $\theta^*$, we obtain **local identifiability at $\theta^*$**:

$$
\mathcal{M}(\theta) = \mathcal{M}(\theta^*), \quad \theta \in \mathcal{B}(\theta^*, \varepsilon) \implies \theta = \theta^* \tag{4.131}$$

Strict local identifiability (for all $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$) and local identifiability (for almost all $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$) are defined analogously to Definitions 4.7 and 4.8 (see also Problem 4G.4, which links local identifiability at $\theta^*$ to the Jacobian/gradient rank condition).

#### 4.5.7 Use of the Identifiability Concept: Approximating the True System & $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$

How do we use structural identifiability when identifying an actual system? Let $\mathcal{S}$ denote a given LTI system description:
$$

\mathcal{S} : \quad y(t) = G_0(q)u(t) + H_0(q)e(t) \tag{4.132}$$

(which we may view as the "true" or "ideal" mathematical description of the physical plant, with associated true predictor $W_0(q)$). Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a candidate model structure based on one-step-ahead predictors for:

>
> $$
> y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t) \tag{4.133}$$
>
> [!important] Definition: The True Parameter Set $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$
> We define the set $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$ as the set of all parameter values $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ for which the model $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$ exactly matches the system description $\mathcal{S}$ ($\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{M}(\theta)$):
> $$
>D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \left\{ \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \;\middle|\; G_0(z) = G(z, \theta), \; H_0(z) = H(z, \theta) \text{ almost all } z \right\} \tag{4.134}$$

Three fundamental cases arise for $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$:
1. **Undermodeled / System Not in the Model Set ($\mathcal{S} \notin \mathcal{M}$)**:
   *(Here, with slight abuse of notation, $\mathcal{M}$ also denotes the range $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}) = \mathcal{M}^*$ of the mapping $\mathcal{M}$).* If no parameter $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ can reproduce $G_0(z)$ and $H_0(z)$, then:

   $$D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \emptyset$$

   In this case, identification algorithms will converge to the best *approximation* of $\mathcal{S}$ within $\mathcal{M}$ (analyzed in Chapter 8).
2. **System in the Model Set ($\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$) and Globally Identifiable at $\theta_0$**:
   Suppose that $\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$ so that $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{M}(\theta_0)$ for some true parameter vector $\theta_0 \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$, and suppose further that **$\mathcal{M}$ is globally identifiable at $\theta_0$** (Definition 4.6). Then by (4.130), no other $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ can equal $\mathcal{M}(\theta_0)$, so $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$ is a **singleton**:

   $$D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \{\theta_0\} \tag{4.135}$$

3. **System in the Model Set ($\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$) at an Unidentifiable / Overparametrized Point**:
   If $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{M}(\theta_0)$ where $\theta_0 \in \delta D_{\mathcal{M}}$ lies on a singular lower-order hypersurface (e.g., our model structure has higher order than the true system $\mathcal{S}$, causing pole-zero cancellation, or redundant parameters exist), then $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$ is an infinite **manifold/subspace of equivalent parameters** rather than a single point. For example:
   - *Overparametrization / Pole-Zero Cancellation*: If the true system $\mathcal{S}$ is white noise $y(t) = e(t)$ ($G_0(q) = 0, H_0(q) = 1$) and we fit an ARMA(1,1) model $y(t) + a y(t-1) = e(t) + c e(t-1)$ with $\theta = [a \quad c]^T$ and $|c| < 1$, then $H(z, \theta) = \frac{1 + c z^{-1}}{1 + a z^{-1}} = 1 \iff a = c$. Hence $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \{[a \quad c]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid a = c, |c| < 1\}$ is a continuous line segment in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
   - *Redundant Gain Parametrization*: If $y(t) = b_0 b_1 u(t-1) + e(t)$ with $\theta = [b_0 \quad b_1]^T$ and true system $y(t) = u(t-1) + e(t)$, then $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \{[b_0 \quad b_1]^T \mid b_0 b_1 = 1\}$ is a hyperbola where $\mathcal{M}$ is not even locally identifiable anywhere.

**Engineering Takeaway**: A primary goal in model structure selection is to choose $\mathcal{M}$ so that $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \{\theta_0\}$ in (4.135) holds for the unknown plant $\mathcal{S}$. Because $\mathcal{S}$ (and its true order) is unknown a priori, this requires testing several candidate structures $\mathcal{M}$ of different orders and using the identifiability criteria developed in Section 4.6.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 (Unstable Plant vs. Stable Predictor in Definition 4.1 & Example 4.4)**: Do not confuse the stability of the *system* $G(q), H(q)$ with the stability of the *one-step-ahead predictor* $W(q) = [H^{-1}(q)G(q) \quad 1 - H^{-1}(q)]$. Because $H^{-1}(q)$ cancels the unstable pole polynomial $A(q)$ in an ARX or ARMAX model, an unstable system can have a completely stable predictor $W(q)$! What matters for predictor stability in the general family (4.35) is that $C(z)F(z)$ has all zeros strictly inside the unit circle (Lemma 4.1)—the roots of $A(z)$ and $D(z)$ appear in the *numerator* of $W(z, \theta)$ and therefore never cause predictor instability.
> - **Leap 2 (Why "Strict Global Identifiability" Fails for Standard Linear Structures)**: Intuitively, you might expect a good model structure like ARMAX($n, n, n$) to be injective at *every* parameter vector $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ (Definition 4.7). Watch closely on pages 112–113 why Ljung introduces **Definition 4.8 (Globally Identifiable at almost all $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$)**: whenever the true parameters lie on the lower-dimensional hypersurface $\delta D_{\mathcal{M}}$ where $A(q), B(q), C(q)$ share a common factor (i.e., the system is actually of order $< n$), pole-zero cancellation creates a continuum of equivalent parameters. Thus, linear model structures can at best be globally identifiable *almost everywhere* (outside $\delta D_{\mathcal{M}}$ of Lebesgue measure zero).
> - **Notation Trap 1 ($\mathcal{M}^*$ vs. $\mathcal{M}$ vs. $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$)**: Keep Ljung's calligraphic typography razor-sharp:
>   - $\mathcal{M}^*$ (with an asterisk) is a **model set**—an unparametrized collection of predictor filters $W(q)$.
>   - $\mathcal{M}$ (without an asterisk) is a **model structure**—the differentiable mapping $\theta \mapsto \mathcal{M}(\theta)$ from $D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ to $\mathcal{M}^*$. (Note: on page 113, Ljung mentions that by slight abuse of notation, writing $\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$ means $\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}) = \mathcal{M}^*$).
>   - $\mathcal{M}(\theta) = W(q, \theta)$ is the **individual model** corresponding to a single parameter vector $\theta$.
> - **Notation Trap 2 (Matrix Dimensions of $\Psi(z, \theta)$ and $T'(q, \theta)$)**: For a SISO system with a $d$-dimensional parameter vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $W(z, \theta) = [W_u(z, \theta) \quad W_y(z, \theta)]$ is $1 \times 2$, and differentiating with respect to the column vector $\theta$ produces a **$d \times 2$ matrix** $\Psi(z, \theta) = [\Psi_u(z, \theta) \quad \Psi_y(z, \theta)]$. That is why multiplying $\Psi(q, \theta)$ ($d \times 2$) on the right by the $2 \times 1$ data vector $z(t) = [u(t) \quad y(t)]^T$ yields the $d \times 1$ predictor gradient column vector $\psi(t, \theta)$.
