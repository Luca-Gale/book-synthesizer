# 4.1 Linear Models and Sets of Linear Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 4**: Section 4.1 bridges the non-parametric, theoretical characterization of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems and optimal predictors from Chapters 2 and 3 with practical system identification by introducing finite-dimensional parameter vectors $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$. It establishes the foundational distinction between a single model $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$, a parametrized *set of models*, and a *model structure* $\mathcal{M}$—setting the stage for the specific black-box transfer-function (Section 4.2), state-space (Section 4.3), and continuous-time/physically parametrized (Section 4.4) model families studied throughout Chapter 4.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

### 1.1 The Philosophy of a Model in System Identification
Chapter 4 opens with a pragmatic philosophical statement that governs the entire discipline of system identification:

> *"A model of a system is a description of (some of) its properties, suitable for a certain purpose. The model need not be a true and accurate description of the system, nor need the user have to believe so, in order to serve its purpose."*

From an engineering perspective, real physical processes (chemical reactors, aircraft aeroelasticity, power grids) are infinite-dimensional, nonlinear, and subject to complex environmental disturbances. System identification does not seek ontological "truth"; rather, it seeks to construct or select a tractable mathematical description from a candidate **class of models** that is fit for a specific engineering task (e.g., feedback control design, minimum-variance prediction, fault detection, or simulation). Therefore, the very first step in any identification procedure is defining the candidate class of models within which the search for the most suitable model will be conducted.

### 1.2 Why Infinite Sequences and Arbitrary PDFs Are Impractical
In Chapters 2 and 3, a general discrete-time LTI description was specified by:
1. The infinite deterministic impulse response sequence $\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$ defining the plant transfer operator $G(q)$.
2. The infinite stochastic impulse response sequence $\{h(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}$ (or disturbance spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$) defining the noise filter $H(q)$.
3. The full probability density function (PDF) $f_e(\cdot)$ of the driving white-noise innovation sequence $\{e(t)\}$.

Attempting to identify a system by directly estimating two infinite sequences of numbers $\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ and $\{h(k)\}_{k=1}^\infty$ alongside an arbitrary continuous function $f_e(x)$ from a finite record of input-output data $\{u(t), y(t)\}_{t=1}^N$ is hopeless—there would be infinitely many unknowns and only finitely many observations.

### 1.3 Turning Identification into a Finite-Dimensional Parameter Search
To make system identification computationally and statistically tractable, we introduce two key simplifications:
1. **Finite-Dimensional Structural Parametrization**: Instead of enumerating infinite impulse response sequences $\{g(k)\}$ and $\{h(k)\}$, we work with algebraic structures—such as rational transfer functions (ratios of finite-order polynomials in $q^{-1}$) or finite-dimensional state-space matrices—whose entire infinite impulse response is completely determined by a finite number of numerical coefficients.
2. **Moment Characterization of the Noise PDF**: Instead of specifying the entire function $f_e(x)$, we typically characterize the white noise $e(t)$ by only its first two moments (zero mean and variance $\lambda$), or assume $e(t)$ is Gaussian so that $\lambda$ completely determines $f_e(x)$.

Because these finite numerical coefficients (or a subset of them) cannot usually be deduced purely from first-principles physics, they must be left to estimation procedures as unknown adjustable parameters collected into a $d$-dimensional parameter vector $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$. Once $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ are parametrized by $\theta$, writing down the system equation no longer specifies a *single* model—it defines a **set of models** indexed by $\theta$, turning system identification into a structured optimization search over $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ for the parameter vector $\theta$ whose one-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ best explains the observed data.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

### 4.1.1 Complete Unparametrized LTI Model Specification
Recall from Chapter 2 that a general discrete-time linear time-invariant (LTI) model with additive disturbance $v(t) = H(q)e(t)$ is specified by the impulse response $\{g(k)\}_{1}^{\infty}$, the disturbance spectrum $\Phi_v(\omega) = \lambda |H(e^{i\omega})|^2$, and, for a complete probabilistic characterization, the probability density function (PDF) $f_e(\cdot)$ of the white-noise disturbance $e(t)$.

Thus, a **complete unparametrized LTI model** is given by Eq. (4.1):

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t) \\
f_e(\cdot), &\quad \text{the PDF of } e
\end{aligned} \tag{4.1}
$$

where the transfer operators $G(q)$ and $H(q)$ are defined by their causal power-series (impulse response) expansions in the backward shift operator $q^{-1}$ as in Eq. (4.2):

$$
G(q) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} g(k)q^{-k}, \qquad H(q) = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k)q^{-k} \tag{4.2}
$$

> [!note] Structural Assumptions in Eq. (4.2)
> 1. **Strict One-Sample Delay in $G(q)$**: The summation for $G(q)$ starts at $k = 1$ ($g(0) = 0$), meaning the plant has at least a one-sample delay so that $u(t)$ does not instantaneously affect $y(t)$ at the exact same sample instant $t$.
> 2. **Monicity of $H(q)$**: The disturbance filter $H(q)$ is normalized so that its leading zero-lag coefficient is $h(0) = 1$ ($H(\infty) = 1$). Any non-unit scaling of the driving noise is absorbed into the variance $\lambda$ (and PDF $f_e(\cdot)$) of $e(t)$.

---

### 4.1.2 Finite Characterization of the Disturbance PDF $f_e(\cdot)$
Specifying a particular model via (4.1)–(4.2) requires specifying the three functions $G$, $H$, and $f_e$. Because enumerating the infinite sequences $\{g(k)\}$ and $\{h(k)\}$ and an arbitrary continuous function $f_e(x)$ is impractical, we restrict $G$ and $H$ to structures determined by finitely many numerical values (such as rational transfer functions or finite-dimensional state-space descriptions), and we describe $f_e(x)$ using only its first and second moments as given in Eq. (4.3):

$$
\begin{aligned}
Ee(t) &= \int x f_e(x)\,dx = 0 \\
Ee^2(t) &= \int x^2 f_e(x)\,dx = \lambda
\end{aligned} \tag{4.3}
$$

- **Zero-Mean Assumption ($Ee(t) = 0$)**: Ensures that $e(t)$ represents zero-mean random innovations rather than a deterministic constant offset.
- **Second-Moment Parameter ($\lambda$)**: Captures the intensity (variance) of the innovations.
- **Gaussian Sufficiency**: It is very common to assume that $e(t)$ is Gaussian ($e(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda)$), in which case the entire PDF $f_e(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}} \exp(-x^2 / (2\lambda))$ is *uniquely and completely specified* by the two moment conditions in Eq. (4.3).

---

### 4.1.3 Parametrization by a Finite-Dimensional Vector $\theta$
When the finite number of numerical coefficients defining $G$, $H$, and $f_e$ cannot be determined *a priori* from physical laws, some or all of them must be left to estimation procedures as unknown **parameters to be determined**. Grouping these unknown coefficients into a $d$-dimensional parameter vector $\theta$, the model description (4.1) becomes Eq. (4.4):

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t) \qquad &(4.4\text{a}) \\
f_e(x, \theta), &\quad \text{the PDF of } e(t); \; \{e(t)\} \text{ white noise} \qquad &(4.4\text{b})
\end{aligned}
$$

where the parameter vector $\theta$ ranges over an admissible subset $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ of $d$-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$, as stated in Eq. (4.5):

$$
\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d \tag{4.5}
$$

Here $d = \dim(\theta)$ is the total number of adjustable scalar parameters, and $D_{\mathcal{M}}$ typically enforces structural constraints such as stability of $G(q, \theta)$ and invertibility (minimum-phase property) of $H(q, \theta)$.

> [!important] Crucial Conceptual Distinction: A Model vs. A Set of Models
> Equations (4.4)–(4.5) **no longer define a single model**—they define a **set of models** indexed by $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$. Each specific numerical choice of $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ picks out one individual candidate model from this set. It is the job of the parameter estimation procedure to select the single member in the set that appears most suitable for the intended purpose. (As Ljung notes, engineers often loosely refer to "the model (4.4)", which is convenient shorthand but formally an abuse of notation.)

---

### 4.1.4 Parametrized One-Step-Ahead Predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$
Recall from Section 3.2, Eq. (3.20), that for any given LTI model $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ with monic and stably invertible $H(q)$, the optimal one-step-ahead prediction $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ of $y(t)$ given all past input-output data $\{y(s), u(s)\}_{s \le t-1}$ is obtained by filtering $u(t)$ and $y(t)$ through $H^{-1}(q)$:
1. Multiply $y(t) = G(q)u(t) + H(q)e(t)$ on the left by $H^{-1}(q, \theta)$:

$$
H^{-1}(q, \theta)y(t) = H^{-1}(q, \theta)G(q, \theta)u(t) + e(t)
$$

2. Rearrange $y(t)$ by adding and subtracting $H^{-1}(q, \theta)y(t)$:

$$
y(t) = \underbrace{H^{-1}(q, \theta)G(q, \theta)u(t) + \left[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)\right]y(t)}_{\text{depends only on data up to } t-1} + e(t)
$$

3. Because $H(q, \theta) = 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} h(k, \theta)q^{-k}$ is monic, its inverse $H^{-1}(q, \theta)$ is also monic ($1 + \tilde{h}_1 q^{-1} + \cdots$), meaning $[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)]$ contains only strictly causal delays $q^{-1}, q^{-2}, \dots$, and $G(q, \theta)$ likewise starts at $q^{-1}$. Taking the conditional expectation given past data up to $t-1$ annihilates the unpredictable white noise innovation $e(t)$ and yields the **parametrized one-step-ahead predictor** $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ in Eq. (4.6):

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = H^{-1}(q, \theta)G(q, \theta)u(t) + \left[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)\right]y(t) \tag{4.6}
$$

Here the notation $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ replaces $\hat{y}(t|t-1)$ to explicitly emphasize that the prediction is a function of the candidate parameter vector $\theta$.

---

### 4.1.5 Predictor Models vs. Probabilistic Models & Model Structure $\mathcal{M}$
A fundamental structural property of Eq. (4.6) is that **the predictor form $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ does not depend on the noise PDF $f_e(x, \theta)$** (not even on the noise variance $\lambda$, unless $\lambda$ somehow enters $G$ or $H$). In fact, as emphasized in Section 3.3, one can arrive at the linear predictor rule (4.6) directly from non-probabilistic considerations (such as deterministic curve-fitting or frequency-domain approximation) without ever postulating a stochastic process $\{e(t)\}$ or a PDF $f_e(x, \theta)$.

This motivates Ljung's formal taxonomy at the end of Section 4.1:
1. **Predictor Models**: Models that specify *only* the transfer operators $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ in (4.4a), or equivalently specify only the one-step-ahead predictor rule $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ in Eq. (4.6), without specifying $f_e(x, \theta)$.
2. **Probabilistic Models**: Complete descriptions of the form (4.4a)–(4.4b) that specify $G(q, \theta)$, $H(q, \theta)$, *and* the noise PDF $f_e(x, \theta)$, thereby providing a full probabilistic characterization of the random process $\{y(t)\}$.
3. **Model Structure ($\mathcal{M}$) and Individual Model ($\mathcal{M}(\theta)$)**:
   - A parametrized set of models like Eq. (4.6) (or Eq. (4.4)) is called a **model structure** and is denoted by the script letter $\mathcal{M}$.
   - For a specific numerical parameter vector $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$, the particular individual model associated with $\theta$ is denoted by $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$.
   - *(Preview of Sections 4.5–4.6)*: Formally, $\mathcal{M}$ is the mapping $\theta \mapsto \mathcal{M}(\theta)$ from the parameter space $D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ into the space of models (the **model set** $\mathcal{M}^* = \{\mathcal{M}(\theta) \mid \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}\}$). Section 4.5 formalizes model sets, parametrizations, model structures, and uniqueness of parametrization, while Section 4.6 addresses *identifiability* (whether distinct parameter vectors $\theta_1 \neq \theta_2$ can produce the exact same model $\mathcal{M}(\theta_1) = \mathcal{M}(\theta_2)$).

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 — Why $[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)]y(t)$ Uses Only Past Outputs**: At first glance, Eq. (4.6) has $y(t)$ on the right-hand side of the formula for $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$, which looks circular! Remember that $H(q, \theta) = 1 + h_1(\theta)q^{-1} + h_2(\theta)q^{-2} + \cdots$ is **monic** (starts with $1$). Consequently, its series inverse $H^{-1}(q, \theta) = 1 - h_1(\theta)q^{-1} + \cdots$ also starts with $1$. Subtracting $H^{-1}(q, \theta)$ from $1$ cancels the leading $1$, leaving $[1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)] = h_1(\theta)q^{-1} + \cdots$, which operates exclusively on strictly past outputs $y(t-1), y(t-2), \dots$.
> - **Leap 2 — Independence of the Predictor from $f_e(x, \theta)$**: Even though $H(q, \theta)$ models the disturbance pathway, the predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ in Eq. (4.6) requires only the operators $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$—not the variance $\lambda$ or the shape of $f_e(x, \theta)$. The PDF $f_e(x, \theta)$ only comes into play later when constructing maximum likelihood (ML) criteria or computing asymptotic parameter covariance matrices.
> - **Notation Trap — "The Model (4.4)" vs. Model Structure $\mathcal{M}$ vs. Individual Model $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$**: Watch out for casual phrasing in the literature where Eq. (4.4) is called "a model". Strictly speaking, once $\theta$ is an unfixed variable in $D_{\mathcal{M}}$, Eq. (4.4) or (4.6) is a **model structure** $\mathcal{M}$ (a family/mapping of models), whereas $\mathcal{M}(\theta)$ for a fixed vector $\theta$ is a single **model**.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *How do different ways of parametrizing $G(q, \theta)$ and $H(q, \theta)$ as rational functions in Section 4.2 affect the algebraic form of the predictor filters $H^{-1}(q, \theta)G(q, \theta)$ and $1 - H^{-1}(q, \theta)$ in Eq. (4.6)?*
>   2. *When is the predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ a strictly linear function of the parameter vector $\theta$ (allowing one-shot linear least-squares estimation), and when does $H^{-1}(q, \theta)$ make $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ nonlinear in $\theta$?*
