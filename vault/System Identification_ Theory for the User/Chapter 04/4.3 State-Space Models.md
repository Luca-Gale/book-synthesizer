# 4.3 State-Space Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 4**: While Section 4.2 introduced black-box polynomial input-output structures (ARX, ARMAX, OE, BJ) that treat the plant as an external transfer operator in the shift operator $q$, Section 4.3 introduces **state-space models** that represent the internal mechanisms of linear dynamical systems through an auxiliary state vector $x(t)$. This provides the primary mathematical bridge between **continuous-time physical laws** (grey-box modeling with unknown physical constants $\theta$), **sampled-data discrete-time systems**, **Kalman filtering and innovations representations**, and **multivariable (MIMO) canonical black-box parametrizations**, preparing the ground for distributed-parameter (PDE) models in Section 4.4.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Why move from the polynomial difference equations of Section 4.2 to state-space models? There are four fundamental engineering reasons highlighted in Section 4.3:

1. **Physical Insight Lives in Continuous-Time Differential Equations (Grey-Box Modeling)**:
   - Fundamental laws of physics—Newton's laws of motion, Kirchhoff's voltage/current laws, mass and energy balances—are naturally formulated in continuous time as coupled first-order differential equations:
     $$\dot{x}(t) = F(\theta)x(t) + G(\theta)u(t)$$
   - Here, the components of the state vector $x(t)$ have direct physical significance (e.g., angular positions, velocities, currents, temperatures), and the parameter vector $\theta$ represents unknown physical constants (e.g., moments of inertia, friction coefficients, resistances, thermal conductivities).
   - In polynomial input-output models (like ARX or OE), physical parameters get scrambled nonlinearly across all polynomial coefficients when you eliminate internal states and discretize. Keeping the model in state-space form allows us to preserve the exact structural sparsity and physical parametrization of $\theta$, often reducing the number of unknown parameters to estimate and thereby **decreasing the variance of the parameter estimates**.

2. **Exact Zero-Order Hold Discretization**:
   - Even though physical modeling starts in continuous time, computer-based system identification collects measurements at discrete sampling instants $t = kT$.
   - When the input signal $u(t)$ is held piecewise constant over each sampling interval $kT \le t < (k+1)T$ (standard zero-order hold from a digital-to-analog converter), integrating the continuous-time state equation over one sample period $T$ yields an **exact** discrete-time state-space model with transition matrix $A_T(\theta) = e^{F(\theta)T}$ and input matrix $B_T(\theta) = \int_0^T e^{F(\theta)\tau} G(\theta)\,d\tau$—involving **zero approximation**.

3. **Distinguishing Process Noise from Measurement Noise via the Kalman Filter**:
   - Unlike polynomial models that lump all disturbances into a single output term $v(t) = H(q, \theta)e(t)$, state-space models naturally separate **process noise** $w(t)$ (disturbances acting directly on the internal states, such as random load torque fluctuations on a motor shaft) from **measurement noise** $v(t)$ (sensor imperfections, such as potentiometer quantization or thermal noise).
   - Once $w(t)$ and $v(t)$ are specified with covariance matrices $R_1(\theta)$, $R_2(\theta)$, and cross-covariance $R_{12}(\theta)$, the **Kalman filter** immediately provides the optimal one-step-ahead predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta)$ via a steady-state algebraic Riccati equation (or a time-varying Riccati recursion when initial-state uncertainty on short data records matters).

4. **The Directly Parametrized Innovations Form & Black-Box Canonical Forms**:
   - Rewriting the Kalman filter in terms of the one-step-ahead prediction error (the **innovation** $e(t) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$) produces the **innovations representation** (Equations `(4.91a)`–`(4.91b)`), where the single noise source $e(t)$ drives the state update through the Kalman gain $K(\theta)$.
   - If we lack physical insight into the noise covariances $R_1, R_2, R_{12}$, we can bypass the nonlinear Riccati equation completely by **parametrizing the Kalman gain $K(\theta)$ directly**. Furthermore, by choosing $A(\theta), B(\theta), C(\theta), K(\theta)$ in **companion / observer canonical form** (Example 4.2), the directly parametrized innovations form becomes algebraically identical to an ARMAX model—while offering superior numerical conditioning and effortless generalization to multi-output (MIMO) systems.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

### 4.3.1 Continuous-Time Models Based on Physical Insight

When we model a physical plant from first principles (Newton's laws, electrical circuit laws, conservation equations), the dynamics are naturally expressed in continuous time using an $n$-dimensional state vector $x(t)$ (positions, velocities, currents, etc.) and an $m$-dimensional input vector $u(t)$:

$$
\dot{x}(t) = F(\theta)x(t) + G(\theta)u(t) \tag{4.62}$$

where $\dot{x}(t) = \frac{d}{dt}x(t)$, $F(\theta)$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, $G(\theta)$ is an $n \times m$ matrix, and $\theta$ is a vector of unknown physical parameters (material constants, resistances, friction coefficients, etc.).

Let $\eta(t)$ denote the $p$-dimensional output vector that would be obtained with **ideal, noise-free sensors** measuring known linear combinations of the states:
$$

\eta(t) = H x(t) \tag{4.63}$$

#### Conversion to Continuous-Time Transfer Function
Introducing the differentiation operator $p = \frac{d}{dt}$, Equation `(4.62)` can be written algebraically as:

$$
[pI - F(\theta)]x(t) = G(\theta)u(t)$$

Solving for $x(t) = [pI - F(\theta)]^{-1} G(\theta)u(t)$ and substituting into `(4.63)` yields the continuous-time input-output relationship:
$$

\eta(t) = G_c(p, \theta)u(t)$$

$$
G_c(p, \theta) = H[pI - F(\theta)]^{-1} G(\theta) \tag{4.64}$$

This gives a continuous-time transfer-function model (as in Section 2.2, Equation `(2.22)`) directly parametrized in terms of the physical coefficient vector $\theta$.

#### Introducing Sampled Measurements with Lumped Disturbances
In practice, $\eta(t)$ is corrupted by both measurement imperfections and process disturbances acting on `(4.62)`. In the simplest formulation, we sample the output at discrete time instants $t = kT$ ($k = 1, 2, \dots$) and lump all disturbance and sensor noise effects at those instants into an additive discrete-time disturbance term $v_T(kT)$:
$$

y(kT) = H x(kT) + v_T(kT) = G_c(p, \theta)u(t)\big|_{t=kT} + v_T(kT) \tag{4.65}$$

---

### 4.3.2 Sampling the Continuous-Time Model (Zero-Order Hold)

To convert the continuous-time state equation `(4.62)` into an explicit discrete-time representation, we invoke the **Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) assumption**:

> [!important] Assumption: Piecewise Constant Input (Zero-Order Hold)
> The input signal $u(t)$ is held constant over each sampling interval $[kT, (k+1)T)$:
>
> $$
> u(t) = u_k = u(kT), \qquad kT \le t < (k + 1)T \tag{4.66}$$
>
> #### Step-by-Step Derivation of the Sampled State Equation
> 1. Recall the general variation-of-constants solution to the linear time-invariant ODE $\dot{x}(t) = F(\theta)x(t) + G(\theta)u(t)$ over the interval $t \in [kT, kT + T]$:
>    $$x(kT + T) = e^{F(\theta)T} x(kT) + \int_{kT}^{kT+T} e^{F(\theta)(kT + T - s)} G(\theta) u(s)\,ds$$
> 2. Because $u(s) = u(kT)$ is constant for all $s \in [kT, kT + T)$ by `(4.66)`, $u(kT)$ can be factored out to the right of the integral.
> 3. Applying the change of integration variable $\tau = kT + T - s$ (so $ds = -d\tau$, with limits $\tau = T$ at $s = kT$ and $\tau = 0$ at $s = kT + T$), we obtain the exact discrete-time state update:
> $$
>x(kT + T) = A_T(\theta)x(kT) + B_T(\theta)u(kT) \tag{4.67}$$

where the sampled state matrices are given by:

$$
A_T(\theta) = e^{F(\theta)T} \tag{4.68a}$$
$$

B_T(\theta) = \int_{\tau=0}^{T} e^{F(\theta)\tau} G(\theta)\,d\tau \tag{4.68b}$$

#### Sampled Transfer Operator
Introducing the forward shift operator $q$ of $T$ time units ($q x(kT) = x(kT + T)$), Equation `(4.67)` becomes:

$$
[qI - A_T(\theta)]x(kT) = B_T(\theta)u(kT) \tag{4.69}$$

Substituting $x(kT) = [qI - A_T(\theta)]^{-1} B_T(\theta)u(kT)$ into $\eta(kT) = H x(kT)$ yields the discrete-time transfer operator $G_T(q, \theta)$:
$$

\eta(kT) = G_T(q, \theta)u(kT) \tag{4.70}$$

$$
G_T(q, \theta) = H[qI - A_T(\theta)]^{-1} B_T(\theta) \tag{4.71}$$

Consequently, the noisy sampled output model `(4.65)` can be written in sampled-data transfer-function form:
$$

y(t) = G_T(q, \theta)u(t) + v_T(t), \qquad t = T, 2T, 3T, \dots \tag{4.72}$$

> [!note] Exactness vs. Parametric Complexity
> Whenever the input is piecewise constant (`(4.66)`), **no approximation whatsoever** is involved in `(4.67)`–`(4.72)`. However, because $A_T(\theta)$ and $B_T(\theta)$ involve the matrix exponential $e^{F(\theta)T}$ (`(4.68)`), the sampled transfer function $G_T(q, \theta)$ can be a fairly complicated nonlinear function of the physical parameter vector $\theta$.

### 4.3.3 Example 4.1: Physical Modeling & Sampling of a DC Servomotor

To make the continuous-to-discrete grey-box pipeline concrete, Ljung analyzes a physical DC servomotor whose input is the applied armature voltage $u(t)$ and whose measured output is the angular position $\eta(t)$ of the motor shaft.

![[assets/fig_4_3_dc_motor_schematic.png]]

> **How to Read This Diagram (`Figure 4.5`)**:
> - This physical cutaway schematic shows the **DC servomotor**: the stationary outer stator poles create a magnetic field across the air gap, while the inner cylindrical rotor (armature) carries longitudinal conductor windings connected to the left-hand terminals via commutator brushes.
> - Applying a voltage $u(t)$ across the input terminals drives an armature current $i(t)$ through the rotor windings, generating an electromagnetic torque $T_a(t)$ that rotates the central motor shaft through angle $\eta(t)$.

![[assets/fig_4_3_dc_motor_block_diagram.png]]

> **How to Read This Diagram (`Figure 4.6`)**:
> - **Electrical Armature Circuit (Left)**: The input voltage $u$ is applied across the series armature resistance $R_a$, armature inductance $L_a$, and the rotating armature brush symbol. As the rotor spins, it generates an opposing back-electromotive force (back-EMF) voltage $s$, with current $i$ flowing into the armature.
> - **Electromechanical Coupling & Mechanical Load (Right)**: The rotor drives the mechanical shaft and attached inertial load block through shaft angle $\eta$, while an external load torque $T_\ell$ opposes the rotation.

#### Step 1: Continuous-Time Physical Equations
1. **Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (Armature Circuit)**: The relationship between applied voltage $u(t)$, rotor current $i(t)$, armature resistance $R_a$, armature inductance $L_a$, and back-EMF $s(t)$ is:
   $$u(t) = R_a i(t) + L_a \frac{di(t)}{dt} + s(t) \tag{4.73}$$
2. **Back-EMF Law**: The back electromotive force $s(t)$ induced by rotating the armature in the magnetic field is proportional to the angular velocity $\frac{d}{dt}\eta(t)$:
   $$s(t) = k_v \frac{d}{dt}\eta(t)$$
3. **Electromagnetic Motor Torque**: The current $i(t)$ produces a turning torque $T_a(t)$ on the shaft proportional to $i(t)$:
   $$T_a(t) = k_a \cdot i(t)$$
4. **Newton's Second Law for Rotation (Mechanical Shaft + Load)**: Let $J$ be the combined moment of inertia of the rotor plus load, $f$ be the viscous friction coefficient, and $T_\ell(t)$ be the external load torque opposing motion:
   $$J \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\eta(t) = T_a(t) - T_\ell(t) - f \frac{d}{dt}\eta(t) \tag{4.74}$$

#### Step 2: Neglecting Armature Inductance & Forming the State-Space Model
> [!important] Physical Simplification Assumption ($L_a \approx 0$)
> Assuming the electrical time constant of the armature inductance is negligible compared to the mechanical dynamics ($L_a \approx 0$), `(4.73)` simplifies algebraically to:
>
> $$
> i(t) = \frac{u(t) - s(t)}{R_a} = \frac{u(t) - k_v \dot{\eta}(t)}{R_a}$$
>
> Substituting $T_a(t) = k_a i(t) = \frac{k_a}{R_a}u(t) - \frac{k_a k_v}{R_a}\dot{\eta}(t)$ into Newton's rotational equation `(4.74)` gives:
> $$
>J \ddot{\eta}(t) + \left(f + \frac{k_a k_v}{R_a}\right)\dot{\eta}(t) = \frac{k_a}{R_a}u(t) - T_\ell(t)$$

Dividing through by $\left(f + \frac{k_a k_v}{R_a}\right) = \frac{f R_a + k_a k_v}{R_a}$ and defining the physical state vector of shaft angle and angular velocity:

$$
x(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \eta(t) \\ \frac{d}{dt}\eta(t) \end{bmatrix}$$

along with the three composite physical constants (time constant $\tau$, voltage-to-velocity gain $\beta$, and load-torque gain $\gamma'$):
$$

\tau = \frac{J R_a}{f R_a + k_a k_v}, \qquad \beta = \frac{k_a}{f R_a + k_a k_v}, \qquad \gamma' = -\frac{R_a}{f R_a + k_a k_v}$$

we arrive at the second-order continuous-time state-space model:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}x(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1/\tau \end{bmatrix} x(t) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \beta/\tau \end{bmatrix} u(t) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \gamma'/\tau \end{bmatrix} T_\ell(t) \tag{4.75}$$
$$

\eta(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} x(t)$$

#### Step 3: Exact Zero-Order Hold Sampling of the DC Motor
Assume now that the external load torque $T_\ell(t)$ is identically zero, and we apply a piecewise constant input $u(t)$ with sampling period $T$. Then `(4.75)` is parameterized by the 2-dimensional physical parameter vector:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \tau \\ \beta \end{bmatrix}$$

and evolves at the sample instants according to:
$$

x(t + T) = A_T(\theta)x(t) + B_T(\theta)u(t) \tag{4.76}$$

Let us explicitly verify the matrix exponential integrals `(4.68a)`–`(4.68b)` for $F(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1/\tau \end{bmatrix}$ and $G(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \beta/\tau \end{bmatrix}$:
- Since $F^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1/\tau \\ 0 & 1/\tau^2 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{\tau}F$, the power series $e^{F\tau_1} = I + F\tau_1 + \frac{F^2 \tau_1^2}{2!} + \dots$ sums in the $(1,2)$ entry to $\tau(1 - e^{-\tau_1/\tau})$ and in the $(2,2)$ entry to $e^{-\tau_1/\tau}$. Evaluating at $\tau_1 = T$ gives $A_T(\theta)$.
- Multiplying $e^{F\tau_1} G(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \beta(1 - e^{-\tau_1/\tau}) \\ (\beta/\tau)e^{-\tau_1/\tau} \end{bmatrix}$ and integrating from $\tau_1 = 0$ to $T$ yields $B_T(\theta)$:

$$
A_T(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tau(1 - e^{-T/\tau}) \\ 0 & e^{-T/\tau} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B_T(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \beta(\tau e^{-T/\tau} - \tau + T) \\ \beta(1 - e^{-T/\tau}) \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.77}$$

#### Step 4: Measurement Noise and Natural Predictor
Assume the actual measurement $y(t)$ of the shaft angle $\eta(t)$ is corrupted by a sensor error $v(t)$ (e.g., due to the finite winding resolution/truncation of a potentiometer):
$$

y(t) = \eta(t) + v(t) \tag{4.78}$$

If measurements are not taken too frequently, $v(t)$ can be modeled as a sequence of independent random variables (white noise) with zero mean and known variance $R_2$, yielding the output-error-type sampled model:

$$
y(t) = G_T(q, \theta)u(t) + v(t)$$

Since $v(t)$ is white noise, the natural one-step-ahead predictor is simply the noise-free output:
$$

\hat{y}(t|\theta) = G_T(q, \theta)u(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} [qI - A_T(\theta)]^{-1} B_T(\theta)u(t) \tag{4.79}$$

> [!tip] Grey-Box Parsimony vs. Black-Box Linearity Trade-Off
> Notice the fundamental engineering trade-off revealed by `(4.79)`:
> - **Grey-box state-space predictor `(4.79)`**: Parametrized by **only 2 parameters** ($\tau$ and $\beta$). Estimating fewer parameters reduces the variance of the resulting parameter estimates!
> - **Black-box 2nd-order ARX or OE model**: If we only used physical insight to say "the system is second-order," a general second-order discrete-time transfer function would require **4 adjustable parameters** ($a_1, a_2, b_1, b_2$).
> - **The Price Paid**: The 2-parameter predictor `(4.79)` is a much more complicated, nonlinear function of $\theta = [\tau \;\; \beta]^T$ (via the exponentials $e^{-T/\tau}$ in `(4.77)`) than a 4-parameter ARX or OE model.

### 4.3.4 Standard Discrete-Time State-Space Notation ($T = 1$)

For notational simplicity across the rest of Section 4.3, we normalize the sampling interval to $T = 1$, drop the subscript $T$, and allow the output observation matrix $H = C(\theta)$ to depend on $\theta$. Equations `(4.67)` and `(4.65)` then take the standard discrete-time form:

$$
x(t + 1) = A(\theta)x(t) + B(\theta)u(t) \tag{4.80a}$$
$$

y(t) = C(\theta)x(t) + v(t) \tag{4.80b}$$

which corresponds to the input-output transfer representation:

$$
y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + v(t) \tag{4.81}$$
$$

G(q, \theta) = C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1} B(\theta) \tag{4.82}$$

Note that `(4.80)` can either be obtained by sampling a continuous-time model via `(4.68)` or be posed directly in discrete time with $A(\theta), B(\theta), C(\theta)$ parametrized directly in terms of $\theta$.

### 4.3.5 Noise Representation and the Time-Invariant Kalman Filter

How should we model the disturbance properties in `(4.80)`–`(4.81)`?
- **Approach 1 (Output Noise Filter)**: Postulate directly that the lumped output disturbance $v(t)$ is generated by filtering white noise $e(t)$ (with covariance $\Lambda$) through a noise transfer matrix $H(q, \theta)$:
  $$v(t) = H(q, \theta)e(t) \tag{4.83}$$
- **Approach 2 (State-Space Process & Measurement Noise)**: In state-space descriptions, it is much more natural to split the disturbances into **process noise** $w(t)$ acting directly on the state transition (e.g., the load torque variation $T_\ell(t)$ in Example 4.1) and **measurement noise** $v(t)$ acting on the sensors (e.g., potentiometer inaccuracy):

$$
x(t + 1) = A(\theta)x(t) + B(\theta)u(t) + w(t) \tag{4.84a}$$
$$

y(t) = C(\theta)x(t) + v(t) \tag{4.84b}$$

> [!important] Assumptions on Process and Measurement Noise
> In `(4.84)`, $\{w(t)\}$ and $\{v(t)\}$ are assumed to be sequences of independent random vectors (white noises) with zero mean and covariance/cross-covariance matrices:
>
> $$
> E w(t)w^T(t) = R_1(\theta)$$
> $$
>E v(t)v^T(t) = R_2(\theta) \tag{4.85}$$
>
> $$
> E w(t)v^T(t) = R_{12}(\theta)$$
>
> *(If physical disturbances such as $T_\ell(t)$ are colored rather than white, they can still be brought into the white-noise framework of `(4.84)`–`(4.85)` by modeling their coloring dynamics and augmenting the state vector $x(t)$—see Problem 4G.2).*
>
> #### The Time-Invariant (Steady-State) Kalman Filter
> Given infinite past data $y(s), u(s)$ for $s \le t - 1$ (from $s = -\infty$ up to $t-1$), and assuming $v$ and $w$ are Gaussian processes, the conditional expectation of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ is given by the **steady-state Kalman filter**:
> $$
>\hat{x}(t + 1, \theta) = A(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) + B(\theta)u(t) + K(\theta)\big[y(t) - C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta)\big] \tag{4.86a}$$

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) \tag{4.86b}$$

Here, the steady-state **Kalman gain matrix** $K(\theta)$ is computed as:
$$

K(\theta) = \big[A(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)C^T(\theta) + R_{12}(\theta)\big]\big[C(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)C^T(\theta) + R_2(\theta)\big]^{-1} \tag{4.87a}$$

where $\overline{P}(\theta)$ is obtained as the positive semidefinite solution ($\overline{P}(\theta) \ge 0$) of the **stationary algebraic Riccati equation (ARE)**:

$$
\overline{P}(\theta) = A(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)A^T(\theta) + R_1(\theta) - \big[A(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)C^T(\theta) + R_{12}(\theta)\big]\big[C(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)C^T(\theta) + R_2(\theta)\big]^{-1}\big[A(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)C^T(\theta) + R_{12}(\theta)\big]^T \tag{4.87b}$$

Physically and statistically, $\overline{P}(\theta)$ is the steady-state **covariance matrix of the state estimation error** $\tilde{x}(t, \theta) = x(t) - \hat{x}(t, \theta)$:
$$

\overline{P}(\theta) = E\big[x(t) - \hat{x}(t, \theta)\big]\big[x(t) - \hat{x}(t, \theta)\big]^T \tag{4.89}$$

#### Operator Form of the Kalman Predictor Filter
Rewriting `(4.86a)` using the forward shift operator $q$:

$$
\big[qI - A(\theta) + K(\theta)C(\theta)\big]\hat{x}(t, \theta) = B(\theta)u(t) + K(\theta)y(t)$$

and multiplying on the left by $C(\theta)\big[qI - A(\theta) + K(\theta)C(\theta)\big]^{-1}$ gives the explicit linear predictor filter mapping past inputs and outputs to $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$:
$$

\hat{y}(t|\theta) = C(\theta)\big[qI - A(\theta) + K(\theta)C(\theta)\big]^{-1} B(\theta)u(t) + C(\theta)\big[qI - A(\theta) + K(\theta)C(\theta)\big]^{-1} K(\theta)y(t) \tag{4.88}$$

*(Notice that stability of the predictor filter `(4.88)` requires the closed-loop observer matrix $A(\theta) - K(\theta)C(\theta)$ to have all eigenvalues strictly inside the unit circle).*

### 4.3.6 Innovations Representation & Directly Parametrized Innovations Form

Consider the one-step-ahead prediction error in `(4.86)`:

$$
e(t) \triangleq y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta) = y(t) - C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) = C(\theta)\big[x(t) - \hat{x}(t, \theta)\big] + v(t) \tag{4.90}$$

This quantity $e(t)$ represents the part of $y(t)$ that cannot be predicted from past data—the **innovation** at time $t$ (as introduced in Section 3.2, Equation `(3.25)`). Substituting $e(t) = y(t) - C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta)$ directly into the Kalman filter equations `(4.86)` yields the **innovations form** of the state-space description:
$$

\hat{x}(t + 1, \theta) = A(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) + B(\theta)u(t) + K(\theta)e(t) \tag{4.91a}$$

$$
y(t) = C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) + e(t)$$

From `(4.90)` and `(4.89)` (noting that the state estimation error $x(t) - \hat{x}(t, \theta)$ depends only on noises up to $t-1$ and is therefore uncorrelated with the white measurement noise $v(t)$ at time $t$), the covariance matrix $\Lambda(\theta)$ of the innovation sequence $\{e(t)\}$ is immediately obtained as:
$$

E e(t)e^T(t) = \Lambda(\theta) = C(\theta)\overline{P}(\theta)C^T(\theta) + R_2(\theta) \tag{4.91b}$$

#### Equivalence to the General Input-Output Model Structure `(4.4)`
Applying the shift operator $q$ to `(4.91a)` gives $\hat{x}(t, \theta) = [qI - A(\theta)]^{-1}[B(\theta)u(t) + K(\theta)e(t)]$. Substituting this into $y(t) = C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) + e(t)$ yields:

$$
y(t) = G(q, \theta)u(t) + H(q, \theta)e(t) \tag{4.92a}$$

where the deterministic transfer matrix $G(q, \theta)$ and the monic noise transfer matrix $H(q, \theta)$ are:
$$

G(q, \theta) = C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1} B(\theta) \tag{4.92b}$$

>
> $$
> H(q, \theta) = C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1} K(\theta) + I$$
>
> This establishes the exact equivalence between the state-space model `(4.84)` and the general linear model structure `(4.4)` (or `(4.83)`).
>
> #### Directly Parametrized Innovations Form
> In `(4.91)`, if we parametrize the noise covariance matrices $R_1(\theta), R_2(\theta), R_{12}(\theta)$, computing $K(\theta)$ for every candidate parameter vector $\theta$ during numerical optimization requires solving the algebraic Riccati equation `(4.87)`.
>
> Instead, we can sidestep `(4.85)` and `(4.87)` entirely by **parametrizing $K(\theta)$ directly** in terms of $\theta$ in `(4.91a)`. This is called a **directly parametrized innovations form**.
>
> [!tip] Parameter Counting: When to Parametrize $R_1, R_2, R_{12}$ vs. $K(\theta)$ Directly?
> Let $\dim x = n$ and $\dim y = p$:
> - **Full symmetric covariance matrices $R_1, R_2, R_{12}$**: Contain $\frac{1}{2}n(n+1) + np + \frac{1}{2}p(p+1)$ independent matrix elements.
> - **Kalman gain matrix $K(\theta)$**: Contains only $np$ matrix elements! (And $\Lambda(\theta)$ adds $\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)$ elements, though $\Lambda$ does not affect the predictor `(4.88)`).
> - **Rule of Thumb**:
> 1. **No prior physical knowledge of noise covariances**: Directly parametrizing $K(\theta)$ is far superior—it requires fewer parameters ($\dim \theta$ is smaller) and avoids solving the Riccati equation `(4.87b)`.
> 2. **Strong physical insight into the noise mechanism**: For example, if we know physically that process noise enters only a single state variable and is independent of measurement noise ($R_{12}=0$) with known sensor variance $R_2$, then parametrizing $R_1(\theta)$ via `(4.85)` and `(4.87)` can require *fewer* parameters than the $np$ elements of $K(\theta)$.
>
> ---
>
> ### 4.3.7 Example 4.2: Companion Form (Observer Canonical Form) Parametrizations
>
> Directly parametrized innovations forms also encompass black-box models that map directly onto the polynomial structures of Section 4.2. Consider a 3rd-order SISO system ($n=3, m=1, p=1$) in `(4.91a)` with parameter vector:
> $$
>\theta^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 & b_1 & b_2 & b_3 & k_1 & k_2 & k_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

and matrices chosen in **companion form** (specifically **observer canonical form**):

$$
A(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} -a_1 & 1 & 0 \\ -a_2 & 0 & 1 \\ -a_3 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad K(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} k_1 \\ k_2 \\ k_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

#### Step-by-Step Verification of Transfer Functions
Let us write out the state equations $\hat{x}(t+1) = A\hat{x}(t) + Bu(t) + Ke(t)$ component by component using the shift operator $q$:
1. State 3: $q\hat{x}_3(t) = -a_3 \hat{x}_1(t) + b_3 u(t) + k_3 e(t) \implies \hat{x}_3(t) = -a_3 q^{-1}\hat{x}_1(t) + b_3 q^{-1}u(t) + k_3 q^{-1}e(t)$.
2. State 2: $q\hat{x}_2(t) = -a_2 \hat{x}_1(t) + \hat{x}_3(t) + b_2 u(t) + k_2 e(t) \implies \hat{x}_2(t) = -(a_2 q^{-1} + a_3 q^{-2})\hat{x}_1(t) + (b_2 q^{-1} + b_3 q^{-2})u(t) + (k_2 q^{-1} + k_3 q^{-2})e(t)$.
3. State 1: $q\hat{x}_1(t) = -a_1 \hat{x}_1(t) + \hat{x}_2(t) + b_1 u(t) + k_1 e(t)$. Substituting $\hat{x}_2(t)$ and collecting terms in $\hat{x}_1(t) = C(\theta)\hat{x}(t)$ gives:
   $$\big(1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2} + a_3 q^{-3}\big)\hat{x}_1(t) = \big(b_1 q^{-1} + b_2 q^{-2} + b_3 q^{-3}\big)u(t) + \big(k_1 q^{-1} + k_2 q^{-2} + k_3 q^{-3}\big)e(t)$$

Therefore, the transfer functions in `(4.92b)` are immediately verified to be:
$$

G(q, \theta) = C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1} B(\theta) = \frac{b_1 q^{-1} + b_2 q^{-2} + b_3 q^{-3}}{1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2} + a_3 q^{-3}}$$

and

$$
C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1} K(\theta) = \frac{k_1 q^{-1} + k_2 q^{-2} + k_3 q^{-3}}{1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2} + a_3 q^{-3}}$$

Adding $1$ to form $H(q, \theta) = 1 + C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1}K(\theta)$ yields:
$$

1 + C(\theta)[qI - A(\theta)]^{-1} K(\theta) = \frac{1 + (a_1 + k_1)q^{-1} + (a_2 + k_2)q^{-2} + (a_3 + k_3)q^{-3}}{1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2} + a_3 q^{-3}} = \frac{1 + c_1 q^{-1} + c_2 q^{-2} + c_3 q^{-3}}{1 + a_1 q^{-1} + a_2 q^{-2} + a_3 q^{-3}}$$

with

$$
c_i \triangleq a_i + k_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, 3$$

Thus, the observer canonical innovations model is an exact parametrization of the **ARMAX model** (`(4.15)`–`(4.16)`) for $n_a = n_b = n_c = 3$. Furthermore, in state-space form we are not forced to use the numerically ill-conditioned companion matrix—we can choose better-conditioned state-space bases (see Problem 16E.1) and systematically extend canonical parametrizations to multi-output (MIMO) systems (Appendix 4A).

### 4.3.8 Time-Varying Predictors (*)

The steady-state predictor `(4.86)`–`(4.87)` assumes data from $t = -\infty$ are available. In practice, measurements start at $t = 0$.
- **Suboptimal Zero-Initialization**: As in Section 3.2, we could simply initialize the steady-state filter `(4.86)` at $t = 0$ with $\hat{x}(0) = 0$.
- **Exact Finite-History Treatment via the Time-Varying Kalman Filter**: A major advantage of the state-space formulation is that incomplete prior information before $t = 0$ can be handled **exact-optimally** by specifying an initial state estimate $\hat{x}(0, \theta) = x_0(\theta)$ and its initial error covariance matrix:
$$

\Pi_0(\theta) = E\big[x(0) - x_0(\theta)\big]\big[x(0) - x_0(\theta)\big]^T \tag{4.93}$$

Then the time-varying Kalman filter provides the exact one-step-ahead predictor for $t \ge 0$:

$$
\hat{x}(t + 1, \theta) = A(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta) + B(\theta)u(t) + K(t, \theta)\big[y(t) - C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta)\big] \tag{4.94}$$
$$

\hat{y}(t|\theta) = C(\theta)\hat{x}(t, \theta), \qquad \hat{x}(0, \theta) = x_0(\theta)$$

where the time-varying Kalman gain $K(t, \theta)$ and error covariance $P(t, \theta)$ evolve via the **dynamic Riccati difference equation**:

$$
K(t, \theta) = \big[A(\theta)P(t, \theta)C^T(\theta) + R_{12}(\theta)\big]\big[C(\theta)P(t, \theta)C^T(\theta) + R_2(\theta)\big]^{-1} \tag{4.95a}$$
$$

P(t + 1, \theta) = A(\theta)P(t, \theta)A^T(\theta) + R_1(\theta) - K(t, \theta)\big[C(\theta)P(t, \theta)C^T(\theta) + R_2(\theta)\big]K^T(t, \theta), \qquad P(0, \theta) = \Pi_0(\theta) \tag{4.95b}$$

> [!note] Rapid Convergence & Short Data Records
> Under general detectability/stabilizability conditions, $P(t, \theta)$ and $K(t, \theta)$ in `(4.95)` converge rapidly to their steady-state values $\overline{P}(\theta)$ and $K(\theta)$ in `(4.87)`.
> - For **long data records**, using the steady-state filter `(4.86)`–`(4.87)` directly is completely reasonable and computationally simpler. Notice that `(4.86)`–`(4.87)` is simply the special case of `(4.94)`–`(4.95)` when initialized at the stationary distribution $\Pi_0(\theta) = \overline{P}(\theta)$ with $x_0(\theta) = 0$.
> - For **short data records** where initial transients cannot be neglected, `(4.93)`–`(4.95)` provides an exact treatment and even allows $x_0(\theta)$ and $\Pi_0(\theta)$ to be included in the parameter vector $\theta$.

### 4.3.9 Sampling Continuous-Time Process Noise (*)

Just as physical insight gives the deterministic dynamics in continuous time (`(4.62)`), we often have physical insight into how process disturbances enter in continuous time:

$$
\dot{x}(t) = F(\theta)x(t) + G(\theta)u(t) + \overline{w}(t) \tag{4.96}$$

where $\overline{w}(t)$ is **continuous-time formal white noise** (the formal derivative of a Wiener / Brownian motion process) with covariance function:
$$

E\overline{w}(t)\overline{w}^T(s) = \overline{R}_1(\theta)\delta(t - s) \tag{4.97}$$

Here $\delta(t - s)$ is Dirac's delta function and $\overline{R}_1(\theta) \ge 0$ is the continuous-time intensity (spectral density) matrix.

#### Derivation of the Sampled Process Noise Covariance $R_1(\theta)$
When the input $u(t)$ is piecewise constant over $[kT, kT + T)$ as in `(4.66)`, integrating `(4.96)` from $kT$ to $kT + T$ yields the discrete-time state equation:

$$
x(kT + T) = A_T(\theta)x(kT) + B_T(\theta)u(kT) + w_T(kT) \tag{4.98}$$

where $A_T(\theta)$ and $B_T(\theta)$ are given by `(4.68)`, and the sampled process noise vector is the stochastic integral:
$$

w_T(kT) = \int_{kT}^{kT+T} e^{F(\theta)(kT + T - t)}\overline{w}(t)\,dt$$

Because $\overline{w}(t)$ on distinct sampling intervals $[kT, kT+T)$ are independent, $\{w_T(kT)\}$ ($k = 1, 2, \dots$) is a sequence of independent zero-mean random vectors with covariance matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E w_T(kT)w_T^T(kT) &= \int_{kT}^{kT+T} \int_{kT}^{kT+T} e^{F(\theta)(kT+T-t)} \underbrace{E\overline{w}(t)\overline{w}^T(s)}_{\overline{R}_1(\theta)\delta(t-s)} e^{F^T(\theta)(kT+T-s)}\,dt\,ds \\
&= \int_{kT}^{kT+T} e^{F(\theta)(kT+T-t)} \overline{R}_1(\theta) e^{F^T(\theta)(kT+T-t)}\,dt
\end{aligned}$$

Substituting $\tau = kT + T - t$ gives the exact sampled process noise covariance matrix:
$$

E w_T(kT)w_T^T(kT) = R_1(\theta) = \int_{0}^{T} e^{F(\theta)\tau} \overline{R}_1(\theta) e^{F^T(\theta)\tau}\,d\tau \tag{4.99}$$

*(See Åström, 1970, for a rigorous stochastic differential equation derivation).*

### 4.3.10 Summary: The Spectrum of State-Space Modeling Possibilities

Section 4.3 concludes by emphasizing that state-space models offer a continuous **spectrum of modeling possibilities** spanning white/grey-box physical models to black-box structures:
1. **Purely Physical Continuous-Time Dynamics + Physical Continuous-Time Noise**: Model $F(\theta), G(\theta)$ from physics (`(4.62)`), model $\overline{R}_1(\theta)$ in continuous time (`(4.96)`–`(4.97)`), sample via `(4.68)` and `(4.99)`, and compute the Kalman gain via the Riccati equation `(4.87)`.
2. **Physical Continuous-Time Dynamics + Black-Box Innovations Noise**: Model $F(\theta), G(\theta)$ from physical insight and sample via `(4.68)` to obtain $A(\theta), B(\theta)$, but combine this with a directly parametrized black-box Kalman gain $K(\theta)$ in the innovations form `(4.91)`.
3. **Purely Black-Box State-Space Structures**: Parametrize $A(\theta), B(\theta), C(\theta), K(\theta)$ directly in discrete time (such as the companion/observer canonical form of Example 4.2), which reproduces input-output black-box structures like ARMAX while offering greater numerical flexibility (choice of state coordinates to improve numerical conditioning, Problem 16E.1) and natural MIMO extensions.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1: How a Single Noise Source $e(t)$ Replaces Two Noise Sources $w(t)$ and $v(t)$**:
>   In `(4.84)`, the system is driven by *two* distinct random vectors: an $n$-dimensional process noise $w(t)$ and a $p$-dimensional measurement noise $v(t)$. Yet in the innovations representation `(4.91a)`, the state $\hat{x}(t+1, \theta)$ and output $y(t)$ are driven by a *single* $p$-dimensional white noise process—the innovation $e(t)$! Do not think this is an approximation: as far as the second-order statistics (spectra/covariances) of the output $y(t)$ and the optimal linear predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ are concerned, the innovations representation `(4.91)` is **100% statistically equivalent** to `(4.84)`.
> - **Leap 2: Even Diagonal Continuous-Time Noise $\overline{R}_1(\theta)$ Becomes Full Sampled Noise $R_1(\theta)$**:
>   In Example 4.1 (`(4.75)`), a random load torque $T_\ell(t)$ enters *only* the second state equation (angular acceleration), so $\overline{R}_1(\theta)$ has a non-zero entry only in position $(2,2)$. However, when integrated over a sampling interval $T$ via `(4.99)`, the matrix exponential $e^{F(\theta)\tau}$ couples velocity into position, making the discrete-time covariance matrix $R_1(\theta)$ a **full $2 \times 2$ matrix**!
> - **Notation Trap 1: $v(t)$ as Lumped Output Disturbance vs. Sensor Measurement Noise**:
>   In Equations `(4.65)`, `(4.72)`, and `(4.80b)`–`(4.83)`, $v(t)$ denotes the **total lumped disturbance** at the output (so $v(t) = H(q, \theta)e(t)$ can be colored). Starting at Equation `(4.84)`, when process noise $w(t)$ is introduced explicitly into the state equation, $v(t)$ is restricted to mean **white measurement noise** with covariance $R_2(\theta)$. Always check whether $w(t)$ is present in the equation to know which role $v(t)$ is playing!
> - **Notation Trap 2: Drop of the Sampling Period Index $T$ at Equation `(4.80)`**:
>   Up to Equation `(4.79)`, Ljung writes $A_T(\theta), B_T(\theta), G_T(q, \theta)$ and $t = kT$ to emphasize the dependence on the sampling interval $T$. From `(4.80)` through `(4.95)`, he sets $T = 1$ and writes $A(\theta), B(\theta), G(q, \theta)$ with integer $t$, before briefly returning to $A_T(\theta), B_T(\theta), w_T(kT)$ in `(4.98)`–`(4.99)` when discussing continuous-time noise sampling.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *Why does physical grey-box modeling in `(4.79)` yield lower parameter variance than a black-box second-order OE/ARX model, and what computational price do we pay for it during numerical optimization?*
>   2. *Why is $c_i = a_i + k_i$ in Example 4.2, and how does setting $K(\theta) = 0$ specialize the innovations form `(4.91)` to an Output-Error (OE) model?*
