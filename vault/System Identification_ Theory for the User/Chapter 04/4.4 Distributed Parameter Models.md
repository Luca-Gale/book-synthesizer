# 4.4 Distributed Parameter Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 4**: This section (*) extends the finite-dimensional continuous-time state-space modeling framework of Section 4.3 to infinite-dimensional systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs), showing that linear time-invariant distributed parameter systems still fit directly into the general parametrized transfer-function model structure (4.4) via either early spatial discretization ("lumping") or exact Laplace-domain analytical solution.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Up to Section 4.3, every mechanistic state-space model we constructed assumed that the internal state of the physical plant at time $t$ could be summarized by a finite-dimensional column vector $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ governed by ordinary differential equations (ODEs). However, many real-world physical processes—such as thermal conduction along a metal bar, acoustic wave propagation in a duct, fluid flow in a long pipeline, or flexible beam vibrations—do not have their energy storage concentrated at discrete points. Instead, their physical state varies continuously across both **time** $t$ and **spatial coordinates** $\xi$.

Models that directly or indirectly involve **partial differential equations (PDEs)** to relate the input signal to the output signal are called **distributed parameter models**:
- Why **"distributed"**? Because at any fixed time instant $t$, the internal state $x(t, \cdot)$ is distributed continuously over space and therefore belongs to an **infinite-dimensional function space** (e.g., the space of smooth temperature profiles along a rod of length $L$) rather than the finite-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

When building a parametrized model from a linear, time-invariant (LTI) PDE for system identification, Ljung highlights **two fundamental modeling strategies**:

1. **Strategy 1 — Early Spatial Discretization ("Lumping" into an ODE State-Space Model)**:
   - Replace the spatial partial derivatives (e.g., $\partial^2 / \partial \xi^2$) with finite-difference approximations on a spatial grid $\xi = k \cdot \Delta L$, or truncate an infinite spatial basis-function series expansion (e.g., Fourier/modal Galerkin truncation).
   - This approximates the PDE by a coupled system of **ordinary differential equations (ODEs)** in time. Because the continuously distributed states are grouped ("lumped") together into a finite collection of $n = L / \Delta L$ spatial compartments, the resulting model is a standard **lumped finite-dimensional state-space model** of the exact form studied in Section 4.3.

2. **Strategy 2 — Exact PDE Retention in the Laplace Domain (Late Numerical Approximation)**:
   - Stick to the exact PDE during the analytical modeling stage by taking the Laplace transform with respect to time $t$, which turns the PDE in $(t, \xi)$ into a boundary-value ordinary differential equation in the spatial variable $\xi$ (with $s$ acting as a parameter).
   - Solving this spatial boundary-value problem yields an exact continuous-time transfer function $G_c(s, \theta)$ from the boundary input $U(s)$ to the measured output $Y(s)$.
   - Crucially, even though the resulting transfer function $G_c(s, \theta)$ is **non-rational** (containing transcendental functions of $\sqrt{s}$, exponentials $e^{\pm L\sqrt{s/\kappa}}$, etc.), it remains an **analytic function of $s$** and fits seamlessly into our general LTI model structure (4.4) and continuous-time parametrization (4.49), delaying numerical approximations until the final computation stage.

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

| Symbol | Meaning / Physical Interpretation | Space / Dimensions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $t$ | Continuous time variable (or discrete sampling instants $t = 1, 2, \dots$) | $\mathbb{R}_+$ (or $\mathbb{Z}_+$) |
| $\xi$ | Spatial coordinate measuring distance from the insulated measured end of the rod | $[0, L] \subset \mathbb{R}$ |
| $L$ | Total physical length of the insulated metal rod | $\mathbb{R}_+$ (known constant) |
| $x(t, \xi)$ | Distributed internal state: temperature at time $t$ and spatial position $\xi$ | Function space on $[0, L]$ for each $t$ |
| $u(t)$ | External input signal: heating power applied at the far end ($\xi = L$) at time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}$ (scalar input) |
| $y(t)$ | Measured output signal: noisy temperature measured at the near end ($\xi = 0$) at $t = 1, 2, \dots$ | $\mathbb{R}$ (scalar output) |
| $v(t)$ | Measurement noise corrupting the sensor reading at $\xi = 0$ | $\mathbb{R}$ (stochastic process) |
| $\kappa$ | Coefficient of thermal conductivity (thermal diffusivity) of the rod | $\mathbb{R}_+$ (unknown physical parameter) |
| $K$ | Heat-transfer coefficient coupling heating power $u(t)$ into the spatial temperature gradient at $\xi = L$ | $\mathbb{R}_+$ (unknown physical parameter) |
| $\theta$ | Parameter vector collecting the unknown physical constants: $\theta = [\kappa \;\; K]^T$ | $\mathbb{R}^2$ |
| $\Delta L$ | Spatial grid step size used in the finite-difference ("lumped") approximation | $\mathbb{R}_+$ |
| $n$ | Order of the lumped finite-dimensional state-space approximation ($n = L / \Delta L$) | $\mathbb{Z}_+$ |
| $s$ | Laplace transform complex frequency variable (with respect to time $t$) | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| $X(s, \xi)$ | Laplace transform of $x(t, \xi)$ with respect to time $t$ at fixed spatial position $\xi$ | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| $X'(s, \xi), X''(s, \xi)$ | First and second partial derivatives of $X(s, \xi)$ with respect to the spatial variable $\xi$ | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| $U(s), Y(s), V(s)$ | Laplace transforms of the input $u(t)$, output $y(t)$, and measurement noise $v(t)$ | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| $A(s), B(s)$ | Integration "constants" (functions of $s$ independent of $\xi$) in the general spatial ODE solution | $\mathbb{C}$ |
| $G_c(s, \theta)$ | Exact continuous-time (non-rational analytic) transfer function from $U(s)$ to $X(s, 0)$ | $\mathbb{C}$ |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### Example 4.3: Heating Dynamics of an Insulated Metal Rod

To illustrate both modeling approaches concretely, consider the physical thermal conduction process depicted in **Figure 4.7**.

![[assets/fig_4_4_heat_rod_system.png]]

> [!note] How to Read This Diagram (Figure 4.7 — The Heat-Rod System)
> - **Physical Geometry & Coordinate Axis ($\xi$)**: A well-insulated metal rod of total length $L$ is parameterized by the spatial coordinate $\xi \in [0, L]$, with the arrow indicating increasing $\xi$ from the front-left measured end ($\xi = 0$) to the back-right heated end ($\xi = L$).
> - **Boundary Input at the Far End ($\xi = L$)**: A heat source (depicted as a candle/burner) applies heating power $u(t)$ at the far boundary $\xi = L$.
> - **Boundary Output at the Near End ($\xi = 0$)**: A thermometer inserted at the near boundary $\xi = 0$ measures the local temperature $y(t)$, which is sampled at discrete times $t = 1, 2, \dots$. All lateral surfaces and the near end $\xi = 0$ are thermally insulated so heat flows purely longitudinally along $\xi$.

##### 1. Governing PDE, Boundary Conditions, and Measurement Equation

Under ideal one-dimensional heat conduction conditions, let $x(t, \xi)$ denote the temperature at time $t$ and distance $\xi$ from the measured end of the rod. Inside the rod ($0 < \xi < L$), the temperature profile satisfies the **one-dimensional heat-diffusion partial differential equation**:

$$
\frac{\partial x(t, \xi)}{\partial t} = \kappa \frac{\partial^2 x(t, \xi)}{\partial \xi^2} \tag{4.100}
$$

where $\kappa > 0$ is the coefficient of thermal conductivity (thermal diffusivity). Because the PDE is second-order in space $\xi$, it requires two spatial boundary conditions (here, **Neumann boundary conditions** specifying spatial temperature gradients / heat fluxes at both ends):

1. **Far-End Heated Boundary ($\xi = L$)**: By Fourier's law of heat conduction, the inward heat flux at $\xi = L$ is proportional to the applied heating power $u(t)$:

$$
\left. \frac{\partial x(t, \xi)}{\partial \xi} \right|_{\xi = L} = K \cdot u(t) \tag{4.101}
$$

   where $K$ is a heat-transfer coefficient.
2. **Near-End Insulated Boundary ($\xi = 0$)**: Because no heat escapes through the insulated end at $\xi = 0$, the spatial temperature gradient there vanishes identically:

$$
\left. \frac{\partial x(t, \xi)}{\partial \xi} \right|_{\xi = 0} = 0 \tag{4.102}
$$

3. **Discrete-Time Sensor Measurement Equation**: The thermometer measures the temperature at the near end $\xi = 0$ at sampling instants $t = 1, 2, \dots$, corrupted by additive measurement noise $\{v(t)\}$:

$$
y(t) = x(t, 0) + v(t), \qquad t = 1, 2, \dots \tag{4.103}
$$

4. **Unknown Parameter Vector**: Assuming the rod length $L$ is known, the unknown physical constants to be estimated from input-output data are collected into the $2 \times 1$ parameter vector:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \kappa \\ K \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.104}
$$

---

##### 2. Strategy 1: Spatial Finite-Difference Approximation ("Lumping")

To convert the infinite-dimensional PDE (4.100) into a finite-dimensional state-space model of the type treated in Section 4.3, we divide the rod of length $L$ into $n = L / \Delta L$ spatial segments of width $\Delta L$ at grid points $\xi = k \cdot \Delta L$ ($k = 0, 1, \dots, n$). Replacing the second-order spatial derivative $\partial^2 x(t, \xi) / \partial \xi^2$ by its central second-order finite-difference quotient yields:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 x(t, \xi)}{\partial \xi^2} \approx \frac{x(t, \xi + \Delta L) - 2x(t, \xi) + x(t, \xi - \Delta L)}{(\Delta L)^2}, \qquad \xi = k \cdot \Delta L
$$

Substituting this approximation into the PDE (4.100) transforms the distributed system into a coupled system of $n = L / \Delta L$ ordinary differential equations in time $t$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} x(t, k \cdot \Delta L) = \frac{\kappa}{(\Delta L)^2} \Big[ x(t, (k+1)\Delta L) - 2x(t, k\Delta L) + x(t, (k-1)\Delta L) \Big]
$$

where each state variable $x(t, k \cdot \Delta L)$ serves as a **lumped representative** for the continuous temperature profile $x(t, \xi)$ over the spatial interval $k \cdot \Delta L \le \xi < (k+1) \cdot \Delta L$. While this finite-dimensional state-space approximation is often reasonable in practice, its state dimension $n = L / \Delta L$ grows large as $\Delta L \to 0$.

---

##### 3. Strategy 2: Exact Laplace-Domain Solution & Non-Rational Transfer Function

Instead of discretizing space early, we can retain the exact PDE (4.100) by applying the **Laplace transform with respect to time $t$** while holding the spatial coordinate $\xi$ fixed. Let $X(s, \xi) = \mathcal{L}\{x(t, \xi)\}$ and $U(s) = \mathcal{L}\{u(t)\}$ (assuming zero initial conditions $x(0, \xi) = 0$). Using prime ($'$) and double prime ($''$) to denote differentiation with respect to the spatial coordinate $\xi$, equations (4.100)–(4.102) transform into a second-order linear boundary-value ODE in $\xi$ parametrized by $s$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s X(s, \xi) &= \kappa X''(s, \xi) \\
X'(s, L) &= K \cdot U(s) \\
X'(s, 0) &= 0
\end{aligned} \tag{4.105}
$$

Let us solve the boundary-value problem (4.105) step by step for a fixed complex frequency $s$:

- **Step 1 — General Solution of the Spatial ODE**:
  Rewrite the differential equation in $\xi$ as $X''(s, \xi) - (s / \kappa) X(s, \xi) = 0$. Its characteristic equation in the spatial wavenumber $\lambda$ is $\lambda^2 - s/\kappa = 0$, which has roots $\lambda = \pm \sqrt{s / \kappa}$. Therefore, the general solution for $X(s, \xi)$ is a linear combination of spatial exponentials:

$$
X(s, \xi) = A(s) e^{-\xi \sqrt{s/\kappa}} + B(s) e^{\xi \sqrt{s/\kappa}}
$$

  where $A(s)$ and $B(s)$ are integration constants with respect to $\xi$ (i.e., functions of $s$ determined by the two boundary conditions).

- **Step 2 — Differentiate with Respect to $\xi$**:
  Taking the spatial derivative $X'(s, \xi) = \frac{\partial}{\partial \xi} X(s, \xi)$ gives:

$$
X'(s, \xi) = \sqrt{s/\kappa} \left( -A(s) e^{-\xi \sqrt{s/\kappa}} + B(s) e^{\xi \sqrt{s/\kappa}} \right)
$$

- **Step 3 — Apply the Insulated Boundary Condition at $\xi = 0$**:
  Evaluating $X'(s, \xi)$ at $\xi = 0$ and enforcing $X'(s, 0) = 0$ yields:

$$
X'(s, 0) = \sqrt{s/\kappa} \big( -A(s) + B(s) \big) = 0 \implies A(s) = B(s)
$$

- **Step 4 — Apply the Heated Boundary Condition at $\xi = L$**:
  Substituting $B(s) = A(s)$ into $X'(s, \xi)$ and evaluating at $\xi = L$ where $X'(s, L) = K \cdot U(s)$ gives:

$$
X'(s, L) = A(s) \sqrt{s/\kappa} \left( e^{L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} - e^{-L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} \right) = K \cdot U(s)
$$

  Solving for $A(s) = B(s)$ produces Equation (4.106):

$$
A(s) = B(s) = \frac{K \cdot U(s)}{\sqrt{s/\kappa} \left( e^{L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} - e^{-L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} \right)} \tag{4.106}
$$

- **Step 5 — Evaluate the Output at the Measured End $\xi = 0$**:
  At the thermometer location $\xi = 0$, the Laplace-transformed temperature is simply:

$$
X(s, 0) = A(s) e^{0} + B(s) e^{0} = A(s) + B(s) = 2 A(s)
$$

  Inserting (4.106) into the Laplace transform of the measurement equation (4.103), $Y(s) = X(s, 0) + V(s)$, gives the exact input-output transfer-function relationship:

$$
Y(s) = X(s, 0) + V(s) = G_c(s, \theta) U(s) + V(s) \tag{4.107}
$$

  where the continuous-time transfer function $G_c(s, \theta)$ is explicitly parameterized by $\theta = [\kappa \;\; K]^T$ as:

$$
G_c(s, \theta) = \frac{2K}{\sqrt{s/\kappa} \left( e^{L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} - e^{-L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} \right)} = \frac{K}{\sqrt{s/\kappa} \sinh\!\left(L\sqrt{s/\kappa}\right)} \tag{4.108}
$$

  *(Note: Using the hyperbolic sine identity $e^z - e^{-z} = 2\sinh(z)$, the denominator is nothing other than $2\sqrt{s/\kappa}\sinh(L\sqrt{s/\kappa})$.)*

- **Step 6 — Connection to the General Model Structure (4.4)**:
  Equation (4.107)–(4.108) is a continuous-time transfer-function parametrization of the exact kind introduced in (4.49). Combined with an appropriate discrete-time sampling procedure (as in Section 4.3, Eqs. (4.86)–(4.92) or frequency-domain evaluations) and a noise model for $\{v(t)\}$, it reduces directly to the standard discrete-time LTI model structure (4.4). Even though $G_c(s, \theta)$ is **not a rational function of $s$** (it cannot be written as a ratio of finite-degree polynomials in $s$), it is an **analytic function of $s$** (Notice that the apparent square-root branch point $\sqrt{s/\kappa}$ cancels out in power-series expansion because $\sqrt{z}\sinh(L\sqrt{z}) = Lz + \frac{L^3}{6}z^2 + \dots$ is an even function of $\sqrt{z}$, hence an entire function of $s$!). Consequently, all standard system-theoretic concepts—poles, zeros, frequency response $G_c(i\omega, \theta)$, and stability—still apply directly.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Leap 1 — Non-Rational yet Analytic Transfer Functions Fit into (4.4)**: A common misconception is that transfer-function models in system identification must be rational ratios of polynomials $B(s)/A(s)$. Example 4.3 demonstrates that exact distributed-parameter transfer functions like (4.108) are transcendental (non-rational) functions of $s$, yet because $\sqrt{s/\kappa}(e^{L\sqrt{s/\kappa}} - e^{-L\sqrt{s/\kappa}})$ is analytic in $s$ (in fact, expandable as a power series in $s$ with an infinite number of poles along the negative real axis at $s_k = -\kappa (k\pi / L)^2$), the general framework of Chapter 4 accommodates them without any structural modification.
> - **Leap 2 — Two-Parameter Parsimony vs. High-Order Lumping**: Notice that whether you discretize the rod into $n = 100$ spatial compartments (Strategy 1) or solve the PDE exactly in the Laplace domain (Strategy 2), the unknown parameter vector remains just $\theta = [\kappa \;\; K]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$! A black-box high-order ARX/OE approximation of a distributed system might require dozens of unstructured polynomial coefficients, whereas physical PDE modeling captures the entire infinite-dimensional dynamics with only two physical parameters.
> - **Notation Trap — Primes Denote Spatial Derivatives, Not Time Derivatives**: In Equation (4.105), $X'(s, \xi)$ and $X''(s, \xi)$ denote partial derivatives with respect to the **spatial coordinate $\xi$** ($\partial X / \partial \xi$ and $\partial^2 X / \partial \xi^2$), whereas differentiation with respect to **time $t$** has already been converted into algebraic multiplication by the Laplace variable $s$.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why does the insulated boundary condition at $\xi = 0$ ($X'(s, 0) = 0$) immediately force the forward and backward spatial exponential waves $A(s)$ and $B(s)$ to have equal amplitudes ($A(s) = B(s)$)?
>   2. When would you prefer Strategy 1 (spatial finite differences / Galerkin basis truncation) over Strategy 2 (exact Laplace transfer function)? *(Hint: Think about spatially varying coefficients $\kappa(\xi)$ or nonlinear PDEs where closed-form Laplace solutions like (4.108) do not exist, which is why the literature cited on p. 105—Banks, Crowley, and Kunisch (1983) and Kubrusly (1977)—focuses heavily on basis functions and computational approximation.)*
