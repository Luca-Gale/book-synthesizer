# 4.6 Identifiability of Some Model Structures

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 4**: Section 4.6 applies the abstract identifiability framework of Section 4.5 (Definition 4.6 and Equation (4.116)) to concrete model structures—evaluating continuous-time physical parametrizations, deriving exact polynomial coprimeness and order-excess conditions for the general SISO black-box transfer-function family (Theorems 4.1 and 4.2), and establishing the controllability condition for SISO state-space models in observer canonical form.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

- **From Abstract Set Equivalence to Concrete Algebraic Tests**:
  In Section 4.5, identifiability was defined in terms of the parameter-to-model mapping $\theta \mapsto \mathcal{M}(\theta)$. Two parameter vectors $\theta$ and $\theta^*$ yield identical input-output and noise transfer functions if and only if $G(z,\theta) \equiv G(z,\theta^*)$ and $H(z,\theta) \equiv H(z,\theta^*)$ for almost all $z$. Section 4.6 answers the practical engineering question: *Given a specific model structure, under what conditions does transfer-function equality uniquely pin down $\theta = \theta^*$?*

- **Physical Continuous-Time Parametrizations & Sampling Ambiguities**:
  1. When modeling physical processes in continuous time, parameters often appear in coupled combinations (such as ratios or products of physical constants like gain-to-time-constant $K/\tau$, as seen earlier in the DC-motor model of Example 4.3). Testing whether $G_c(s,\theta) = G_c(s,\theta^*) \Rightarrow \theta = \theta^*$ requires solving nonlinear algebraic equations relating the physical constants to the rational transfer-function coefficients.
  2. Furthermore, testing continuous-time transfer function identity $G_c(s,\theta) = G_c(s,\theta^*)$ is **neither strictly sufficient nor strictly necessary** for discrete-time identifiability of the sampled model:
     - *Not sufficient*: Sampling $G_c$ with sample interval $T$ can introduce aliasing/sampling ambiguities where two distinct continuous-time systems $G_c(s,\theta_1) \neq G_c(s,\theta_2)$ produce the exact same discrete-time pulse transfer function $G_T(z)$ (unless $T$ is chosen carefully).
     - *Not necessary*: A $\theta$-parametrized noise model $H(z,\theta)$ might carry extra information about the physical parameters $\theta$ that resolves ambiguities left open by $G_c(s,\theta)$ alone—though in most engineering applications, noise characteristics rarely provide reliable information about physical plant constants.

- **Polynomial Coprimeness in Black-Box SISO Models (Theorems 4.1 & 4.2)**:
  For the general SISO polynomial model $A(q)y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t)$, non-uniqueness of $\theta^*$ arises if and only if one can multiply and cancel a non-constant common polynomial factor (or shift a factor between $A(q)$ and $F(q), D(q)$) without altering the overall transfer functions $G(z,\theta) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)F(z)}$ and $H(z,\theta) = \frac{C(z)}{A(z)D(z)}$ or exceeding the prescribed polynomial degrees:
  1. **Direct pole-zero cancellations**: Common factors across $(z^{n_a}A^*, z^{n_b}B^*, z^{n_c}C^*)$, between $(z^{n_b}B^*, z^{n_f}F^*)$, or between $(z^{n_c}C^*, z^{n_d}D^*)$.
  2. **Factor trading between $A(q)$ and $(F(q), D(q))$**:
     - If $n_a \ge 1$, a common factor shared by $z^{n_f}F^*$ and $z^{n_d}D^*$ can be pulled out of both denominators and absorbed into $z^{n_a}A^*$.
     - If $n_d \ge 1$, a common factor shared by $z^{n_a}A^*$ and $z^{n_b}B^*$ can be cancelled from $G = B/(AF)$ and pushed into $z^{n_d}D^*$ in $H = C/(AD)$.
     - If $n_f \ge 1$, a common factor shared by $z^{n_a}A^*$ and $z^{n_c}C^*$ can be cancelled from $H = C/(AD)$ and pushed into $z^{n_f}F^*$ in $G = B/(AF)$.
  When searching for an unknown "true" system $\mathcal{S}$ of minimal orders $(n_a^0, n_b^0, n_c^0, n_d^0, n_f^0)$, **Theorem 4.2** translates these six coprimeness conditions into six **order-excess conditions** (e.g., $\min(n_a - n_a^0, n_b - n_b^0, n_c - n_c^0) = 0$). This proves we do not need to search over all multi-dimensional order combinations simultaneously—incrementing a single common order $n$ one unit at a time in an ARMAX structure $\mathcal{M}_n$ is guaranteed to hit a globally identifiable representation of $\mathcal{S}$ as soon as $n = \max(n_a^0, n_b^0, n_c^0)$!

- **Parameter Counting and Canonical Forms in SISO State-Space Models**:
  An $n$-dimensional linear SISO system in innovations form has transfer functions $G(z)$ and $H(z)$ sharing a common monic $n$-th order denominator polynomial ($n$ parameters) and two $(n-1)$-th order numerator polynomials ($2n$ parameters, assuming zero direct feedthrough and monic innovation), totaling **$3n$ input-output parameters**. Consequently, a fully populated state-space parameterization $(A, B, C, K)$ with $n^2 + 3n$ entries is massively over-parametrized due to continuous similarity transformations $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. By fixing the basis via the **observer canonical form** (which has exactly $3n$ free parameters and is observable by construction), global and local identifiability at $\theta^*$ holds if and only if the two-input pair $\{A(\theta^*), [B(\theta^*)\;\; K(\theta^*)]\}$ is **controllable** (guaranteeing minimal state dimension $n$ and no pole-zero cancellations across all three polynomials).

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

### 4.6.1 Fundamental Global Identifiability Criterion

Recall from Definition 4.6 (Section 4.5) and the transfer-function uniqueness condition (4.116) that a model structure $\mathcal{M}$ is **globally identifiable at $\theta^*$** if and only if

$$
G(z,\theta) \equiv G(z,\theta^*) \quad \text{and} \quad H(z,\theta) \equiv H(z,\theta^*) \quad \text{for almost all } z \implies \theta = \theta^* \tag{4.136}
$$

- **Local Identifiability**: If condition (4.136) is required to hold only when $\theta$ is restricted to a sufficiently small open neighborhood of $\theta^*$, the structure $\mathcal{M}$ is **locally identifiable at $\theta^*$** (which can be tested via the Jacobian rank condition of Problem 4G.4).
- **Strict Global Identifiability vs. Global Identifiability**: As established in Definition 4.6, $\mathcal{M}$ is *strictly globally identifiable* if (4.136) holds for **all** $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$, whereas it is *globally identifiable* if (4.136) holds for **almost all** $\theta^* \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ (i.e., everywhere except possibly on lower-dimensional hyper-surfaces of measure zero in $\mathbb{R}^d$).

---

### 4.6.2 Parametrizations in Terms of Physical Parameters

When modeling a physical system from first principles (Equations (4.62)–(4.65) with unit sampling $T = 1$), we begin with a continuous-time input-output model:

$$
y(t) = G_c(p,\theta)u(t) + v(t) \tag{4.137}
$$

To test identifiability rigorously via (4.136), one should sample $G_c(p,\theta)$ with the chosen sampling interval $T$ to obtain the discrete-time transfer function $G_T(z,\theta)$ and append a discrete-time noise model $H(z,\theta)$. However, in practice, one instead examines the continuous-time transfer function directly via the test:

$$
G_c(s,\theta) = G_c(s,\theta^*) \quad \text{for almost all } s \implies \theta = \theta^*\text{?} \tag{4.138}
$$

> [!important] Logical Relationship Between Continuous-Time Test (4.138) and Discrete-Time Identifiability (4.136)
> Equation (4.138) is **not identical** to (4.136):
> 1. **Why (4.138) is NOT strictly sufficient for (4.136)**: When sampling $G_c(s,\theta)$, aliasing ambiguities can occur—two distinct continuous-time transfer functions $G_c(s,\theta) \neq G_c(s,\theta^*)$ can fold into the exact same sampled transfer function $G_T(z,\theta) \equiv G_T(z,\theta^*)$ (cf. Equation (2.24)). However, if the sampling interval $T$ is chosen carefully (avoiding pathological pole imaginary parts that differ by multiples of $2\pi/T$), this sampling ambiguity does not cause practical problems.
> 2. **Why (4.138) is NOT strictly necessary for (4.136)**: If the noise model $H(z,\theta)$ is also parametrized by the physical vector $\theta$, matching $H(z,\theta) \equiv H(z,\theta^*)$ in (4.136) provides additional algebraic equations that could resolve parameter ambiguities left unresolved by $G_c(s,\theta) = G_c(s,\theta^*)$ alone. In most practical applications, however, disturbance characteristics are not known precisely enough to carry reliable information about physical plant constants.
> 3. **Practical Conclusion**: Despite these two theoretical caveats, (4.138) serves as the standard, reasonable test for global identifiability of physically parametrized model structures at $\theta^*$.

- **Why Physical Models Often Fail (4.138) (Connection to the DC-Motor Example)**:
  In mechanistic continuous-time models, the coefficients of $G_c(s,\theta)$ are nonlinear functions of multiple underlying physical constants (masses, resistances, inductances, friction coefficients). For instance, in the continuous-time DC-motor transfer function $G_c(s) = \frac{\beta}{s(\alpha s + \gamma)} = \frac{\beta/\gamma}{s(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}s + 1)}$, if one parametrizes the system in terms of the three raw physical constants $\theta = [\alpha, \beta, \gamma]^T$, scaling all three parameters by any common nonzero factor $c$ leaves $G_c(s,\theta)$ completely unchanged. Only the two lumped ratios $\tau = \alpha/\gamma$ (time constant) and $K = \beta/\gamma$ (gain) are uniquely determined by the coefficients of $G_c(s)$, requiring a reparametrization to $\theta = [\tau, K]^T$ so that (4.138) holds. For general nonlinear state-space models, testing (4.138) requires either brute-force solution of the nonlinear coefficient equations or differential-algebraic elimination methods (e.g., Walter, 1982; Godfrey, 1983; Ljung and Glad, 1994b).

### 4.6.3 SISO Transfer-Function Model Structures

We now analyze the general black-box SISO model family (4.33) with parameter vector (4.41), starting with two foundational special cases: **ARX** and **OE**.

#### Case 1: The ARX Model Structure (Strictly Globally Identifiable)
Consider the ARX model structure (4.7)–(4.9):

$$
\begin{aligned}
G(z,\theta) &= \frac{B(z)}{A(z)}, \qquad H(z,\theta) = \frac{1}{A(z)} \\
\theta &= \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & \dots & a_{n_a} & b_1 & \dots & b_{n_b} \end{bmatrix}^T
\end{aligned} \tag{4.139}
$$

**Step-by-Step Identifiability Proof for ARX**:
1. Suppose $G(z,\theta) \equiv G(z,\theta^*)$ and $H(z,\theta) \equiv H(z,\theta^*)$ for almost all $z$.
2. From the noise transfer function equality $H(z,\theta) \equiv H(z,\theta^*)$, we have $\frac{1}{A(z)} \equiv \frac{1}{A^*(z)}$, which immediately forces the denominator polynomials to coincide identically: $A(z) \equiv A^*(z)$, so $a_i = a_i^*$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n_a$.
3. Substituting $A(z) \equiv A^*(z)$ into the input-output equality $\frac{B(z)}{A(z)} \equiv \frac{B^*(z)}{A^*(z)}$ immediately forces the numerator polynomials to coincide identically: $B(z) \equiv B^*(z)$, so $b_j = b_j^*$ for all $j = 1, \dots, n_b$.
4. Because this holds for **every** $\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}^{n_a + n_b}$ without exception (even if $A^*(z)$ and $B^*(z)$ share common factors, since $H(z,\theta) = 1/A(z)$ locks down $A(z)$ independently!), **the ARX structure (4.139) is strictly globally identifiable**.

---

#### Case 2: The Output-Error (OE) Model Structure
Now consider the Output-Error (OE) structure (4.25) with orders $n_b$ and $n_f$, where $H(z,\theta) \equiv 1$. At $\theta = \theta^*$, the transfer function is

$$
\begin{aligned}
G(z,\theta^*) = \frac{B^*(z)}{F^*(z)} &= \frac{b_1^* z^{-1} + \dots + b_{n_b}^* z^{-n_b}}{1 + f_1^* z^{-1} + \dots + f_{n_f}^* z^{-n_f}} \\
&= z^{n_f - n_b} \frac{b_1^* z^{n_b - 1} + \dots + b_{n_b}^*}{z^{n_f} + f_1^* z^{n_f - 1} + \dots + f_{n_f}^*} = z^{n_f - n_b} \frac{z^{n_b} B^*(z)}{z^{n_f} F^*(z)}
\end{aligned} \tag{4.140}
$$

> [!tip] Why Multiply by $z^{n_f}$ and $z^{n_b}$? Avoiding "Roots at Infinity" /Trailing Zero Coefficient Ambiguities
> The polynomials $F^*(z) = 1 + f_1^* z^{-1} + \dots + f_{n_f}^* z^{-n_f}$ and $B^*(z) = b_1^* z^{-1} + \dots + b_{n_b}^* z^{-n_b}$ are polynomials in the backward variable $z^{-1}$. If the trailing coefficient $f_{n_f}^*$ happens to be zero, $F^*(z)$ as a polynomial in $z^{-1}$ drops in degree below $n_f$.
> By defining the shifted polynomials in positive powers of $z$:
>
> $$
> \tilde{F}^*(z) \triangleq z^{n_f} F^*(z) = z^{n_f} + f_1^* z^{n_f - 1} + \dots + f_{n_f}^*, \qquad \tilde{B}^*(z) \triangleq z^{n_b} B^*(z) = b_1^* z^{n_b - 1} + \dots + b_{n_b}^*
> $$
>
> the polynomial $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ is **monic in $z$ and always has exact degree $n_f$**, regardless of whether $f_{n_f}^* = 0$ (if $f_{n_f}^* = 0$, $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ simply has a root at $z = 0$!). This allows exact root counting via the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.

**Step-by-Step Derivation of the OE Coprimeness Condition**:
1. Let $\theta$ be an arbitrary parameter vector with shifted polynomials $\tilde{F}(z) = z^{n_f}F(z)$ and $\tilde{B}(z) = z^{n_b}B(z)$. The identifiability condition $G(z,\theta^*) = G(z,\theta)$ becomes

$$
G(z,\theta^*) = G(z,\theta) = \frac{B(z)}{F(z)} = z^{n_f - n_b} \frac{\tilde{B}(z)}{\tilde{F}(z)}
$$

2. Cancelling the non-zero factor $z^{n_f - n_b}$ and cross-multiplying yields the polynomial identity in $z$:

$$
\tilde{F}(z)\tilde{B}^*(z) - \tilde{F}^*(z)\tilde{B}(z) \equiv 0 \tag{4.141}
$$

3. Because $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ is a monic polynomial in $z$ of exact degree $n_f$, it has exactly $n_f$ roots (zeros) in $\mathbb{C}$:

$$
\tilde{F}^*(\alpha_i) = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n_f
$$

4. **Sufficiency (Coprime $\implies$ Identifiable)**:
   Suppose that $\tilde{B}^*(\alpha_i) \neq 0$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n_f$; that is, $\tilde{B}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ are **coprime** (share no common roots/factors). Evaluating (4.141) at $z = \alpha_i$ gives

$$
\tilde{F}(\alpha_i)\tilde{B}^*(\alpha_i) - \underbrace{\tilde{F}^*(\alpha_i)}_{=0}\tilde{B}(\alpha_i) = 0 \implies \tilde{F}(\alpha_i) = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n_f
$$

   *(Note on repeated roots: If a root $\alpha_i$ of $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ has multiplicity $n_i > 1$, differentiating (4.141) $n_i - 1$ times with respect to $z$ and evaluating at $z = \alpha_i$ recursively proves that $\alpha_i$ is also a root of $\tilde{F}(z)$ of the exact same multiplicity $n_i$.)*
   Since $\tilde{F}(z)$ and $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ are both monic polynomials of degree $n_f$ sharing all $n_f$ roots (counting multiplicity), they must be identical:

$$
\tilde{F}(z) \equiv \tilde{F}^*(z) \implies \tilde{B}(z) \equiv \tilde{B}^*(z) \implies \theta = \theta^*
$$

5. **Necessity (Common Factor $\implies$ Unidentifiable Globally and Locally)**:
   Conversely, suppose $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{B}^*(z)$ are **not** coprime, so they share a non-constant monic common factor $\gamma(z)$ of degree $d_\gamma \ge 1$:

$$
\tilde{F}^*(z) = \gamma(z)\tilde{F}_1^*(z), \qquad \tilde{B}^*(z) = \gamma(z)\tilde{B}_1^*(z)
$$

   *(Note: If $B^*(z) \equiv 0$, then $\tilde{B}^*(z) \equiv 0$ shares all of $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ as a common factor, leaving $\tilde{F}(z)$ completely arbitrary!)*
   Then for **any** monic polynomial $\beta(z)$ of degree $d_\gamma$, constructing the candidate polynomials

$$
\tilde{F}(z) = \beta(z)\tilde{F}_1^*(z), \qquad \tilde{B}(z) = \beta(z)\tilde{B}_1^*(z)
$$

   satisfies the degree constraints ($\deg \tilde{F} = n_f$, $\deg \tilde{B} \le n_b - 1$) and satisfies (4.141) identically:

$$
\tilde{F}(z)\tilde{B}^*(z) - \tilde{F}^*(z)\tilde{B}(z) = \beta(z)\tilde{F}_1^*(z)\gamma(z)\tilde{B}_1^*(z) - \gamma(z)\tilde{F}_1^*(z)\beta(z)\tilde{B}_1^*(z) \equiv 0
$$

   Since $\beta(z)$ can be chosen arbitrarily close to $\gamma(z)$ (with $\beta(z) \neq \gamma(z)$), there is a continuum of distinct parameter vectors $\theta \neq \theta^*$ arbitrarily close to $\theta^*$ that produce the exact same transfer function.
   - **Conclusion for OE**: *The OE structure (4.25) is globally and locally identifiable at $\theta^*$ if and only if the shifted numerator and denominator polynomials $z^{n_f}F^*(z)$ and $z^{n_b}B^*(z)$ are coprime.*

#### Case 3: The General Black-Box SISO Structure (Theorem 4.1 & Corollary)

Extending this polynomial root-cancellation analysis to the general SISO model structure (4.33) yields **Theorem 4.1**:

> [!important] Theorem 4.1: Global Identifiability of the General Black-Box SISO Model Structure
> Consider the model structure $\mathcal{M}$ corresponding to
>
> $$
> A(q)y(t) = \frac{B(q)}{F(q)}u(t) + \frac{C(q)}{D(q)}e(t) \tag{4.142}
> $$
>
> with $\theta$, given by (4.41), being the coefficients of the polynomials involved, and with formal polynomial degrees $n_a, n_b, n_c, n_d, n_f$. This model structure is **globally identifiable at $\theta^*$** if and only if **all six** of the following conditions (i) to (vi) hold:
> - **i.** There is no common factor to all three polynomials $z^{n_a}A^*(z)$, $z^{n_b}B^*(z)$, and $z^{n_c}C^*(z)$.
> - **ii.** There is no common factor to $z^{n_b}B^*(z)$ and $z^{n_f}F^*(z)$.
> - **iii.** There is no common factor to $z^{n_c}C^*(z)$ and $z^{n_d}D^*(z)$.
> - **iv.** If $n_a \ge 1$, then there must be no common factor to $z^{n_f}F^*(z)$ and $z^{n_d}D^*(z)$.
> - **v.** If $n_d \ge 1$, then there must be no common factor to $z^{n_a}A^*(z)$ and $z^{n_b}B^*(z)$.
> - **vi.** If $n_f \ge 1$, then there must be no common factor to $z^{n_a}A^*(z)$ and $z^{n_c}C^*(z)$.
>
> *(Here the starred polynomials correspond to $\theta^*$. Note that if a polynomial is identically zero, e.g., $B^*(z) \equiv 0$, any factor divides it, so condition (ii) is automatically violated if $n_f \ge 1$.)*

**Algebraic Intuition Behind Each of the Six Conditions in Theorem 4.1**:
Write the two transfer functions in terms of the shifted polynomials in $z$:

$$
G(z,\theta) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)F(z)} = z^{n_a + n_f - n_b} \frac{\tilde{B}(z)}{\tilde{A}(z)\tilde{F}(z)}, \qquad H(z,\theta) = \frac{C(z)}{A(z)D(z)} = z^{n_a + n_d - n_c} \frac{\tilde{C}(z)}{\tilde{A}(z)\tilde{D}(z)}
$$

Each condition prevents a specific algebraic pole-zero cancellation or factor-swapping degree of freedom:
- **Condition (i)**: Prevents multiplying $\tilde{A}^*(z)$, $\tilde{B}^*(z)$, and $\tilde{C}^*(z)$ simultaneously by $\beta(z)/\gamma(z)$, which would cancel out of both $G = B/(AF)$ and $H = C/(AD)$.
- **Condition (ii)**: Prevents multiplying $\tilde{B}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ by $\beta(z)/\gamma(z)$ inside $B/(AF)$.
- **Condition (iii)**: Prevents multiplying $\tilde{C}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{D}^*(z)$ by $\beta(z)/\gamma(z)$ inside $C/(AD)$.
- **Condition (iv)** ($n_a \ge 1$): If $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{D}^*(z)$ share a common factor $\gamma(z)$ while $\tilde{A}^*(z)$ has room to change ($n_a \ge 1$, e.g., if $\tilde{A}^*(z)$ has a root at $z=0$ or shares a factor), one can trade a factor $\gamma(z)$ between the pair $(\tilde{F}, \tilde{D})$ and $\tilde{A}$ without changing the products $\tilde{A}\tilde{F}$ or $\tilde{A}\tilde{D}$.
- **Condition (v)** ($n_d \ge 1$): If $\tilde{A}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{B}^*(z)$ share a factor $\gamma(z)$, replacing that factor in $\tilde{A}$ and $\tilde{B}$ leaves $G = B/(AF)$ invariant, and if $n_d \ge 1$, the inverse adjustment can be absorbed into $\tilde{D}$ so that $\tilde{A}\tilde{D}$ (and hence $H = C/(AD)$) also remains invariant!
- **Condition (vi)** ($n_f \ge 1$): Symmetric to (v): if $\tilde{A}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{C}^*(z)$ share a factor $\gamma(z)$, replacing it in $\tilde{A}$ and $\tilde{C}$ leaves $H = C/(AD)$ invariant, and if $n_f \ge 1$, the inverse adjustment can be absorbed into $\tilde{F}$ so that $\tilde{A}\tilde{F}$ (and hence $G = B/(AF)$) remains invariant.

**Specialization to Common Sub-Structures**:
- **ARX ($n_c = n_d = n_f = 0$)**: Since $C^*(z) \equiv D^*(z) \equiv F^*(z) \equiv 1$, their shifted polynomials are $z^0 \cdot 1 = 1$, which have degree $0$ and cannot have any non-constant common factors. Moreover $n_d = 0$ and $n_f = 0$ deactivate (v) and (vi). Thus (i)–(vi) hold for **all** $\theta^*$, recovering strict global identifiability!
- **ARMAX ($n_d = n_f = 0$)**: $\tilde{D}^*(z) = \tilde{F}^*(z) = 1$, so conditions (ii)–(vi) are automatically satisfied. Only **Condition (i)** remains: $z^{n_a}A^*(z)$, $z^{n_b}B^*(z)$, and $z^{n_c}C^*(z)$ must have no common factor to all three.
- **Box-Jenkins ($n_a = 0$)**: $\tilde{A}^*(z) = 1$, so (i), (iv), (v), and (vi) are automatically satisfied. Only **Conditions (ii) and (iii)** remain: $(z^{n_b}B^*, z^{n_f}F^*)$ must be coprime, and $(z^{n_c}C^*, z^{n_d}D^*)$ must be coprime.

> [!tip] Corollary: Global Identifiability of the General Black-Box Structure (4.142)
> Two polynomials share a common root if and only if their Sylvester resultant vanishes—an algebraic equation that defines a lower-dimensional hyper-surface (of Lebesgue measure zero) in the parameter space $\mathbb{R}^d$. Because conditions (i) to (vi) can be violated only for "special" $\theta^*$ lying on these hyper-surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^d$, we obtain immediately:
>
> **Corollary**: *The model structure given by (4.142) is globally identifiable.*

### 4.6.4 Looking for a "True" System Within Identifiable Structures (Theorem 4.2)

We now apply Theorem 4.1 to a critical practical question: Suppose the true data-generating system $\mathcal{S}$ belongs to the model class ($\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$), so that by (4.135) the asymptotic limit set $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \{\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \mid \mathcal{M}(\theta) = \mathcal{S}\}$ is non-empty. When is $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M})$ a **singleton** $\{\theta_0\}$ (i.e., when is $\mathcal{M}$ globally identifiable at $\theta_0$)?

Suppose the true system $\mathcal{S}$ is described in minimal form by

$$
\mathcal{S}: \quad G_0(q) = \frac{B_0(q)}{A_0(q)F_0(q)}, \qquad H_0(q) = \frac{C_0(q)}{A_0(q)D_0(q)} \tag{4.143}
$$

with **true minimal orders** $n_a^0, n_b^0, n_c^0, n_d^0, n_f^0$ obtained *after all possible cancellations of common factors* in $z^{-1}$ (meaning the trailing coefficients $a_{n_a^0}^0, b_{n_b^0}^0, c_{n_c^0}^0, d_{n_d^0}^0, f_{n_f^0}^0$ are all non-zero, so the shifted true polynomials $z^{n_a^0}A_0(z)$, etc., have **no roots at $z = 0$** and satisfy conditions (i)–(vi) of Theorem 4.1 at the true orders).

The true system $\mathcal{S}$ belongs to the model structure $\mathcal{M}$ in (4.142) if and only if all chosen model orders are at least as large as the true orders:

$$
n_a \ge n_a^0, \qquad n_b \ge n_b^0, \qquad n_c \ge n_c^0, \qquad n_d \ge n_d^0, \qquad n_f \ge n_f^0 \tag{4.144}
$$

When (4.144) holds, there exists at least one parameter vector $\theta_0 \in D_{\mathcal{M}}$ (obtained by padding the true polynomials $A_0, B_0, C_0, D_0, F_0$ with trailing zero coefficients from $n_x^0 + 1$ up to $n_x$) such that

$$
\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{M}(\theta_0) \tag{4.145}
$$

**How Over-Specified Orders Introduce Common Factors at $z = 0$**:
When we pad $A_0(z)$ with $n_a - n_a^0$ trailing zeros to view it as a polynomial of formal degree $n_a$, its shifted polynomial becomes:

$$
z^{n_a}A_0(z) = z^{n_a - n_a^0} \underbrace{\left(z^{n_a^0}A_0(z)\right)}_{\text{no root at } z=0}
$$

Because the minimal polynomials $z^{n_a^0}A_0(z), z^{n_b^0}B_0(z), \dots$ already have no non-zero common roots in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$, the **only** possible common factor between any subset of the padded polynomials $z^{n_a}A_0(z), z^{n_b}B_0(z), \dots$ is a power of $z$ (a root at $z = 0$)! Specifically:
- $z^{n_a}A_0(z)$ has a factor $z$ (a root at $z=0$) if and only if $n_a - n_a^0 \ge 1$.
- Consequently, a collection of padded polynomials shares a common factor $z^k$ ($k \ge 1$) if and only if **every** polynomial in that collection has excess order $\ge 1$, i.e., if and only if the minimum of their order excesses is $\ge 1$.
- Negating this, they have **no common factor** if and only if the minimum of their order excesses is **zero**!

Translating Conditions (i)–(vi) of Theorem 4.1 via this exact equivalence yields **Theorem 4.2**:

> [!important] Theorem 4.2: Order-Excess Conditions for Uniquely Representing a True System $\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$
> Consider the system description $\mathcal{S}$ in (4.143) with true minimal polynomial orders $n_a^0, n_b^0, n_c^0, n_d^0, n_f^0$ as defined above. Consider the model structure $\mathcal{M}$ of Theorem 4.1 with orders satisfying (4.144). Then $\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$ and corresponds to a **globally identifiable $\theta$-value** (so $D_T(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{M}) = \{\theta_0\}$ is a singleton) if and only if:
> - **i.** $\min(n_a - n_a^0,\; n_b - n_b^0,\; n_c - n_c^0) = 0$.
> - **ii.** $\min(n_b - n_b^0,\; n_f - n_f^0) = 0$.
> - **iii.** $\min(n_c - n_c^0,\; n_d - n_d^0) = 0$.
> - **iv.** If $n_a \ge 1$, then also $\min(n_f - n_f^0,\; n_d - n_d^0) = 0$.
> - **v.** If $n_d \ge 1$, then also $\min(n_a - n_a^0,\; n_b - n_b^0) = 0$.
> - **vi.** If $n_f \ge 1$, then also $\min(n_a - n_a^0,\; n_c - n_c^0) = 0$.

- **Practical Impact on Model Order Selection**:
  Without Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2, one might fear that to obtain a globally identifiable representation of $\mathcal{S}$, one would have to guess the exact true orders $n_a = n_a^0, n_b = n_b^0, \dots$ via an exhaustive combinatorial search over all combinations of polynomial degrees.
  Theorem 4.2 proves that **such a multi-dimensional search is unnecessary!** For example, if $\mathcal{S}$ can be described in **ARMAX form** with finite true orders $n_a^0, n_b^0, n_c^0$ (so $n_f = n_d = 0$), we only need Condition (i):

$$
\min(n_a - n_a^0,\; n_b - n_b^0,\; n_c - n_c^0) = 0
$$

  If we simply set a single common order $n_a = n_b = n_c = n$ to define a one-parameter family of ARMAX structures $\mathcal{M}_n$ and increment $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ one unit at a time, then as soon as $n$ reaches

$$
n = \max(n_a^0,\; n_b^0,\; n_c^0)
$$

  at least one of the three differences $n - n_a^0$, $n - n_b^0$, or $n - n_c^0$ is guaranteed to equal **zero**, while all three are $\ge 0$! Thus $\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}_n$ and $\mathcal{M}_n$ is globally identifiable at $\theta_0$.

### 4.6.5 SISO State-Space Models

Finally, consider a discrete-time $n$-dimensional SISO state-space model structure in innovations form (4.91):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t + 1, \theta) &= A(\theta)x(t, \theta) + B(\theta)u(t) + K(\theta)e(t) \\
y(t) &= C(\theta)x(t, \theta) + e(t)
\end{aligned} \tag{4.146a}
$$

#### 1. Parameter Counting ($3n$ Free Parameters)
From the corresponding input-output transfer functions (4.92):

$$
G(q, \theta) = C(\theta)\big(qI - A(\theta)\big)^{-1}B(\theta) = \frac{b_1 q^{n-1} + \dots + b_n}{q^n + a_1 q^{n-1} + \dots + a_n}, \qquad H(q, \theta) = I + C(\theta)\big(qI - A(\theta)\big)^{-1}K(\theta) = \frac{q^n + c_1 q^{n-1} + \dots + c_n}{q^n + a_1 q^{n-1} + \dots + a_n}
$$

the entire input-output behavior is completely specified by **$3n$ parameters**:
- $n$ coefficients of the common monic $n$-th order characteristic denominator polynomial $\det(qI - A(\theta))$, and
- $2n$ coefficients of the two $(n-1)$-th order numerator polynomials (for $G$ and $H - 1$).

Therefore, the matrices $A(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, $B(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$, $C(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times n}$, and $K(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$ **cannot** be "filled" with free adjustable parameters ($n^2 + 3n$ entries), because any non-singular similarity transformation $\tilde{x}(t) = T x(t)$ ($T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, $n^2$ degrees of freedom) yields the exact same transfer functions $G(q,\theta)$ and $H(q,\theta)$. To obtain an identifiable state-space structure, we must parametrize $(A, B, C, K)$ using at most $3n$ parameters.

#### 2. The Observer Canonical Form
One natural $3n$-parameter canonical structure is the **observer canonical form** introduced in Example 4.2:

$$
A(\theta) = \left[\begin{array}{c:ccc}
\times & & & \\
\times & & I_{n-1} & \\
\vdots & & & \\ \hdashline
\times & 0 & \dots & 0
\end{array}\right], \qquad
B(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \times \\ \times \\ \vdots \\ \times \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
K(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \times \\ \times \\ \vdots \\ \times \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
C(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{4.146b}
$$

where $I_{n-1}$ is the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ identity matrix and each $\times$ marks one of the $3n$ adjustable parameters in $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{3n}$ (specifically, the first column of $A(\theta)$ holds $[-a_1, \dots, -a_n]^T$, $B(\theta)$ holds $[b_1, \dots, b_n]^T$, and $K(\theta)$ holds $[c_1 - a_1, \dots, c_n - a_n]^T$).

#### 3. Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Identifiability of (4.146)
1. **One-to-One Correspondence with ARMAX ($n_a = n_b = n_c = n$)**:
   As shown in Example 4.2, the observer canonical form (4.146) is in strict one-to-one linear correspondence with an ARMAX model structure having equal formal orders $n_a = n_b = n_c = n$.
2. **Application of Theorem 4.1**:
   By Theorem 4.1 (Condition (i)), this ARMAX structure is globally (and locally) identifiable at $\theta^*$ if and only if the three shifted polynomials $z^n A^*(z)$, $z^n B^*(z)$, and $z^n C^*(z)$ do **not** share a common factor—meaning that the input-output description cannot be represented using a smaller state dimension $< n$ (i.e., the realization is **minimal**).
3. **Kalman Minimal Realization Theorem (Observability + Controllability)**:
   From linear systems theory, a state-space realization is minimal if and only if it is both **observable** and **controllable**.
   - Because $(A(\theta), C(\theta))$ is in observer canonical form (4.146b), its observability matrix is lower triangular with ones on the main diagonal, so the realization is **observable by construction for all $\theta$**.
   - Therefore, non-minimality (a common factor across $z^n A^*(z)$, $z^n B^*(z)$, and $z^n C^*(z)$) can occur if and only if the state-space realization at $\theta^*$ is **uncontrollable** from the combined inputs $\begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ e(t) \end{bmatrix}$.

> [!important] Identifiability Criterion for the Observer Canonical Form (4.146)
> The SISO state-space structure (4.146) in observer canonical form is **globally and locally identifiable at $\theta^*$** if and only if the two-input system
>
> $$
> \Big\{\, A(\theta^*),\; \big[\, B(\theta^*) \quad K(\theta^*) \,\big] \,\Big\}
> $$
>
> is **controllable** (i.e., the $n \times 2n$ controllability matrix has full row rank $n$).
> *(Note: This result applies specifically to the observer canonical parametrization (4.146), where observability is guaranteed by construction and all $3n$ parameters are free.)*

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Notation Trap 1: Why Theorem 4.1 Checks $z^{n_f}F^*(z)$ Instead of $F^*(z)$**:
>   In system identification, polynomials like $F^*(z) = 1 + f_1^* z^{-1} + \dots + f_{n_f}^* z^{-n_f}$ are written in powers of $z^{-1}$. If you forget to multiply by $z^{n_f}$ and $z^{n_b}$ before checking coprimeness, you will miss **"common roots at $z = 0$"**! Specifically, if both trailing coefficients vanish ($f_{n_f}^* = 0$ and $b_{n_b}^* = 0$), then $F^*(z)$ and $B^*(z)$ might have no non-zero common roots in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$, yet $z^{n_f}F^*(z)$ and $z^{n_b}B^*(z)$ both share the factor $z$—meaning the model order was over-specified by at least $1$, and one can shift a factor $(1 + \alpha z^{-1})$ into both $F$ and $B$ without violating the degree bounds $n_f, n_b$!
> - **Conceptual Leap 1: Why ARX Never Suffers from Pole-Zero Cancellation Unidentifiability**:
>   Students often wonder why an over-parametrized ARX model $A(q)y(t) = B(q)u(t) + e(t)$ remains strictly globally identifiable even when $A^*(z)$ and $B^*(z)$ share a common factor. Look at $H(z,\theta) = \frac{1}{A(z)}$: because the numerator of the noise model is fixed to $1$ ($n_c = 0$), any common factor multiplied into $A(z)$ and $B(z)$ immediately alters $H(z,\theta)$! Only when $n_c \ge 1$ (as in ARMAX) or $n_d \ge 1$ (as in Box-Jenkins/BJ) can a factor multiplied into $A(z)$ be compensated in the noise path.
> - **Conceptual Leap 2: Two-Input Controllability $\{A, [B\;\; K]\}$ vs. Deterministic Controllability $\{A, B\}$**:
>   In the state-space observer canonical form (4.146), identifiability does **not** require $\{A(\theta^*), B(\theta^*)\}$ alone to be controllable! Even if a mode of $A(\theta^*)$ is uncontrollable from the deterministic input $u(t)$ (so $z^n A^*(z)$ and $z^n B^*(z)$ share a root), the full parameter vector $\theta^*$ is still uniquely identifiable provided that mode is excited by the stochastic innovation $e(t)$ through $K(\theta^*)$ (so $z^n C^*(z)$ does not share that root).
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why does a common factor in $\tilde{F}^*(z)$ and $\tilde{B}^*(z)$ destroy *both* global and local identifiability simultaneously? *(Hint: Because the replacement factor $\beta(z)$ can be chosen arbitrarily close to $\gamma(z)$.)*
>   2. Using Theorem 4.2, if a true Box-Jenkins system has orders $(n_b^0, n_f^0, n_c^0, n_d^0) = (2, 2, 1, 3)$ and we choose $n_b = n_f = n_c = n_d = 3$ with $n_a = 0$, is the model globally identifiable at $\theta_0$? *(Hint: Check Condition (ii): $\min(n_b - n_b^0, n_f - n_f^0) = \min(3-2, 3-2) = 1 \neq 0$, so NO—the input pathway is over-parametrized in both numerator and denominator!)*
