# 1.4 The System Identification Procedure

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 1**: After walking through a concrete least-squares modeling example in Section 1.3, Section 1.4 abstracts those lessons into the universal architecture of system identification. It formalizes the **Three Basic Entities** (the Data Record, the Model Structure, and the Identification Criterion), establishes **Model Validation** as the ultimate gatekeeper of model utility, and ties everything together into the iterative **System Identification Loop** (Figure 1.10).

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

In Section 1.3, we saw that fitting even a simple first-order linear difference equation to experimental data required making specific choices: how to excite the system with an input signal $u(t)$, what mathematical form the candidate model should take, and how to measure "fit" (e.g., minimizing the sum of squared prediction errors).

Section 1.4 elevates this into a general engineering workflow. Why does Ljung separate the procedure into distinct entities rather than treating "system identification" as a single black-box algorithm?
1. **Separation of Concerns**: What you can learn is fundamentally bounded by **what is in the data** (Experiment Design), **what shapes your equations are allowed to take** (Model Structure), and **how you score competing candidates** (Identification Method). When an identified model fails in practice, diagnosing the failure requires knowing *which* of these three pillars—or the numerical solver—broke down.
2. **The Spectrum of Physical Prior Knowledge**: Real-world engineering problems span a continuum. At one extreme, you might know the exact differential equations from Newtonian mechanics or Kirchhoff's laws, leaving only a few physical constants (mass, resistance, heat transfer coefficients) unknown—this is **Gray-Box** modeling. At the other extreme, the underlying physics may be hopelessly complex or unknown, so you adopt a flexible mathematical family whose parameters have no direct physical meaning and serve purely as knobs to fit the input-output behavior—this is **Black-Box** modeling.
3. **Pragmatism Over "Truth"**: Physical systems are infinite-dimensional, nonlinear, and subject to non-stationary disturbances. Therefore, *no mathematical model is ever the "true" system*. The goal of system identification is never to prove a model is "true," but to test via **Model Validation** whether it is *"good enough"* for its intended engineering purpose (e.g., simulation, prediction, or closed-loop control design), leading naturally to an iterative refinement loop.

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

| Symbol | Meaning / Physical Interpretation | Space / Dimensions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $t$ | Discrete time index (sampling instant) | $\mathbb{Z}^+$ |
| $u(t)$ | Measured input signal at time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ (or $\mathbb{R}$ for SISO) |
| $y(t)$ | Measured output signal at time $t$ | $\mathbb{R}^p$ (or $\mathbb{R}$ for SISO) |
| $Z^N$ | Complete recorded input-output data set over $N$ samples, $\{u(1), y(1), \dots, u(N), y(N)\}$ | $\mathbb{R}^{N \times (m+p)}$ |
| $Z^{t-1}$ | Past input-output data record strictly prior to time $t$, $\{u(1), y(1), \dots, u(t-1), y(t-1)\}$ (and current/past inputs up to $t$ if direct feedthrough is present) | $\mathbb{R}^{(t-1) \times (m+p)}$ |
| $\theta$ | Finite-dimensional parameter vector indexing a specific candidate model within the model structure | $\theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ |
| $\mathcal{M}$ | Model structure: a parameterized collection of candidate models | Family of mappings $\{g(\theta, \cdot) : \theta \in D_{\mathcal{M}}\}$ |
| $\hat{y}(t\|\theta)$ | One-step-ahead predicted output at time $t$ given parameter vector $\theta$ and past data $Z^{t-1}$ | $\mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $g(\theta, Z^{t-1})$ | Parameterized predictor mapping from past data $Z^{t-1}$ to the space of model outputs | $D_{\mathcal{M}} \times \mathcal{Z}^{t-1} \to \mathbb{R}^p$ |
| $\bar{R}_N$ | Sample covariance / information matrix of the regressors (from Eq. 1.18 in Section 1.3: $\bar{R}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$) | $\mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (symmetric positive semi-definite) |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Formulations

### 1.4.1 Three Basic Entities

The construction of a mathematical model from observed data always rests on three foundational entities:

1. **The Data Set ($Z^N$)**: The recorded input-output measurements (as introduced in Eq. 1.6):

$$
Z^N = \{u(1), y(1), u(2), y(2), \dots, u(N), y(N)\}
$$

2. **The Model Structure ($\mathcal{M}$)**: A set of candidate models (such as the first-order deterministic model in Eq. 1.1 or the stochastic ARX model in Eq. 1.5).
3. **The Identification Criterion (Selection Rule)**: A quantitative rule by which candidate models are assessed and ranked using the data $Z^N$ (such as the Least-Squares criterion in Eq. 1.9).

Let us examine the formal role and engineering implications of each entity:

#### Entity 1: The Data Record & Experiment Design
Data acquisition occurs under one of two regimes:
- **Designed Identification Experiments**: The engineer has active control over which signals to measure, when to sample them (sampling interval $T$), and how to shape the input excitation $u(t)$. The objective of **experiment design** (treated formally in Chapter 13) is to make these choices so that the recorded data $Z^N$ become **maximally informative**, subject to physical or operational constraints (such as input amplitude saturation or output safety limits).
  - *Mathematical Link to Section 1.3*: Recall from Eq. (1.18) in Section 1.3 that the covariance of the parameter estimate error $\tilde{\theta}_N = \hat{\theta}_N - \theta_0$ is proportional to $\bar{R}_N^{-1}$, where:

$$
\bar{R}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)
$$

    Making the inverse matrix $\bar{R}_N^{-1}$ as "small" as possible (equivalently, making the information matrix $\bar{R}_N$ large and well-conditioned through persistent excitation of $u(t)$) is the archetypal objective of experiment design.
- **Normal Operating Data**: In many industrial or economic systems, active experimentation is impossible or forbidden, and the engineer must work passively with data logged during normal operation.

#### Entity 2: The Set of Models (The Model Structure)
Specifying the collection of candidate models within which we search for a suitable description is **the single most important—and most difficult—choice** in the entire system identification procedure. It is the exact point where *a priori* physical knowledge, engineering intuition, and formal mathematical properties must be synthesized.

Depending on how prior physical knowledge is incorporated, model sets fall into two fundamental paradigms:

| Paradigm | Construction Principle | Role of Parameter Vector $\theta$ | Typical Use Case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Gray-Box Models** | Constructed via careful physical modeling from basic physical laws (e.g., Newton's laws, thermodynamics, mass/energy balances) and well-established constitutive relationships, leaving some physical parameters unknown. | Adjustable parameters $\theta$ possess **direct physical interpretation** (e.g., friction coefficients, thermal capacitances, reaction rates). | Systems with well-understood physics where specific physical constants cannot be measured directly on the bench. |
| **Black-Box Models** | Standard linear (or nonlinear) mathematical model families employed without reference to the specific physical background of the plant. | Parameters $\theta$ act strictly as **vehicles (tuning knobs) for adjusting the fit to the data** and do not reflect individual physical components inside the system. | Complex, poorly understood, or high-order processes where an accurate input-output predictor is needed for control or forecasting. |

**Formal Predictor Definition of a Model Structure**:
Regardless of whether a model set is gray-box or black-box, its mathematical essence in system identification is that every candidate model acts as a **predictor** of the next output $y(t)$ given the history of past observations $Z^{t-1}$. Formally, a **model structure** is a parameterized mapping from past inputs and outputs $Z^{t-1}$ to the space of model outputs:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(\theta, Z^{t-1}) \tag{1.19}
$$

- **Key Assumptions & Properties of Eq. (1.19)**:
  - **Finite-Dimensional Parameterization**: The parameter vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is finite-dimensional, indexing a specific predictor $g(\theta, \cdot)$ within the candidate family (covered in detail in Chapters 4 and 5).
  - **Causality**: The prediction $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ at time $t$ depends only on past recorded data $Z^{t-1}$ available prior to observing $y(t)$.

#### Entity 3: Determining the "Best" Model in the Set (The Identification Method)
Once $Z^N$ is recorded and the predictor family $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(\theta, Z^{t-1})$ is specified, we need an **identification method** to select the "best" parameter value $\hat{\theta}_N$ guided by the data.
- **Core Principle**: Model quality is assessed by how well each candidate model reproduces the measured data—specifically, by comparing the model's one-step-ahead predictions $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ against the actual measured outputs $y(t)$ via the prediction error $\varepsilon(t, \theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$.
- **Modularity**: The basic principles for formulating this criterion of fit (e.g., prediction error methods, maximum likelihood, instrumental variables) can be developed and analyzed **independently of the specific model structure** $g(\theta, Z^{t-1})$ used (treated in Chapter 7).

### 1.4.2 Model Validation

Having settled on the three basic choices—Data ($Z^N$), Model Structure ($\mathcal{M}$), and Identification Criterion—we arrive (at least implicitly) at one specific model $\hat{y}(t|\hat{\theta}_N)$: the single candidate in the chosen set that best describes the estimation data according to our chosen criterion.

However, **being the "best" model inside a chosen candidate set does not guarantee that the model is actually good!** If the entire candidate set $\mathcal{M}$ is fundamentally flawed, the "best" model in $\mathcal{M}$ will still be poor.

We must therefore subject the identified model to **model validation** (treated in Chapter 16): testing whether the model is **"good enough"**—that is, whether it is *valid for its intended purpose*. Model validation procedures evaluate how the model relates to:
1. **Observed Data**: Does it accurately reproduce input-output behavior (especially on fresh *validation data* not used to estimate $\hat{\theta}_N$), and are the residual prediction errors uncorrelated with past inputs?
2. **Prior Knowledge**: Do the estimated step response, frequency response, or physical parameters agree with known engineering bounds and domain insights?
3. **Intended Use**: Is the model sufficiently accurate in the specific frequency bands or operating regimes required by its downstream application (e.g., closed-loop stability margins for a controller, or multi-step prediction accuracy for a forecaster)?

> [!important] The Philosophical Axiom of System Identification
> *"A model can never be accepted as a final and true description of the system. Rather, it can at best be regarded as a good enough description of certain aspects that are of particular interest to us."* — Lennart Ljung

Deficient behavior in validation tests forces us to **reject** the model, whereas consistent success across diverse validation tests builds practical **confidence** in the model.

### 1.4.3 The System Identification Loop

In theory, system identification follows a clean linear progression:

$$
\text{Collect Data } (Z^N) \longrightarrow \text{Choose Model Set } (\mathcal{M}) \longrightarrow \text{Pick "Best" Model } (\hat{\theta}_N)
$$

In practice, it is **highly likely that the very first model obtained will fail the model validation tests**. We must then loop back and revise earlier steps of the procedure. When a model fails validation, the deficiency stems from one of **four distinct root causes**:

1. **Numerical Optimization Failure**: The numerical search algorithm failed to find the true global optimum $\hat{\theta}_N$ of our chosen criterion (e.g., it became trapped in a local minimum or suffered from ill-conditioned gradients).
2. **Inappropriate Criterion of Fit**: The identification criterion was poorly chosen for the noise environment or intended application.
3. **Inappropriate Model Set ($\mathcal{M}$)**: The candidate model structure was too restrictive (undermodeling / bias) or structurally mismatched, meaning that *no* parameter vector $\theta$ inside $\mathcal{M}$ could provide a "good enough" description of the physical system.
4. **Uninformative Data Set ($Z^N$)**: The experimental data lacked sufficient information (e.g., poor input excitation, excessive noise, improper sampling rate, or closed-loop identifiability issues) to guide the selection of a good model.

Because addressing these four problems—**especially the third one (revising the model set $\mathcal{M}$)**—requires trial, diagnosis, and refinement guided by both prior knowledge and the outcomes of previous attempts, practical system identification is inherently an **iterative loop** (Figure 1.10). Consequently, **interactive software** (such as the MATLAB System Identification Toolbox developed by Ljung) is an indispensable engineering tool for navigating this loop efficiently.

![[assets/fig_1_10_sysid_loop.png]]
- **How to Read This Diagram (`Figure 1.10: The system identification loop`)**:
  - **The Primary Vertical Flow (Left/Center Spine)**: Represents the forward execution of a modeling attempt:
    1. **Experiment Design** shapes how the physical plant is excited and sampled to produce **Data** ($Z^N$).
    2. In parallel, the user selects a candidate family via **Choose Model Set** ($\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(\theta, Z^{t-1})$) and selects an estimation metric via **Choose Criterion of Fit**.
    3. All three basic entities converge into the computational engine: **Calculate Model** (notice the original textbook printing typo *"Calcuate Model"* inside the box!), which solves for $\hat{\theta}_N$.
    4. The resulting model enters the decision block: **Validate Model**.
  - **The Exit Path (`OK: Use it!`)**: If the model passes validation for its intended purpose, the loop terminates and the model is deployed.
  - **The Feedback Spine (`Prior Knowledge` + `Not OK: Revise`)**:
    - Look closely at the right-hand vertical bus: **Prior Knowledge** enters from the top and feeds directly into *every single choice*—**Experiment Design**, **Choose Model Set**, **Choose Criterion of Fit**, and **Calculate Model**.
    - When validation fails (**Not OK: Revise**), the diagnostic insights gained from *how* the model failed join the **Prior Knowledge** bus on the right and route back to revise whichever step caused the deficiency:
      - Route to **Calculate Model** if the numerical optimization failed (Reason 1).
      - Route to **Choose Criterion of Fit** if the cost function was inappropriate (Reason 2).
      - Route to **Choose Model Set** if the model structure lacked the capacity to capture the system dynamics (Reason 3 — the most frequent inner-loop iteration in practice).
      - Route all the way back to **Experiment Design** to collect a new **Data** set if the existing measurements were not informative enough (Reason 4 — the most expensive outer-loop iteration).

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Conceptual Leap — A Model Structure Is a *Predictor*, Not Just a Differential Equation**: Look carefully at Eq. (1.19): $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = g(\theta, Z^{t-1})$. Instead of defining a model merely as a transfer function $G(q)$ or state-space realization, Ljung unifies *all* deterministic and stochastic models under a single umbrella: a rule $g(\theta, Z^{t-1})$ that maps past observed data $Z^{t-1}$ into a prediction $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ of the current output. This single abstraction is the key to the entire book!
> - **Terminology Trap — "Black Box" vs. "Gray Box"**: Students often think "black-box" means neural networks or non-parametric lookup tables, while linear transfer functions are "physical." In Ljung's terminology, **any** standard linear model structure (ARX, ARMAX, Output-Error, Box-Jenkins, generic State-Space) whose polynomial coefficients $\theta$ are tuned purely to fit input-output data without being tied to specific physical constants (like masses or resistances) is a **black-box model**. It only becomes a **gray-box model** when the structure is derived from physical laws so that $\theta$ represents interpretable physical parameters.
> - **Practical Hierarchy of the 4 Failure Modes**: Notice the order in which Ljung lists the four reasons a model fails validation on page 14—from cheapest to fix in software (numerical algorithm $\to$ criterion $\to$ model structure) to most expensive to fix in hardware (re-running the physical experiment to get better data $Z^N$). Ljung explicitly highlights that revising the **model structure** (the 3rd bullet) constitutes the major part of a practical identification application.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why does Ljung emphasize that the *identification method* (Entity 3, Chapter 7) can be treated *independently* of the *model structure* (Entity 2, Chapters 4–5)? *(Hint: Because once any model structure produces a prediction $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$, the criterion only cares about the prediction error $\varepsilon(t,\theta) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$!)*
>   2. If a model fits the estimation data $Z^N$ with a tiny least-squares error $V_N(\hat{\theta}_N)$ but fails miserably during validation, which of the four root causes are most likely at fault?
