# 1.1 Dynamic Systems

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 1**: Establishes the philosophical and engineering foundation of *System Identification*—inferring mathematical models of dynamical systems from observed input-output data—by defining the four fundamental signal classes ($u, w, v, y$) and illustrating core identification challenges through three real-world case studies (a solar-heated house, an unstable fighter aircraft, and human speech production).

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

At its core, all of empirical science is concerned with **inferring models from observations**—linking observed phenomena together into coherent patterns ("hypotheses," "laws of nature," or "paradigms"). **System Identification** is the engineering and mathematical discipline that formalizes this scientific method specifically for **dynamical systems**: building mathematical models directly from observed time-series measurements.

### What Makes a System "Dynamic"?
In loose terms, a **system** is an object or process in which variables of different kinds interact and produce observable signals.
- In a **static (memoryless) system**, the output at time $t$ is determined instantaneously and exclusively by the external stimuli acting at that exact instant $t$.
- In a **dynamic system**, the system possesses *memory* (internal energy storage, inertia, momentum, or thermal capacity): the current output $y(t)$ depends not only on the current external stimuli, but also on their **earlier (past) values**.

### Why Categorize Signals Before Writing Equations?
Before choosing a mathematical model structure, an engineer must draw a boundary around the system and classify how the outside world interacts with it. Not all external stimuli are created equal:
1. Some signals are under our direct control (e.g., turning a pump on/off or deflecting an aircraft control surface)—these are **inputs ($u$)**.
2. Other external forces cannot be controlled by the user, which makes them **disturbances**. However, if we can *measure* a disturbance with a sensor (e.g., measuring solar irradiance with a pyranometer), we can use that measurement to explain variations in the output just like a control input—these are **measured disturbances ($w$)**.
3. Finally, there are always unmeasured environmental fluctuations, internal turbulence, or sensor noise that we cannot measure directly and only observe through their corrupting or driving influence on the output—these are **unmeasured disturbances ($v$)**.

Ljung introduces Section 1.1 through three concrete engineering archetypes rather than abstract axioms because each archetype exposes a distinct structural regime of system identification:
- **Open-Loop Forced & Disturbance-Driven Dynamics** (*Example 1.1: Solar-Heated House*) — combining manipulated binary/continuous inputs, measured environmental disturbances, and unmeasured thermal losses.
- **Multivariable Closed-Loop Dynamics of an Unstable Plant** (*Example 1.2: Military Aircraft JAS-Gripen*) — where multiple coupled actuators drive an aerodynamically unstable airframe that *cannot* be tested open-loop.
- **Purely Stochastic / Unmeasured-Excitation Dynamics ("Time Series")** (*Example 1.3: Human Speech Generation*) — where no external input is measurable, and the model must be inferred solely from the output signal's temporal structure.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Case-Study Formulations

#### 2.2.1 General Input–Output–Disturbance Signal Architecture
A general dynamical system maps the past and present histories of its external stimuli—inputs $u(\tau)$, measured disturbances$w(\tau)$, and unmeasured disturbances$v(\tau)$for$\tau \le t$—into the current observable output$y(t)$:

$$y(t) = \mathcal{S}\Big(\{u(\tau)\}_{\tau \le t},\; \{w(\tau)\}_{\tau \le t},\; \{v(\tau)\}_{\tau \le t}\Big)$$

where $\mathcal{S}(\cdot)$ denotes the causal dynamic operator of the system.

> [!important] Fundamental Modeling Equivalence of $u(t)$and$w(t)$
> For the **control engineer**, the distinction between $u(t)$(manipulable actuator) and$w(t)$(non-manipulable measured environment) is vital because a feedback controller can command$u(t)$but can only feedforward-compensate$w(t)$.
>
> For the **system identification (modeling) process**, however, the distinction between $u(t)$and$w(t)$ is often **unimportant**: both are known, measured deterministic signals entering the system block, so they can be stacked into an augmented known input vector:
>
> $$u_{\text{aug}}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t) \\ w(t) \end{bmatrix}$$

---

#### 2.2.2 Example 1.1: A Solar-Heated House (Forced + Measured Disturbance System)
In the solar-heated house (Figures 1.2–1.4), solar radiation heats air inside a rooftop solar collector panel. A pump circulates this heated air through a pebble-filled heat storage box, from which thermal energy is later drawn to heat the house.

- **System Boundary & Signal Mapping**:
  - **Output $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}$**: Storage temperature ($^\circ\text{C}$) inside the pebble bed.
  - **Manipulated Input $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}$**: Pump velocity (or binary Fan OFF/ON state$u(t) \in \{0, 1\}$).
  - **Measured Disturbance $w(t) \equiv I(t) \in \mathbb{R}$**: Incident solar radiation intensity.
  - **Unmeasured Disturbance $v(t)$**: Ambient outdoor temperature fluctuations, wind-induced convective heat losses, occupancy disturbances, and temperature sensor noise.

- **Sampling & Data Characteristics**:
  - Observation horizon: $T_{\text{total}} = 50\text{ hours}$ (covering roughly two full diurnal day-night cycles).
  - Sampling interval: $T_s = 10\text{ minutes} = \frac{1}{6}\text{ hour}$, yielding$N = 50 \times 6 = 300$ discrete-time samples.
- **Physical & Identification Insights**:
  1. **Thermal Inertia (Dynamic Memory)**: $y(t)$does not jump instantaneously when$u(t)$or$I(t)$ changes; the pebble bed acts as a thermal integrator/low-pass filter whose current temperature depends on the accumulated history of heat influx minus losses.
  2. **Bilinear / Nonlinear Coupling**: Pumping air ($u(t) > 0$) only heats the storage when solar radiation$I(t)$has raised the collector temperature above the storage temperature. Thus, the physical mechanism involves products of signals (e.g.,$u(t) \cdot I(t)$), challenging purely linear approximations.

#### 2.2.3 Example 1.2: A Military Aircraft — SAAB JAS-Gripen (MISO Closed-Loop Unstable System)
To design flight simulators, synthesize fly-by-wire autopilots, and analyze handling qualities for the Swedish fighter aircraft **JAS-Gripen** (Figures 1.5–1.6), engineers combine physical aerodynamics, wind-tunnel tests, and flight-test system identification.

- **System Boundary & Signal Mapping (Pitch Channel)**:
  - **Output $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}$**: Aircraft pitch angular rate$q(t) = \dot{\theta}(t)$.
  - **Control Input Vector $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$**: Three distinct aerodynamic control surface deflections:
    $$u(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u_1(t) \\ u_2(t) \\ u_3(t) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \delta_{\text{elev}}(t) & \text{(Elevator: trailing-edge aileron combinations)} \\ \delta_{\text{lef}}(t) & \text{(Leading-edge flap: front-wing ailerons)} \\ \delta_{\text{can}}(t) & \text{(Canard: separate foreplane rudders)} \end{bmatrix}$$
  - **Unmeasured Disturbance $v(t)$**: Atmospheric turbulence, wind gusts, and unmodeled aeroelastic vibrations.

- **Key Identification Assumptions & Challenges**:
  1. **Multivariable Input Coupling (MISO)**: All three control surfaces ($u_1, u_2, u_3$) simultaneously excite the pitch rate$y(t)$. Identifying their individual transfer functions requires sufficient independent excitation among the three surfaces; if the flight control law moves them in strict collinear lockstep, their individual effects become unidentifiable.
  2. **Open-Loop Instability & Mandatory Closed-Loop Testing**: At the tested flight condition, the JAS-Gripen is **aerodynamically unstable in the pitch channel** (a deliberate design choice in modern fighters to maximize agility and reduce trim drag). Consequently, the experiment *must* be conducted under stabilizing **closed-loop feedback control**:
     $$u(t) = \mathcal{K}\big(r(t) - y(t)\big)$$
     Because $u(t)$depends on$y(t)$via the feedback controller$\mathcal{K}$, the inputs$u(t)$become correlated with the unmeasured flight disturbances$v(t)$—a classic challenge in system identification (revisited rigorously in Chapter 13 and Section 17.3).

#### 2.2.4 Example 1.3: Human Speech Generation (Time-Series / Unmeasured-Input System)
Human speech (Figures 1.7–1.9) is generated when acoustic energy from either periodic vocal cord vibrations (*voiced* sounds, like vowels) or turbulent throat airflow (*unvoiced* sounds, like fricatives "s" or "f") propagates through the resonant acoustic cavity formed by the vocal tract and lips.

- **System Boundary & Signal Mapping**:
  - **Output $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}$**: Acoustic air pressure wave recorded at the listener/microphone.
  - **Manipulated Inputs $u(t)$& Measured Disturbances$w(t)$**: None ($\varnothing$).
  - **Unmeasured Excitation / Disturbance $v(t)$**: Glottal airflow pulses and vocal cord vibrations entering the base of the vocal tract.

- **Sampling & Time-Series Formulation**:
  - Sampling interval: $T_s = 0.125\text{ ms}$, corresponding to a sampling frequency of:
    $$f_s = \frac{1}{T_s} = \frac{1}{0.125 \times 10^{-3}\text{ s}} = 8000\text{ Hz} = 8\text{ kHz}$$
  - Over the $250\text{ ms}$window shown in Figure 1.9, this yields$N = \frac{250\text{ ms}}{0.125\text{ ms}} = 2000$ samples.
  - **Definition of a Time Series**: When a dynamical system has no observed external inputs ($u(t) \equiv 0, w(t) \equiv 0$), its output sequence$\{y(t)\}$is governed purely by how the system's internal dynamics filter the unmeasured excitation$v(t)$:
    $$y(t) = \mathcal{H}\Big(\{v(\tau)\}_{\tau \le t}\Big)$$
    Such an output-only sequence $\{y(t)\}$ is called a **time series** (ubiquitous in speech processing, seismology, and econometrics).
  - **Time-Varying Dynamics**: As a speaker articulates different phonemes over tens of milliseconds (compare $t \in [0, 150]\text{ ms}$vs.$t \in [150, 250]\text{ ms}$in Figure 1.9), the geometry of the tongue, jaw, and lips changes, making the vocal tract filter$\mathcal{H}$**time-varying** over longer horizons but quasi-stationary over short ($10\text{–}30\text{ ms}$) frames.

## 4. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Notation Trap ($w$vs.$v$in Ljung)**: In many modern control textbooks (such as state-space Kalman filtering texts),$w(t)$denotes *process noise* and$v(t)$ denotes *measurement noise*—both unmeasured. **Ljung uses a different convention in Chapter 1**:
>   - $w(t)$= **Measured** disturbance (known deterministic signal, treated just like an input$u(t)$ during identification).
>   - $v(t)$ = **Unmeasured** disturbance (the combined effect of all unmeasured process and measurement noises).
>   Keep this distinction sharp!
> - **Conceptual Leap 1 (Why Closed-Loop Data Can Fool Naive Identification)**: In Example 1.2 (JAS-Gripen), because the aircraft is unstable, an active controller adjusts the elevator and canard in response to pitch-rate perturbations caused by wind gusts $v(t)$. Naive correlation between$u(t)$and$y(t)$ will therefore mix the forward aerodynamics of the aircraft with the inverse transfer function of the feedback controller!
> - **Conceptual Leap 2 (How Can We Identify a System When the Input $v(t)$Is Unmeasured?)**: In Example 1.3 (Speech), we only measure$y(t)$and never measure$v(t)$. How is system identification even possible without an input? The preview answer (developed in Chapters 2–4) is that we model$v(t)$as a *canonical stochastic process* (such as white noise or a periodic pulse train of unknown variance) and attribute all spectral shaping and temporal correlation in$y(t)$ to the system transfer function!
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. Why does Ljung state that *"the distinction between inputs and measured disturbances is often less important for the modeling process"*? When *does* it become important (e.g., control design vs. model fitting)?
>   2. In Figure 1.4, why are all three subplots labeled `(a)` in the original printing? (A minor typographical quirk in the textbook's figure subcaptions `(a) Storage temperature`, `(a) Pump velocity`, `(a) Solar intensity`—don't let it confuse you!).
