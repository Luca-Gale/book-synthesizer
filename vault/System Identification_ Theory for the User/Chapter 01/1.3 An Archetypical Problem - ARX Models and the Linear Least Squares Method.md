# 1.3 An Archetypical Problem - ARX Models and the Linear Least Squares Method

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 1**: This section is the mathematical microcosm of the entire textbook. By walking through a single concrete estimation problem—fitting a discrete-time **ARX (Auto-Regressive with eXogenous input)** linear difference equation to input-output data via the **Linear Least Squares (LS)** method—Ljung previews every core theme of Chapters 4 through 16: model parameterization, one-step-ahead prediction, quadratic loss minimization, semi-physical ("gray-box") feature engineering, unbiasedness, parameter error covariance, and optimal experiment design.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Before building general abstract frameworks for arbitrary nonlinear or stochastic systems, Ljung anchors the reader in the most widely used workhorse of parametric system identification: **Linear Least Squares estimation of difference equations**.

Why start here?
1. **Discrete-Time Matches Digital Measurement**: Physical systems evolve in continuous time, but experimental data are almost always collected by digital sampling at discrete instants. Representing dynamics directly as a discrete-time difference equation (with normalized sampling interval $\Delta t = 1$ time unit) immediately bridges raw sensor logs and algebraic models.
2. **From Simulation to Prediction**: A difference equation is not merely a static constraint; rearranged properly, it becomes a **one-step-ahead predictor** $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ that answers: *"Given the history of inputs and outputs up to time $t-1$, and assuming parameter vector $\theta$, what output should we expect at time $t$?"*
3. **Linear-in-Parameters vs. Linear-in-Variables**: Because the predictor $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$ is *linear in the unknown parameter vector $\theta$*, minimizing the sum of squared prediction errors leads to a convex quadratic optimization problem with an exact, closed-form matrix solution. Crucially, as **Example 1.5 (Immersion Heater)** demonstrates, the regressors inside $\varphi(t)$ can be *nonlinear functions of physical measurements* (such as squared voltage $v^2(t-1)$ representing electrical heating power)—making "linear regression" vastly more versatile than strictly linear black-box modeling.
4. **Linking Input Excitation to Parameter Uncertainty**: By analyzing how measurement noise $e(t)$ propagates through the Least Squares inverse matrix, this section reveals a profound engineering law: **parameter estimation accuracy scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$, improves with Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), and depends strictly on the covariance matrix $\bar{R}$ of the input signal** rather than its exact waveform.

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 1. The ARX Model & Linear Regression Form ("The Model")

Consider a discrete-time single-input single-output (SISO) system governed by a constant-coefficient **linear difference equation** relating the current output $y(t)$ to $n$ past outputs and $m$ past inputs:

$$
y(t) + a_1 y(t - 1) + \dots + a_n y(t - n) = b_1 u(t - 1) + \dots + b_m u(t - m) \tag{1.1}
$$

* **Implicit Assumptions in (1.1)**:
  1. **Discrete-Time Sampling**: Data are sampled at uniform intervals. Without loss of generality, the sampling period $T_s$ is normalized to $1$ time unit.
  2. **Strict Causality (One-Sample Delay)**: The right-hand side begins at $u(t-1)$ rather than $u(t)$, reflecting that physical systems do not respond instantaneously at the exact sampling instant of an applied input.
  3. **Deterministic Exactness (Initially)**: Equation (1.1) contains no noise term yet; it represents an idealized deterministic relationship that we immediately reframe as a *predictor*.

To use (1.1) pragmatically for **determining the next output value** given past observations up to time $t-1$, we move all lagged outputs to the right-hand side:

$$
y(t) = -a_1 y(t - 1) - \dots - a_n y(t - n) + b_1 u(t - 1) + \dots + b_m u(t - m) \tag{1.2}
$$

We now collect the unknown coefficients into a $(n+m)$-dimensional column **parameter vector** $\theta$:

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & \dots & a_n & b_1 & \dots & b_m \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{1.3}
$$

and collect the known past data terms into a $(n+m)$-dimensional column **regression vector** $\varphi(t)$:

$$
\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t - 1) & \dots & -y(t - n) & u(t - 1) & \dots & u(t - m) \end{bmatrix}^T \tag{1.4}
$$

> [!important] Why the Minus Signs Live Inside $\varphi(t)$
> Notice that in (1.1) the autoregressive coefficients $+a_k$ appear on the left-hand side ($y(t) + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k y(t-k)$), which is standard in control theory because the characteristic polynomial is $A(q) = 1 + a_1 q^{-1} + \dots + a_n q^{-n}$. When $a_k y(t-k)$ moves to the right-hand side in (1.2), it acquires a minus sign: $-a_k y(t-k)$. Rather than defining $\theta$ with negative parameters $-a_k$, Ljung absorbs the minus signs directly into the regression vector entries $-y(t-k)$ in (1.4). That way, $\theta$ retains the standard positive polynomial coefficients $a_k$ while (1.2) collapses cleanly into an inner product.

Using (1.3) and (1.4), equation (1.2) is written compactly as the inner product $y(t) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$. Because this calculated value of $y(t)$ from past data depends on our choice of parameter vector $\theta$, we denote it as the **one-step-ahead prediction** $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$:

$$
\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta \tag{1.5}
$$

#### 2. The Least Squares Criterion & Normal Equations ("The Least Squares Method")

Suppose the true parameter vector $\theta$ is unknown, but we have recorded an input-output sequence over $N$ time steps ($1 \le t \le N$):

$$
Z^N = \{u(1), y(1), \dots, u(N), y(N)\} \tag{1.6}
$$

We wish to choose $\theta$ so that the model predictions $\hat{y}(t|\theta)$ match the measured outputs $y(t)$ as closely as possible in the **least squares sense**:

$$
\min_{\theta} V_N(\theta, Z^N) \tag{1.7}
$$

where the quadratic loss function $V_N(\theta, Z^N)$ is the sample average of squared one-step-ahead prediction errors $\varepsilon(t, \theta) \triangleq y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta)$:

$$
V_N(\theta, Z^N) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \left( y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\theta) \right)^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \left( y(t) - \varphi^T(t)\theta \right)^2 \tag{1.8}
$$

We denote the minimizing parameter vector by $\hat{\theta}_N$:

$$
\hat{\theta}_N = \arg\min_{\theta} V_N(\theta, Z^N) \tag{1.9}
$$

##### Step-by-Step Vector Calculus Derivation of the Normal Equations
Because $V_N(\theta, Z^N)$ is a smooth, convex quadratic function of $\theta$, any global minimum satisfies the first-order stationarity condition $\frac{d}{d\theta} V_N(\theta, Z^N) = 0$. Let us expand every matrix calculus step explicitly:

1. **Expand the squared scalar residual** for a single time step $t$. Since $\varphi^T(t)\theta = \theta^T\varphi(t)$ is a scalar:
   

$$
\left( y(t) - \varphi^T(t)\theta \right)^2 = y^2(t) - 2 y(t) \varphi^T(t)\theta + \theta^T \left( \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) \right) \theta
$$

2. **Apply standard vector differentiation identities** with respect to the column vector $\theta$ ($\frac{d}{d\theta}(c^T\theta) = c$ and $\frac{d}{d\theta}(\theta^T A \theta) = 2A\theta$ for symmetric $A = \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$):
   

$$
\frac{d}{d\theta} \left( y(t) - \varphi^T(t)\theta \right)^2 = -2\varphi(t)y(t) + 2\varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)\theta = -2\varphi(t)\left( y(t) - \varphi^T(t)\theta \right)
$$

3. **Sum over $t = 1, \dots, N$ and set the gradient to zero** (adopting Ljung's sign convention on page 9):
   

$$
0 = \frac{d}{d\theta} V_N(\theta, Z^N) = -\frac{2}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\left( y(t) - \varphi^T(t)\theta \right)
$$

4. **Divide through by $-\frac{2}{N}$ and separate terms** to obtain the **Normal Equations**:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)y(t) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)\theta \tag{1.10}
$$

> [!note] Orthogonality Interpretation of (1.10)
> Rewriting (1.10) as $\sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\big(y(t) - \varphi^T(t)\hat{\theta}_N\big) = 0$ reveals the geometric essence of Least Squares: at the optimal estimate $\hat{\theta}_N$, the prediction error sequence $\varepsilon(t, \hat{\theta}_N) = y(t) - \hat{y}(t|\hat{\theta}_N)$ is **sample-orthogonal** to every regressor in $\varphi(t)$.

5. **Solve for $\hat{\theta}_N$**: Provided the $(n+m) \times (n+m)$ symmetric positive semi-definite matrix $R(N) \triangleq \sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$ is **non-singular (invertible)**, we left-multiply both sides of (1.10) by $\left[\sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)\right]^{-1}$ to obtain the closed-form **Linear Least Squares Estimate**:

$$
\hat{\theta}_N = \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) \right]^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)y(t) \tag{1.11}
$$

*(Note: In some notations, $R(N)$ and $f(N)$ are defined with or without the $\frac{1}{N}$ normalization factor: if $R(N) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$ and $f(N) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)y(t)$, the $1/N$ factors cancel in $\hat{\theta}_N = R(N)^{-1}f(N)$. In Section 1.3, Ljung defines $R(N) = \sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$ unnormalized and introduces $\bar{R} = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N}R(N)$ in (1.17).)*

#### 3. Concrete Walkthroughs: Examples 1.4 & 1.5

##### Example 1.4: First-Order Difference Equation
Consider the first-order ARX model ($n=1, m=1$):

$$
y(t) + a y(t - 1) = b u(t - 1)
$$

* **Parameter & Regressor Vectors**:
  

$$
\theta = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t - 1) \\ u(t - 1) \end{bmatrix}
$$

* **Outer Product $\varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$ and Vector $\varphi(t)y(t)$**:
  

$$
\varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t-1) \\ u(t-1) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -y(t-1) & u(t-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y^2(t-1) & -y(t-1)u(t-1) \\ -y(t-1)u(t-1) & u^2(t-1) \end{bmatrix}
$$

  

$$
\varphi(t)y(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t)y(t-1) \\ y(t)u(t-1) \end{bmatrix}
$$

* **Closed-Form Estimate from (1.11)**:
  

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \hat{a}_N \\ \hat{b}_N \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum_{t=1}^N y^2(t-1) & -\sum_{t=1}^N y(t-1)u(t-1) \\ -\sum_{t=1}^N y(t-1)u(t-1) & \sum_{t=1}^N u^2(t-1) \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} -\sum_{t=1}^N y(t)y(t-1) \\ \sum_{t=1}^N y(t)u(t-1) \end{bmatrix}
$$

* **Boundary Convention at $t=1$**: Because the sum starts at $t=1$, evaluating $\varphi(1)$ requires $y(0)$ and $u(0)$, which lie outside the recorded dataset $Z^N = \{u(1), y(1), \dots, u(N), y(N)\}$. A standard convention is to set unmeasured pre-window values to zero ($y(0) = 0, u(0) = 0$), or alternatively to start the summation at $t = \max(n, m) + 1 = 2$.

##### Example 1.5: An Immersion Heater ("Semi-Physical Modeling")
Consider an immersion heater coil submerged in a cooling liquid. At each sampling instant $t$, we measure three physical quantities:
* $v(t)$: Voltage applied to the heater coil.
* $r(t)$: Temperature of the surrounding cooling liquid.
* $y(t)$: Surface temperature of the heater coil (the output to be modeled).

Instead of blindly throwing raw measurements $[v(t-1), r(t-1)]$ into a black-box linear model, we apply basic first-principles physics (**semi-physical / gray-box modeling**):
1. **Energy Balance**: The change in heater coil temperature over one sampling period, $y(t) - y(t-1)$, is proportional to (electrical inflow power) minus (convective heat loss to the liquid).
2. **Joule Heating Law**: Electrical power dissipated in a resistor of resistance $R_{\text{elec}}$ is $P_{\text{in}}(t) = v^2(t)/R_{\text{elec}} \propto v^2(t)$.
3. **Newton's Law of Cooling**: Heat loss from the coil to the liquid is proportional to the temperature difference $y(t) - r(t)$.

Combining these three physical laws over one sample delay yields:

$$
y(t) = y(t - 1) + \alpha v^2(t - 1) - \beta \big( y(t - 1) - r(t - 1) \big)
$$

Rearranging into the standard difference equation form gives:

$$
y(t) + \theta_1 y(t - 1) = \theta_2 v^2(t - 1) + \theta_3 r(t - 1)
$$

where $\theta_1 = \beta - 1$, $\theta_2 = \alpha$, and $\theta_3 = \beta$.
* **Key Insight**: Even though the relationship between voltage $v(t-1)$ and temperature $y(t)$ is **nonlinear** (quadratic in $v$), the predictor is still **strictly linear in the parameter vector** $\theta = [\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3]^T$ once we define the two-input regression vector:
  

$$
\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} -y(t - 1) & v^2(t - 1) & r(t - 1) \end{bmatrix}^T
$$

* Furthermore, the physics tells us that $\theta_1 + \theta_3 = (\beta - 1) + 1 - \dots = -1$ (since $\theta_1 = \beta - 1$ and $\theta_3 = -\beta$ if written on the same side, or $\theta_1 + \theta_3 = -1$ under Ljung's sign convention where $y(t) + (\beta - 1)y(t-1) = \alpha v^2(t-1) + \beta r(t-1)$ implies $-\theta_1 - \theta_3 = 1 - \beta - \beta$... specifically, if we enforce $\Delta y(t) = y(t) - y(t-1) = \alpha v^2(t-1) - \beta(y(t-1) - r(t-1))$, we can even reduce the model to 2 parameters $[\alpha, \beta]^T$ by defining the target variable as $\tilde{y}(t) = y(t) - y(t-1)$ and the regressors as $[v^2(t-1), -(y(t-1)-r(t-1))]^T$!).

#### 4. Terminology: Linear Regressions & ARX Models

* **Linear Regression**: Any model structure of the form $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$ that is *linear in the parameter vector $\theta$* is called a **linear regression** in statistics.
  * $\varphi(t)$ is the **regression vector**, and its scalar entries are the **regressors** ("regress" refers to explaining $y(t)$ by "going back" to the variables in $\varphi(t)$).
* **Why "ARX"?**:
  * When $\varphi(t)$ contains lagged values of the output variable to be explained ($-y(t-1), \dots, -y(t-n)$), the model is partly an **Auto-Regression (AR)** (regressing $y$ on itself).
  * When $\varphi(t)$ also includes external/control inputs ($u(t-1), \dots, u(t-m)$), those inputs are called **eXtra inputs** or **eXogenous variables (X)** (variables determined outside the system).
  * Hence, (1.1) has the universal standard name **ARX model** (*Auto-Regression with eXogenous inputs*).
  * Special case ($n = 0$): When no lagged outputs are included, the ARX model reduces to a **Finite Impulse Response (FIR)** model.

#### 5. Statistical Properties, Model Quality & Experiment Design ("Model Quality and Experiment Design")

How accurate is the Least Squares estimate $\hat{\theta}_N$ when real measurements are corrupted by random noise? To analyze this cleanly, Ljung first examines the **Finite Impulse Response (FIR)** special case (obtained by setting $n = 0$ in (1.1)):

$$
y(t) = b_1 u(t - 1) + \dots + b_m u(t - m) \tag{1.12}
$$

##### The True Data-Generating System Assumption
Suppose the observed data $Z^N$ have actually been generated by a true physical system of the same structure, corrupted by an additive disturbance $e(t)$ (often denoted $e_0(t)$ to emphasize the true noise process):

$$
y(t) = b_1^0 u(t - 1) + \dots + b_m^0 u(t - m) + e(t) \tag{1.13}
$$

* **Statistical Assumptions on the True System (1.13)–(1.14)**:
  1. **Realizability ($\mathcal{S} \in \mathcal{M}$)**: The true system can be described exactly by a true parameter vector $\theta_0 = \begin{bmatrix} b_1^0 & \dots & b_m^0 \end{bmatrix}^T \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
  2. **White Noise Disturbance**: $\{e(t)\}$ is a sequence of independent random variables with **zero mean** ($E[e(t)] = 0$) and **constant variance $\lambda$** ($E[e^2(t)] = \lambda$). Consequently, its autocovariance function is:
     

$$
E[e(t)e(s)] = \lambda \delta(t - s), \qquad \text{where } \delta(\tau) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \tau = 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } \tau \neq 0 \end{cases}
$$

  3. **Deterministic Input**: The input sequence $\{u(t)\}$ is a known, deterministic sequence of numbers. Because $\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t-1) & \dots & u(t-m) \end{bmatrix}^T$ in an FIR model contains *only* deterministic inputs, $\varphi(t)$ and $R(N)$ are completely **deterministic (non-random)** quantities!

In vector form, the true data-generating mechanism (1.13) is written analogously to (1.5) as:

$$
y(t) = \varphi^T(t)\theta_0 + e(t) \tag{1.14}
$$

##### Step-by-Step Derivation of the Parameter Estimation Error $\tilde{\theta}_N$
Define the sample regressor Gramian (information) matrix:

$$
R(N) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)
$$

Substitute the true output $y(t) = \varphi^T(t)\theta_0 + e(t)$ from (1.14) into the Least Squares estimator $\hat{\theta}_N = R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)y(t)$ from (1.11):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\theta}_N &= R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t) \left( \varphi^T(t)\theta_0 + e(t) \right) \\
&= R(N)^{-1} \left[ \left( \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) \right) \theta_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)e(t) \right] \\
&= R(N)^{-1} R(N) \theta_0 + R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)e(t) \\
&= \theta_0 + R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)e(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting $\theta_0$ from both sides gives the fundamental **parameter estimation error** equation:

$$
\tilde{\theta}_N \triangleq \hat{\theta}_N - \theta_0 = R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)e(t) \tag{1.15}
$$

##### Property 1: Unbiasedness ($E[\tilde{\theta}_N] = 0$) and Consistency Conditions
Taking the mathematical expectation $E[\cdot]$ of (1.15) and using the fact that $u(t)$—and therefore $\varphi(t)$ and $R(N)^{-1}$—are deterministic:

$$
E\left[\tilde{\theta}_N\right] = E\left[ R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)e(t) \right] = R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t) E[e(t)] = 0
$$

because $E[e(t)] = 0$. Thus, **for an FIR model with deterministic inputs, the Least Squares estimator $\hat{\theta}_N$ is strictly unbiased for any finite sample size $N$** ($E[\hat{\theta}_N] = \theta_0$).

> [!warning] General Statistical Considerations: What If $\varphi(t)$ Contains Lagged Outputs (Full ARX Case)?
> In the general ARX model ($n > 0$), $\varphi(t)$ contains past outputs $-y(t-1), \dots, -y(t-n)$, which themselves depend on past noise realizations $e(t-1), \dots, e(t-n)$. Thus $\varphi(t)$ and $R(N)$ become **random** matrices, so $E[R(N)^{-1}\sum \varphi(t)e(t)]$ cannot simply be factored in finite samples. Instead, we divide the numerator and denominator of (1.15) by $N$:
> 
> $$
> \hat{\theta}_N - \theta_0 = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) \right)^{-1} \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)e(t) \right)
> $$
> 
> For $\hat{\theta}_N \to \theta_0$ as $N \to \infty$ (**asymptotic consistency**), two fundamental conditions must hold:
> 1. **Non-Singularity / Persistent Excitation**: The asymptotic regressor covariance matrix $\bar{R} = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) = E[\varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)]$ must exist and be **non-singular (invertible)**.
> 2. **Uncorrelatedness of Regressors and Noise**: The sample cross-correlation must vanish: $\lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)e(t) = E[\varphi(t)e(t)] = 0$.
>    * **When does $E[\varphi(t)e(t)] = 0$ hold in an ARX model?** If $e(t)$ is truly **white noise** (independent from step to step), then $e(t)$ at time $t$ is statistically independent of past noise $e(t-1), e(t-2), \dots$, and therefore uncorrelated with past outputs $-y(t-1), \dots, -y(t-n)$ inside $\varphi(t)$.
>    * **Why Correlated (Colored) Noise Causes Bias in ARX**: If the true disturbance $e(t)$ is *correlated over time* (e.g., $e(t)$ depends on $e(t-1)$), then $e(t)$ is correlated with $y(t-1)$ inside $\varphi(t)$. Consequently, $E[\varphi(t)e(t)] \neq 0$, and Least Squares for an ARX model converges to a **systematically biased** parameter estimate! (Overcoming this bias motivates the ARMAX, Output-Error, and Box-Jenkins structures in Chapter 4.)

##### Property 2: Parameter Error Covariance Matrix ($P_N$)
To quantify the spread (uncertainty) of $\hat{\theta}_N$ around $\theta_0$ in the FIR case, we form the **covariance matrix of the parameter error**, $P_N \triangleq E[\tilde{\theta}_N \tilde{\theta}_N^T]$. Substituting $\tilde{\theta}_N$ from (1.15) and its transpose $\tilde{\theta}_N^T = \left(\sum_{s=1}^N e(s)\varphi^T(s)\right) R(N)^{-1}$ (since $R(N)$ is symmetric, $[R(N)^{-1}]^T = R(N)^{-1}$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_N = E\left[ \tilde{\theta}_N \tilde{\theta}_N^T \right] &= E\left[ R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} \varphi(t) e(t) e(s) \varphi^T(s) R(N)^{-1} \right] \\
&= R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} \varphi(t) \varphi^T(s) R(N)^{-1} E[e(t)e(s)] \\
&= R(N)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \sum_{s=1}^{N} \varphi(t) \varphi^T(s) R(N)^{-1} \lambda \delta(t - s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $\delta(t - s) = 0$ for all $s \neq t$ and $\delta(0) = 1$ when $s = t$, the double sum over $(t, s)$ collapses to a single sum over $t = s$:

$$
P_N = R(N)^{-1} \lambda \underbrace{\left( \sum_{t=1}^{N} \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t) \right)}_{R(N)} R(N)^{-1} = \lambda R(N)^{-1} \tag{1.16}
$$

##### Property 3: Asymptotic Covariance & Experiment Design ($\bar{R}$)
Define the normalized asymptotic covariance matrix of the regressors (the input covariance matrix):

$$
\bar{R} = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} R(N) \tag{1.17}
$$

For the FIR model where $\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u(t-1) & \dots & u(t-m) \end{bmatrix}^T$, the $(i, j)$-element of $\bar{R}$ is the sample auto-covariance of the input at lag $|i - j|$:

$$
[\bar{R}]_{i,j} = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} u(t - i)u(t - j)
$$

For large $N$, $R(N) \approx N\bar{R}$. Provided $\bar{R}$ is **non-singular (invertible)**, substituting $R(N) \approx N\bar{R}$ into (1.16) yields the celebrated asymptotic covariance formula:

$$
P_N = \frac{\lambda}{N} \bar{R}^{-1} \tag{1.18}
$$

##### Four Fundamental Engineering Takeaways from $P_N = \frac{\lambda}{N}\bar{R}^{-1}$
1. **$1/\sqrt{N}$ Convergence Rate**: The variance of each parameter estimate (diagonal entries of $P_N$) decays as $1/N$. Therefore, the standard deviation (typical parameter error magnitude $\|\tilde{\theta}_N\|$) decays at the classic Monte Carlo rate $1/\sqrt{N}$ (to halve the parameter standard deviation, you must collect $4\times$ as much data).
2. **Proportionality to Noise-to-Signal Ratio**: $P_N$ is directly proportional to the noise variance $\lambda$ and inversely proportional to the input power (which scales $\bar{R}$). Doubling the input signal amplitude quadruples $\bar{R}$ and cuts parameter variance by a factor of $4$.
3. **Insensitivity to Exact Signal Shape**: $P_N$ does *not* depend on the specific time-domain waveform of $u(t)$ or probability distribution of $e(t)$—it depends **only on their second-order variance/covariance properties** ($\lambda$ and $\bar{R}$). Many completely different input signals (e.g., white noise, Pseudo-Random Binary Sequences (PRBS), multisine waves) can achieve the exact same $\bar{R}$.
4. **Goal of Optimal Experiment Design**: Selecting the input signal $u(t)$ to maximize model quality is equivalent to making the matrix $\bar{R}^{-1}$ "as small as possible" (i.e., making the information matrix $\bar{R}$ as large and well-conditioned as possible, subject to physical actuator limits).

> [!important] Why a Constant Input Fails for Multi-Parameter Estimation (Persistent Excitation Preview)
> Suppose we try to identify an FIR model with $m = 2$ parameters ($y(t) = b_1 u(t-1) + b_2 u(t-2) + e(t)$) by applying a **constant step input** $u(t) \equiv u_0 \neq 0$ for all $t$.
> Then $\varphi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} u_0 & u_0 \end{bmatrix}^T$ for every $t$, and the asymptotic information matrix (1.17) becomes:
> 
> $$
> \bar{R} = \begin{bmatrix} u_0^2 & u_0^2 \\ u_0^2 & u_0^2 \end{bmatrix} \implies \det(\bar{R}) = u_0^4 - u_0^4 = 0
> $$
> 
> Because the two columns of $\bar{R}$ are identical, **$\bar{R}$ is singular (non-invertible)**! Physically, at steady state $y(t) = (b_1 + b_2)u_0 + e(t)$: the data only reveal the *sum* $b_1 + b_2$ (the DC static gain), leaving individual parameters $b_1$ and $b_2$ completely unidentifiable ($\bar{R}^{-1} \to \infty$). To make $\bar{R}$ invertible, the input $u(t)$ must vary sufficiently over time—a property formalized in Chapter 13 as **Persistent Excitation (PE)**.

## 3. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Trap 1: Sign Convention in $\varphi(t)$ vs. $\theta$**: In Equations (1.3)–(1.4), the parameter vector $\theta$ contains $+a_1, \dots, +a_n$, while the regression vector $\varphi(t)$ carries the negative signs $-y(t-1), \dots, -y(t-n)$. Whenever you manually assemble $\varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$ or $\varphi(t)y(t)$ (as in **Example 1.4**), never forget the minus signs on the lagged output components! Look closely at Example 1.4 on page 9: the off-diagonal terms of $R(N)$ are $-\sum y(t-1)u(t-1)$ and the top entry of $\sum \varphi(t)y(t)$ is $-\sum y(t)y(t-1)$ precisely because of the minus sign inside $\varphi(t)$.
> - **Trap 2: "Linear Regression" Does NOT Restrict You to Linear Physics**: Beginners often assume $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$ can only model linear physical plants. **Example 1.5 (Immersion Heater)** is placed right after the Least Squares derivation to shatter this misconception: as long as a known nonlinearity (like $v^2(t-1)$) is computed from measured signals and placed inside $\varphi(t)$, the estimation problem remains 100% linear in $\theta$.
> - **Trap 3: Unnormalized $R(N)$ vs. Normalized $\bar{R}$**: On page 11, Ljung defines $R(N) = \sum_{t=1}^N \varphi(t)\varphi^T(t)$ *without* dividing by $N$, which grows linearly with $N$ ($R(N) = \mathcal{O}(N)$), so $P_N = \lambda R(N)^{-1} = \mathcal{O}(1/N)$. On page 12, Eq. (1.17), he defines $\bar{R} = \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{1}{N} R(N)$ *with* the $\frac{1}{N}$ factor so that $\bar{R}$ converges to a finite deterministic covariance matrix. Later in the book (e.g., Chapter 7), $R(N)$ itself is sometimes defined with the $\frac{1}{N}$ factor built in—always check whether $1/N$ is inside or outside $R(N)$!
> - **Trap 4: Why Ljung Switches from ARX (1.1) to FIR (1.12) on Page 11**: To compute the exact finite-sample expectation $E[\tilde{\theta}_N] = 0$ and covariance $P_N = \lambda R(N)^{-1}$ in (1.15)–(1.16) without needing asymptotic stochastic convergence theory ($N \to \infty$), Ljung temporarily sets $n = 0$ (FIR model). Why? Because if $n > 0$, $\varphi(t)$ contains random past outputs $y(t-k)$, making $R(N)^{-1}$ a random matrix correlated with $e(t)$ in finite samples! For general ARX models ($n > 0$), unbiasedness and (1.18) hold *asymptotically* as $N \to \infty$ (proved rigorously in Chapters 8–9), provided $e(t)$ is white noise.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. *How do the three basic entities introduced here—the dataset $Z^N$ (1.6), the model structure $\hat{y}(t|\theta) = \varphi^T(t)\theta$ (1.5), and the Least Squares identification criterion (1.8)—generalize when the noise is not white?* (This leads directly into Section 1.4.)
>   2. *If we have actuator amplitude constraints $|u(t)| \le u_{\max}$, what kind of input sequence maximizes the eigenvalues of $\bar{R}$ in (1.17) so that the parameter covariance $P_N = \frac{\lambda}{N}\bar{R}^{-1}$ is minimized?*
