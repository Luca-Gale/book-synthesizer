# 1.2 Models

> [!tip] Pre-Reading Primer
> **Role in Chapter 1**: After Section 1.1 establishes what a *system* is (an object driven by inputs $u$, disturbances $v, e$, and producing outputs $y$), Section 1.2 defines what it means to construct a **model** of that system. It organizes models by their level of mathematical formalism (mental, graphical, analytical, and software models), contrasts the two fundamental paradigms for building them (**first-principles modeling** vs. **system identification**), and introduces the central philosophical premise of the entire book: *"The Fiction of a True System"*—that models are judged by **pragmatic usefulness** rather than ontological truth.

---

## 1. Core Intuition & Engineering Motivation

Whenever we interact with a dynamical system—whether steering an automobile, regulating a paper machine, or forecasting economic inflation—we rely on an assumed relationship among the observed signals. In the broadest sense, any such assumed relationship between system variables is called a **model** of the system.

Why does Ljung pause to classify models before diving into estimation algorithms?
1. **Purpose Dictates Formalism**: Not every engineering problem requires a stochastic partial differential equation. Turning a car steering wheel relies on an implicit, muscle-memory *mental model*; tuning a simple industrial PID loop often requires only a *graphical model* (like a step-response curve or Bode plot); designing an optimal model-predictive controller or state estimator demands a formal *mathematical (analytical) model*; and simulating an entire aircraft flight-control envelope may require a complex *software model*.
2. **Two Complementary Routes to Knowledge**: Engineers arrive at mathematical models via two distinct pathways:
   - **Modeling (First-Principles / Deductive)**: Decomposing a system into well-understood physical subsystems (masses, springs, resistors, reaction vessels) governed by established empirical laws (Newton's laws, Kirchhoff's laws, mass/energy balances) and joining them mathematically.
   - **System Identification (Data-Driven / Inductive)**: Recording input and output signals directly from experimentation on the system (such as the solar-heated house, aircraft flight test, or speech signals in Section 1.1) and applying data analysis to infer a model.
3. **Pragmatism Over "Truth"**: A physical system in the real world is fundamentally a different kind of object from a set of differential or difference equations. Ljung introduces the metaphor of an **"impenetrable but transparent screen"** separating the physical world from the world of mathematical descriptions: we can peer through the window to compare physical measurements against mathematical predictions, but we can never establish an exact identity between nature and equations. Consequently, model acceptance is governed by **usefulness for a specific task**, even though theorists occasionally invoke the mathematical fiction of a **"true system"** to analyze the statistical properties of identification algorithms.

## 2. Mathematical Rigor & Derivations

### 2.1 Symbol & Variable Glossary

Although Section 1.2 is primarily conceptual and taxonomic, it establishes the structural vocabulary and mathematical classes used throughout the rest of the text:

| Symbol / Concept | Meaning / Physical Interpretation | Mathematical Representation / Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $u(t)$ | Measurable input (control/excitation) signal | $\mathbb{R}^m$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$ (continuous) or $t \in \mathbb{Z}$ (discrete) |
| $y(t)$ | Measurable output (response) signal | $\mathbb{R}^p$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$ or $t \in \mathbb{Z}$ |
| $v(t), e(t)$ | Unmeasurable stochastic disturbances and measurement noise | Random processes on probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ |
| $g(t), h(t)$ | Impulse response / step response (Graphical LTI model) | Time-domain kernel functions mapping $u \mapsto y$ |
| $G(i\omega)$ | Frequency response function (Bode/Nyquist graphical model) | Complex-valued mapping $\omega \in \mathbb{R} \mapsto \mathbb{C}^{p \times m}$ |
| $\mathcal{M}$ | A mathematical or software model (assumed relationship among signals) | Functional/operator relationship $\mathcal{M}: u^t \mapsto \hat{y}(t)$ |
| $\mathcal{S}$ | "The True System" (idealized data-generating mechanism) | Hypothetical exact mathematical law assumed in theoretical analysis |

### 2.2 Formal Definitions, Assumptions & Step-by-Step Derivations

#### 1.2.1 Model Types and Their Use

Ljung classifies system models along a spectrum of increasing mathematical and computational structure, driven entirely by the **intended use** of the model (control design, simulation, forecasting/prediction, or fault diagnosis):

1. **Mental Models**:
   - **Definition**: Intuitive, non-mathematical internal representations built from experience and muscle memory.
   - **Example**: Driving a car—knowing that turning the steering wheel counterclockwise induces a left turn, and implicitly compensating for vehicle speed and road traction.
   - **Role**: Essential for everyday human-in-the-loop operation; their sophistication should never be underestimated, though they cannot be directly embedded into automated numerical design algorithms.

2. **Graphical Models**:
   - **Definition**: Descriptions of system properties summarized via numerical tables, lookup curves, or plots.
   - **Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) Examples**: An LTI system is uniquely characterized by its **impulse response** $g(t)$, its **step response** $s(t) = \int_0^t g(\tau)\,d\tau$, or its **frequency function (Bode/Nyquist plot)** $G(i\omega) = \int_0^\infty g(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$:
     

$$
y(t) = \int_0^\infty g(\tau)\,u(t-\tau)\,d\tau \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad Y(i\omega) = G(i\omega)U(i\omega)
$$

   - **Nonlinear Examples**: Static nonlinear characteristics, such as the flow-versus-opening lookup curve of a hydraulic valve $q = f(x)$, are naturally represented graphically.
   - **Role**: Widely used directly for classical controller tuning (e.g., reading gain and phase margins off a Bode plot or rise time/overshoot off a step response).

3. **Mathematical (or Analytical) Models**:
   - **Definition**: Explicit relationships among system variables phrased in terms of mathematical equations—specifically **differential equations** (in continuous time) or **difference equations** (in discrete time).
   - **Taxonomy of Mathematical Models**: A mathematical model is further classified by pairs of adjectives signifying the nature of the underlying equations:
     - **Time-Continuous vs. Time-Discrete**:
       - *Continuous-time*: Signals evolve over $t \in \mathbb{R}$, governed by ordinary or partial differential equations, e.g.,
         

$$
\frac{d^n y(t)}{dt^n} + a_1 \frac{d^{n-1} y(t)}{dt^{n-1}} + \dots + a_n y(t) = b_1 \frac{d^{m} u(t)}{dt^{m}} + \dots + b_m u(t)
$$

       - *Discrete-time*: Signals are sampled at discrete instants $t = k T_s$ (often normalized to $t = 1, 2, 3, \dots$), governed by difference equations, e.g.,
         

$$
y(t) + a_1 y(t-1) + \dots + a_n y(t-n) = b_1 u(t-1) + \dots + b_m u(t-m)
$$

     - **Lumped vs. Distributed Parameters**:
       - *Lumped*: Spatial variations are neglected so the system state lives in a finite-dimensional vector space $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$, described by **Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs)** or difference equations.
       - *Distributed*: State variables depend on both time $t$ and spatial coordinates $z \in \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ (e.g., temperature profile along a heat exchanger or acoustic wave propagation), described by **Partial Differential Equations (PDEs)**.
     - **Deterministic vs. Stochastic**:
       - *Deterministic*: Exact known inputs uniquely determine the outputs; uncertainties and disturbances $v(t), e(t)$ are ignored or treated as known functions.
       - *Stochastic*: Unmeasurable disturbances and measurement errors are modeled explicitly as random variables/processes with specified probability distributions or spectral densities, e.g., adding a stochastic innovation term $e(t)$ to a difference equation.
     - **Linear vs. Nonlinear**:
       - *Linear*: The model obeys the **principle of superposition**—for any two input-output pairs $(u_1, y_1)$ and $(u_2, y_2)$ and scalars $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, the input $\alpha u_1 + \beta u_2$ produces $\alpha y_1 + \beta y_2$.
       - *Nonlinear*: Superposition fails (e.g., saturation, dead-zones, bilinear products of state and input, aerodynamic drag $\propto v^2$).
     - *(Implicitly also Time-Invariant vs. Time-Varying, depending on whether equation coefficients remain constant or drift with $t$.)*
   - **Role**: The foundation of modern engineering design, optimal control, state estimation (Kalman filtering), and **forecasting (prediction)** across both technical fields (aerospace, process control, signal processing) and non-technical domains (economics, ecology, biology).

4. **Software Models**:
   - **Definition**: A computer program that simulates a complex system by interconnecting numerous subroutines, conditional logic branches, and multi-dimensional lookup tables.
   - **Role**: When a system is too intricate to summarize compactly as a closed-form analytical equation, the simulator itself *is* the model. Software models play an increasingly central role in decision-making, verification, and operator training for large-scale systems.

#### 1.2.2 Building Models: First-Principles Modeling vs. System Identification

Regardless of whether the target is a mental, graphical, mathematical, or software model, every model ultimately rests on observed data and empirical experience. Mathematical models are constructed along two fundamental routes (or a hybrid combination of both):

| Dimension | Route 1: **Modeling** (First-Principles / Deductive) | Route 2: **System Identification** (Data-Driven / Inductive) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Mechanism** | Split the system into elementary subsystems whose properties are already known from prior empirical laws (Newton's, Kirchhoff's, conservation balances), then join them mathematically. | Perform experiments directly on the system; record input/output signals $(u(t), y(t))$ and subject them to statistical/numerical data analysis to infer a model. |
| **Experimentation on Actual System?** | **Not necessarily required**—can be performed entirely on paper or in CAD/simulation before the physical system is even built. | **Strictly required**—relies directly on recorded time-series data (such as Figures 1.4, 1.6, and 1.9 in Section 1.1). |
| **Domain Specificity** | **Highly application-dependent**—relies on domain traditions (e.g., bond graphs in mechatronics, compartmental models in pharmacology, finite-element meshes in structural mechanics). | **Universal / Domain-agnostic**—the same mathematical identification algorithms apply equally to solar houses, fighter aircraft, speech signals, and economic time series. |
| **Typical Output** | Block diagrams of simple physical elements; increasingly assembled by computer into large-scale **software models** or structured state-space equations with unknown physical constants. | **Mathematical models** (difference/differential equations) or **graphical models** (nonparametric impulse/frequency responses) calibrated directly to observed behavior. |

- **How They Complement Each Other (Gray-Box vs. Black-Box Preview)**:
  - In practice, the two routes frequently merge. First-principles *modeling* specifies the structural block diagram and conservation equations (leaving unknown physical coefficients like heat-transfer rates or aerodynamic derivatives), while *system identification* estimates those unknown parameters from experimental input-output data (**gray-box identification**), or fits a generic parameterized filter structure when the internal physics are too complex to model from scratch (**black-box identification**).

#### 1.2.3 The Fiction of a True System

The closing subsection of Section 1.2 lays down the epistemological foundation of system identification:

1. **The Impenetrable Screen Between Nature and Mathematics**:
   - A real physical system (a turbulent chemical reactor, a human vocal tract, an economy) and a mathematical model (a finite-order linear difference equation) belong to fundamentally different ontological categories.
   - Between our world of mathematical descriptions and the physical world stands an **"impenetrable but transparent screen"**:
     

$$
\underbrace{\text{Real Physical System}}_{\text{Physical World}} \quad \xleftrightarrow{\text{Transparent Screen (Compare } y(t) \text{ vs. } \hat{y}(t)\text{)}} \quad \underbrace{\text{Mathematical Model } \mathcal{M}}_{\text{World of Equations}}
$$

   - We can look *through* this window to compare measured physical signals $y(t)$ with model-predicted outputs $\hat{y}(t)$, and confirm they agree to high precision under specific operating conditions. Yet we can **never** penetrate the screen to prove that nature itself is governed by our exact equations.

2. **The Pragmatic Criterion ("Usefulness" over "Truth")**:
   - Asking whether a mathematical model is "true" is scientifically meaningless in practice.
   - Instead, model acceptance must be guided strictly by **pragmatic usefulness**: *Does this model predict future outputs accurately enough, or stabilize the closed-loop controller reliably enough, for our intended engineering application?*

3. **Why Theorists Still Invoke "The True System" ($\mathcal{S}$)**:
   - Despite rejecting literal "true systems" in the physical world, Ljung notes that throughout the textbook we will occasionally postulate a **"true system"**—meaning we assume the observed data $\{u(t), y(t)\}$ were actually generated by a well-defined mathematical rule $\mathcal{S}$ (for instance, a true transfer function $G_0(q)$ plus true white noise $e_0(t)$).
   - **Explicit Assumption & Purpose**: This is an acknowledged **idealization (fiction)**. It is indispensable as a theoretical benchmark: by testing whether an identification algorithm recovers $\mathcal{S}$ as the number of data points $N \to \infty$ (consistency, asymptotic variance, bias analysis), we gain rigorous insight into how the method behaves and what its fundamental limitations are.

## 4. Pre-Reading Compass (What to Watch Out For)

> [!warning] Key Conceptual Leaps & Notation Traps
> - **Terminology Trap ("Modeling" vs. "System Identification")**: In everyday conversation, engineers use "modeling" as an umbrella term for creating any model. In Ljung's terminology, however, **modeling** specifically denotes the *deductive, first-principles route* (assembling known subsystem laws without necessarily testing the whole system), whereas **system identification** denotes the *inductive, experimental route* (inferring models from recorded input-output data).
> - **Software Models vs. Analytical Models**: Notice how Ljung distinguishes a *software model* (interconnected subroutines and lookup tables that can simulate trajectories numerically) from a *mathematical/analytical model* (compact differential/difference equations amenable to algebraic analysis and control synthesis). Often, system identification is used to extract a compact analytical model *from* an overly complex software simulator or physical plant!
> - **Philosophical Tension ("The True System")**: Keep "The Fiction of a True System" in mind when reading later chapters on convergence and asymptotic statistics (Chapters 8–9). Whenever you see "Assume the true system is given by $y(t) = G_0(q)u(t) + H_0(q)e(t)$," remember Ljung's caveat here: this is a controlled theoretical sandbox used to test the algorithm's properties, *not* a claim that physical reality is literally a finite-order ARMAX process.
> - **Questions to Keep in Mind While Reading**:
>   1. How does the intended end-use of a model (e.g., rough PID tuning vs. multistep ahead economic forecasting) dictate whether we stop at a graphical model or proceed to a stochastic difference equation?
>   2. When building a model from first principles via block diagrams of subsystems, why does the resulting aggregate representation so often become an intractable *software model* rather than a clean *analytical model*?
